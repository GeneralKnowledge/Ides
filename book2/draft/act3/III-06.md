# Fulginiae

POV: Fulvia · Praeneste · late January 40

---

The rider who brought it had changed horses four times and it showed in the way he came off the last one, which was not so much getting down as stopping being on it, and Fulvia, watching from the window, knew before he was through the gate that he had been sent by somebody who wanted her to know quickly, and that this was not a good sign.

Good news came slowly. She had learned that in the year of Clodius. Men who had good news to tell you came at a walk, in a clean cloak, so that they could stand in your atrium and tell it well. Men with bad news rode until the horse could not, because they wanted it out of their mouths and into yours.

She went down herself.

He was a young man from Ventidius’s horse, a Picene, with the mud of the Flaminian Way on him to the waist and a cut lip from something, and he stood in the court with his hand on the wall as if the wall might go, and told her.

They had come south. All three of them, Ventidius and Pollio and Plancus, in the middle of the month, down out of the hills by the Flaminian Way and the roads either side of it, with their legions, toward Perusia. They had come as far as Fulginiae. He said the name as if she might not know it. A day’s march from Perusia. From the hills above Fulginiae, he said, you could see the smoke of the young Caesar’s camps round the town, and at night the fires, and the men had been told that the consul would see their fires too, from his walls, and know that they had come.

And then they had stopped.

“Stopped,” she said.

He looked at her. He had the look of a young man who has been carrying a sentence for four days and has discovered, now that he has to say it, that it is heavier than when he picked it up.

“There was a council,” he said. “In Ventidius’s tent. I was at the door. Ventidius said go on. Pollio said go on. Plancus said wait.” He swallowed. “Plancus said the young Caesar had Agrippa between us and the town, and Salvidienus behind us, and that if we went on we would have to fight two armies to reach one, and that Antonius had sent no orders. He said nobody knew what Antonius wanted. He said that twice. *Nobody knows what Antonius wants.* And then he said wait, and the others argued, and he said wait again, and they waited.” The young man looked at the ground. “We were still waiting when Ventidius sent me. We could see the fires.”

---

She sent him to the kitchen to be fed. She stood in the court for a while after he had gone.

It was cold. The sky over the plain was the colour of a wet slate and there had been frost on the terraces of the temple in the morning, and she could see her breath. Behind her in the house she could hear Manius, who had been in the room over the court and must have heard every word, coming down the stairs at a pace that meant he was going to talk.

She did not want Manius yet. She went out of the court by the side door and along the lane under the citadel to the place where you could see north, which she had found in the autumn and gone to often since, a corner of the old wall with a fig tree growing out of it, and stood there with her hands under her cloak and looked north.

You could not see Perusia from here. You could not see anything but the plain going away, brown, with the roads on it and the smoke from the farms. Ninety miles. She had done that sum so often that she no longer did it; it simply arrived, every time she looked north, like a debt.

Plancus.

She thought about Plancus. She had known him for ten years. She had sat at his dinners and he at hers, and he had been charming at both, and had told a story better than any man in Rome except Marcus, and had changed sides more neatly than any man in Rome without exception. He had been Caesar’s man. He had been the Senate’s man after the Ides, writing Cicero those long elegant letters from Gaul about his devotion to the Republic. He had been Marcus’s man by the end of that summer, the moment Marcus came over the Alps and it was clear which way it would go. He always waited. He waited until it was clear and then he was on the side it was clear about, and he had a way of making it seem, afterwards, that he had been there all along.

He was a coward. That was all it was. A clever, charming coward, who had sat in a tent at Fulginiae with three armies’ worth of fires in sight and decided that he would rather be on the right side of the young Caesar in a year than on the right side of Lucius tonight.

She held that thought for a while. It was a comfortable one. It had a shape she knew.

*Nobody knows what Antonius wants.*

She did not hold that one. It would not be held. It was Plancus’s sentence, in Plancus’s voice, coming to her through a young man with a cut lip in a cold court, and it went round and round in her the way a stone goes round in a pot of boiling water, knocking.

She did not know what Marcus wanted. That was the truth. She had not known since the spring. She had written to him every month, by every ship, and he had written her two letters, one about silver and one about a fleet, and nothing since, nothing all summer, nothing all autumn, nothing about Lucius or the land or Praeneste or the wall round Perusia; and in the autumn there had begun to be other news out of the East, not in letters, in the talk of ship-masters at Puteoli and Brundisium, about Tarsus, and a gilded barge, and a queen.

Plancus had sat in a tent and said aloud the thing she had not let herself say all winter. And the others had listened to him, Ventidius and Pollio, who were not cowards, who had wanted to go on. They had listened because it was true.

---

She went back to the house when she could not feel her hands.

Manius was in the room over the court, walking up and down, as she had known he would be. He began to talk as soon as she came in. He had a plan. He always had a plan. Another rider to Ventidius, with money. A letter to Plancus that would shame him. A second force raised from the colonies round Praeneste to make a show on the road to Perusia, to draw Agrippa off. He talked for some time. She sat in the chair by the window with her keys in her lap and let him.

When he stopped for breath she said, “Yes. Do that. Send to Ventidius.”

He looked at her, surprised. He had expected to be argued with. Then he went to do it, quickly, pleased, the way men go when they have been given permission to be busy.

She sat by the window.

She was going to leave Italy. She knew it the way she had known, standing over Clodius’s body in her own atrium with the street outside full to the walls, that she was going to open the doors and show them his wounds. It did not arrive as a decision. It arrived as a thing already done, that she was only now noticing.

Not yet. When Perusia fell, and it would fall, because Plancus was right; when there was nothing in Italy any more that her being in it could hold. Then she would take the boys and Claudia and the silver that was left, and go south, to Brundisium, and find a ship, and go to Marcus. Wherever he was. Athens. Alexandria. Wherever the queen’s barge had put in. She would go and stand in front of him and find out, to his face, what he wanted.

She did not tell Manius. She did not tell her woman, though her woman would know within the day, because her woman always knew. She did not write it to Lucius. Lucius was ninety miles north on a hill, eating his horses, waiting for the fires.

In the court below, the young man from Ventidius’s horse had come out of the kitchen with a piece of bread in his hand and was standing by his foundered horse, stroking its neck, talking to it in a low voice, in the Picene way, as if it were a person who had done its best.
