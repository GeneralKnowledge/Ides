# The Ides

POV: Laberius · the young Caesar’s camp below Perusia · 15 March 40

---

They took them out of the pen before light, and Laberius was on the road, so he saw them go and he did not see where.

The pen was on the flat ground east of the camp, below the burnt town, a square of hurdles with a ditch round it and a guard on each side, where the men of rank from Perusia had been kept since the surrender. He had walked past it every day for a month going down to the river for water. Senators, they said, and knights, and the gentlemen of the town council, three or four hundred of them, he had never counted, sitting on the bare ground inside the hurdles in their good cloaks gone filthy, waiting. They had been fed. He had seen the bread going in. Some of them had sat in the same place every day, in the same corner, as if it had become theirs. One old man had a cushion from somewhere and sat on it very straight with his hands on his knees, all day, every day, looking at nothing.

Laberius had been put on the road that night with ten men, at the crossroads below the camp where the track from the pen met the old road to the south. The order had come at the second watch, from a tribune he did not know. Hold the crossroads. Let nobody through either way until you are relieved. That was all.

So he held it. He stood at the crossroads in the dark with his ten men round a small fire, and at the end of the last watch, with the sky going grey over the hills to the east, he heard them coming down the track from the pen.

---

They came in a column, two by two, with guards on both sides and in front and behind, a lot of guards, more than were needed. They were not tied. They walked. They came out of the dark into the light of his fire, two by two, and went past him, close enough to touch, and on along the track to the west, the other way from the road he was holding, toward the camp.

He looked at their faces as they went by because he could not help it.

Some of them were talking to each other, low. Some of them were not. One was weeping. One, a big heavy man with a senator’s stripe on his tunic under the dirty cloak, was walking with his chin up and his eyes on the back of the man in front, as if he were on his way to the Senate house and it was beneath him to notice the road. The old man with the cushion was there. He did not have the cushion. He walked very slowly and the guard behind him did not hurry him.

Laberius counted them. He did not decide to count them. He counted by twos as they went by, the way he would have counted a cohort past a gate, and he got to a hundred and forty and lost it, because a man near the end of the column broke out of it.

He was young. He was a boy of twenty or so in a knight’s narrow stripe, and he simply stepped out of the column on the side nearest the fire, and walked, quite fast but not running, past the end of the guard and toward the crossroads and the old road south, toward Laberius, with his face very white and his eyes fixed on the dark beyond the fire, as if there were a door there and he had just seen it.

Nobody shouted. The guard at the end of the column had his back turned. The boy came past the fire. He came past Laberius, an arm’s length away, close enough that Laberius could see a cut on his jaw where he had shaved with a bad blade, and Laberius had his orders, *let nobody through either way*, and his ten men were sitting by the fire looking at him to see what he would do.

He looked at the fire.

He did not know how long. When he looked up the boy was gone, into the dark down the road to the south, and the column had gone on along the track to the west, toward the camp, two by two, and the last guard had turned round and was looking back along the track at the crossroads with a puzzled face, as if he had heard something.

“All right, Centurion?” the guard called.

“All right,” said Laberius.

---

They were relieved at the third hour. He walked back to the camp with his ten men, and none of them said anything about the boy, then or afterwards. He saw them not say it. He saw one of them, the youngest, start to, and the man next to him put a hand on his arm.

The camp was quiet. That was the thing he noticed. It was the Ides of March, and every man in the young Caesar’s army knew what the Ides of March was, and he had thought there would be something, a sacrifice, games, the men drunk on the triumvir’s wine. There was nothing. The camp was quiet the way a camp is quiet the morning after a battle, when the men who were in it do not want to talk about it and the men who were not have been told not to ask.

The pen was empty. He walked past it on his way to the river. The hurdles were still up and the ditch was still round it, and the ground inside was trodden flat and bare, with a few things left on it: a cloak, a sandal, a cup. In one corner a cushion.

---

By night there were three stories, and he heard all of them.

He heard the first at the fire by the fifth tower, from a man from the Seventh who had been on the guard at the pen and had walked with the column. The man from the Seventh said they had been taken to the young Caesar’s tribunal, where there was a court, and the young Caesar had sat on it, and had read out names. The men of the town council had been put to death, all of them but one, an old man called Aemilius who had once, in Rome, voted to condemn the men who killed the god Julius, and who was let go for it. The rest, the senators and knights from Rome, had been pardoned, or fined, or sent off under guard to wait; he did not know which. He had not stayed. It had been a court. A court, the man from the Seventh said, and looked at the fire, and did not say anything else, and the men round the fire looked at each other.

He heard the second at the river, from a man from the Antonian cohorts who had come out of Perusia with the army and been taken into the young Caesar’s legions and was not happy there. The Antonian said it had not been a court. He said they had been taken to an altar. An altar to the god Julius, built new, of turf, in the camp, and that the young Caesar had stood at it in a priest’s veil on the morning of the Ides of March, the day his father was killed, and that three hundred of them, senators and knights, had been brought to it one by one and killed there, as victims, as you kill a beast. Three hundred. On the Ides. For his father. The Antonian said everybody knew. He said ask anybody.

He heard the third in his own tent, late, from Vibius, who had heard it from a clerk in the commissariat. Vibius said nobody had been killed at all. Vibius said that the prisoners had been taken to the camp to be sorted, and that the rich ones were going to be ransomed and the rest sent to Rome under guard, and that the stories about altars were being put about by Antonius’s people to make trouble for when Antonius came home. Vibius said the clerk had seen the lists. Vibius said, look, it stands to reason. What would he want to kill three hundred senators for? Who would he have left to dine with?

Laberius lay on his blanket in the dark and listened to Vibius tell it, and did not say anything, and thought about the boy with the cut on his jaw going past the fire into the dark, and about the cushion in the corner of the empty pen.

---

In the morning a new man in the cohort, a boy from one of the colonies, asked him at the drains.

“They say you were on the road, Centurion. Last night. When they took them.”

“I was on the road.”

“So what happened?” The boy leaned on his spade. “At the altar. They say three hundred. They say on the Ides, for the god Julius. Is it true?”

Laberius looked at the ditch.

He had three stories. He had to pick one. That was clear to him, standing at the drain with the spade in his hand and the boy waiting: that whatever he said now, the boy would say after him, at the fire tonight, *the centurion was there, the centurion says*, and it would go round the cohort by morning and round the legion by the end of the month. He had seen that happen with smaller things. He did not know which story was true. He thought, standing there, that he might never know, and that it might not matter to anybody but him whether he did.

He thought about the man from the Seventh looking at the fire. He thought about the Antonian saying *ask anybody*. He thought about Vibius saying *who would he have left to dine with*.

“The town council,” he said. “All but one. The one that voted against the men who killed Caesar, they let him go. It was a court.” He put the spade into the clay. “I was on the road. That’s what the man from the Seventh said, and he was there.”

The boy nodded slowly, satisfied, as if a sum had come out.

Laberius dug. He did not say anything about the boy at the fire, the one with the cut on his jaw. He had not said it to anyone and he did not mean to.
