# The Price Before the News

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Janus · February 40

---

The wheat fell on a market day, and it fell before anybody at the Janus knew why.

Hordeonius saw it on his walk in. He came down every morning now by the river, past the grain market below the Aventine, because he had found that the dealers there knew things a day before the Forum did and two days before the Senate. They were hard men, the grain dealers, with hands like boards and no interest in politics whatever except as weather, and they did not gossip; but they moved their prices, and the prices talked.

Sixteen had been the price for three weeks. Sixteen, on the white plaster by his stall, under fifteen and fourteen and sixteen again. This morning the board at the biggest dealer’s, the Sicilian with the cart-horse voice, said thirteen. The dealer next to him had twelve. The one at the end, the small Campanian who always followed the others by an hour, still had sixteen, and was looking up the row at the other two with his mouth open.

Hordeonius stood in the cold and watched the Campanian rub out his sixteen and write fourteen.

There was no ship in. He knew that before he asked; there had been no ship from Sicily into Ostia since the Saturnalia, and the river was empty. There had been no news from the Senate. There had been nothing.

“Why?” he said to the Sicilian.

The Sicilian shrugged. “Everyone’s selling.”

“Why is everyone selling?”

The Sicilian looked at him with the incurious patience of a man explaining rain. “Because everyone’s selling,” he said.

---

He went up to the Janus thinking about it and wrote thirteen on the white plaster, and stood in front of it.

Something had happened at Perusia. That was the only thing it could be. Something had happened, and someone knew, some dealer with a cousin in the young Caesar’s commissariat, some shipper whose mule-drivers had come down the Flaminian Way in the night; and that someone had begun to sell grain he had been holding for a siege, because the siege was over; and the others had seen him sell and had sold too, not knowing why, because a man who does not know why had better follow a man who does. And so the price had come down the river ahead of the news, like a dog running ahead of its master.

The town had fallen. Or the town was about to fall. One or the other.

He felt it, the old feeling, the one he had had at twenty and at thirty and less often since: that he was standing for a moment on the near side of something that everyone else would be on the far side of by tonight, and that there was money in the space between.

---

The paper came to him at the fourth hour, as if he had sent for it.

It was a man he knew a little, a broker of debts who worked out of a room behind the Basilica Aemilia and bought and sold other men’s loans the way a fishwife sells fish, by weight, quickly, before they went off. He came to the board with a basket, as people did now; everyone in Rome seemed to carry their troubles in baskets this winter. He put it down.

“Perusia,” he said.

Hordeonius looked at the basket.

“Loans,” said the broker. “Bonds. Mortgages. Notes of hand. Owed by gentlemen of Rome who went to Praeneste in the summer with the consul, and from Praeneste to Perusia.” He lifted the cloth a little, so that Hordeonius could see the seals. They were good seals. “Owed to people who would like their money now, and not when the gentlemen come home, if they come home. I’m selling for them. I’ll take a quarter of face.”

“A quarter.”

“A quarter.” The broker looked at him steadily. “That’s what the room behind the Basilica says they’re worth this morning. Yesterday it was half. You’ve seen the wheat.”

Hordeonius had seen the wheat.

He looked at the seals. He knew some of them. A knight from the Velabrum with a house full of Greek bronzes. A senator’s younger son with a farm at Tibur. A man named on one tablet as Cestius’s agent, whatever that meant. Men of family, men of property, with houses and lands that would still be there when this was over, whoever won. If the town had fallen, the young Caesar would pardon them, as he had pardoned everyone at the end of every other civil war that anyone could remember, except the proscriptions, and the proscriptions were over, the triumvirs had said so. They would come home in the spring, thin and sorry for themselves, and their loans would be worth face again, and he would have bought them at a quarter.

It was the best paper he had been offered in a year. It was the market being stupid. The market had heard *Perusia has fallen* and had panicked, and was selling the gentlemen of Perusia at a quarter because it could not think past the word *fallen*. That was all.

“How much?” he said.

The broker told him.

He took the key from his belt and opened the strongbox.

---

Sosia was at the Aventine again. He was always at the Aventine this winter. Hordeonius had sent him there that morning before he saw the wheat, and was glad of it now, in a way he did not look at too closely. Sosia would have asked questions. Sosia would have said *You don’t wager*, in his soft voice, and been right, and Hordeonius did not want to be right this morning. He wanted to be early.

He counted the silver out to the broker himself and wrote the purchase in a separate tablet, not the day-book, a small one he kept in the strongbox for things that were not yet anyone’s business. He put the broker’s basket under the board.

The courier came in at the seventh hour.

He came down the Sacred Way at a canter, which no one was allowed to do, with the young Caesar’s badge on his saddle-cloth, and men ran beside him and behind him shouting, and the shout came up into the arch of the Janus ahead of him and through it: *Perusia. Perusia has surrendered. The consul’s come out. The consul’s alive.*

Along the arcade the moneychangers stood up from their stools.

Hordeonius did not stand up. He sat with his hand flat on the board above the basket and waited for the rest of it. There was always a rest of it. The first shout was the thing itself and the rest of it was the price.

It came within the hour, as it always did, by the second rider and the third, and by men coming up from the Forum who had heard it read from the Rostra. The consul was spared. The consul was the young Caesar’s guest. The army was spared, all of it, the soldiers pardoned and fed and taken into the young Caesar’s service. The town had been given to the soldiers to plunder, or would be, or had not been; nobody was sure. And the men of rank who had been found in the town, the senators and the knights and the gentlemen from Rome, were being held. Under guard. In the young Caesar’s camp. Until the young Caesar decided what was just.

Hordeonius heard the word *held* and took his hand off the board.

He sat for a while. Then he bent and took the broker’s basket out from under the board and lifted the cloth, and looked at the seals again: the knight from the Velabrum, and the senator’s younger son, and Cestius’s agent, whatever that meant. He looked at them for some time.

A quarter of face. That was what the room behind the Basilica had said they were worth this morning, before the courier, before anyone at the Janus knew anything. The room had known. Not what had happened, but what it would cost. The price had come down the river ahead of the news, like a dog running ahead of its master, and he had looked at it and seen a market being stupid.

He put the cloth back over the basket and the basket back under the board, and when Sosia came in at the ninth hour with nothing from the Aventine and asked what the shouting had been, he told him that Perusia had fallen and the consul was alive, and that was all he told him.
