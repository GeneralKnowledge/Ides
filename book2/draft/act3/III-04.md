# A Measure of Wheat

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Janus · January 40

---

Wheat was at sixteen sesterces the measure on the morning after the Kalends, and Hordeonius wrote it on the wall.

He had begun doing that in December. There was a smooth patch of plaster on the pier beside his stall, under the arch, where some earlier moneylender had kept his reckonings in charcoal and some later one had whitewashed them over, and Hordeonius had taken to writing the price of a measure of wheat on it every morning, in small figures, one under another, with the date. He did not know why. He had never needed to write down a price to remember it. But he found that he liked to stand in front of the column in the morning before he took his shutter down and look at it, the way he imagined a sailor looks at the sky.

Four, it had been in the spring, which was what it had been for most of his life. Five at the end of the summer, when the consul went to Praeneste. Seven the week Lucius came into Rome. Nine at the Saturnalia, when the news came that the young Caesar’s wall was closed round Perusia and the country between there and the city was full of armies eating whatever grew on it. And then, in the last days of December, the ships from Sicily and Africa had stopped coming into Ostia, because Sextus Pompeius had his fleet off the coast and was taking whatever came, and now it was sixteen.

He wrote *16* and stood back.

Behind him, along the arcade, he could hear Bucco’s men.

---

There were three of them now. Bucco had never needed men before. Bucco had sat on his stool at the end of the row with his oily voice and his strongbox and let the money come to him; that had been his style, and Hordeonius had disliked it and admired it in equal parts for twenty years. But since the consul left Rome, Bucco had hired men. They were big and quiet and came from somewhere south, and they went out every morning with tablets in their hands and came back in the evening with whatever they had got, and every day there were more veterans standing in the arcade with their bronze plates, not wanting to borrow against them now, wanting to know why they were being asked to pay.

Hordeonius knew why. Everyone at the Janus knew why. Bucco had lent to the consul. Bucco had lent a great deal to the consul, against the revenues of a year that the consul would not see, and the consul was in a town in Etruria eating his horses, and Bucco needed every coin that was owed him anywhere in Italy, and he was calling it in, from the men who could least pay it, at the worst possible time.

He had watched Bucco tie the double knots on the consul’s silver in November and thought that Bucco knew something. Bucco had known nothing. Hordeonius had understood that at the Saturnalia, watching Bucco’s face when the news of the wall came into the Janus, and it had not given him the satisfaction he had expected. It had given him a cold feeling, like finding a step missing on a stair he had used for years.

---

The countryman from Venusia came at the fourth hour.

Hordeonius knew him by the cloak. It was the same good thick cloak, gone thin at the shoulders, and it was thinner now, and there was a tear at the hem that someone had mended badly. The man inside it was thinner too. He came down the arcade slowly, past Bucco’s men, who looked at him and decided he was not theirs, and stopped at Hordeonius’s board and stood there.

“Good day,” said Hordeonius.

The countryman did not say good day. He put his hand inside his tunic and took out a purse and laid it on the board, and Hordeonius could see from the way it lay that there was not much in it.

“The interest,” the countryman said. “For the month. What there is.”

Hordeonius did not touch the purse.

“Your land?” he said.

“There’s a man from the Seventh on it. Since the autumn.” The countryman said it without any feeling at all, as if it were the name of a road. “He’s let me keep the house. Two rooms. I pay him for it, in work. My wife’s with her sister in the city. I came up to see about the compensation.” He paused. “There’s none. They say at the treasury, the consul promised it, and the consul’s at Perusia. They say ask the young Caesar. At the young Caesar’s house there’s a woman at the door who writes your name down.”

“And how long have you been in Rome?”

“Since the Saturnalia.”

“And what is bread costing you?”

The countryman looked at him. Then he looked past him, at the column of figures on the white plaster, and Hordeonius saw him read the last one. It took him a moment. He moved his lips.

“Sixteen,” the countryman said. “I didn’t know it was written up.”

---

Sosia was not at the stall. He was on the Aventine, at a house that owed and would not open its door, and would not be back until the ninth hour. Hordeonius had sent him there that morning. He did not ask himself why he had sent him there that morning.

He took the day-book from under the board and opened it at the place, last spring, where the two loans were written one under the other in his own hand: the soldier’s third against the bronze plate from Venusia, and under it the countryman’s quarter, against a claim on the treasury, at one by the month. A line under both.

He looked at the line for some time.

The claim was worthless. That was the plain fact of it, the fact a moneylender lives by. The treasury had not paid and would not pay, not this year, not while the consul who had promised it was in a starving town and the triumvir who had not was digging a ditch round him. The interest would come in a purse like this one, thinner every month, until it did not come at all, and then he would have the black deed in his strongbox and a starving man in a two-room house at Venusia and nothing else. A sensible lender wrote off a claim like that early, before he had spent any more on it in hope. He had done it a hundred times. It was not kindness. It was arithmetic, and the arithmetic said to close it.

He dipped the stylus and wrote, under the line, in the clear hand he kept for the day-book: *Account closed. Nothing further owing. Remitted in full, the day after the Kalends of January, in the consulship of Calvinus and Pollio.*

He had not written that phrase in thirty years. *Remitted in full.* He had closed accounts. He had closed one, two winters ago, without writing anything at all, by burning it. He had never written down that he had given anything back.

He pushed the purse across the board to the countryman.

“There is no interest,” he said. “The account is closed. Here. You can see it.” He turned the day-book round. “Your claim on the treasury is mine now, and the treasury will pay it to me when it pays, which it will not, and that is my concern and not yours. Take that and buy bread with it.”

The countryman looked at the book. Then at the purse. He did not pick it up.

“Why?” he said.

Hordeonius found that he did not have an answer ready. That was unusual. He had an answer ready for everything at the board; that was half of what a lender was for.

“Because it is bad paper,” he said, “and I am cutting my losses.”

The countryman considered this for some time. Then he picked up the purse and put it back inside his tunic, and nodded, once, the way a man nods at a price he has decided not to argue, and went away up the arcade past Bucco’s men without looking back.

---

Sosia came in at the ninth hour with nothing from the Aventine and sat down on his stool and took the day-book on his knees to enter the nothing.

Hordeonius watched him turn the pages to the place. He watched Sosia’s finger go down the column, as it did every evening, checking, and stop, and go back up a line, and rest there.

Sosia did not say anything for a long moment.

“That’s bad paper,” Hordeonius said. “I cut it.”

“Yes,” said Sosia.

He did not look up. He took the stylus and entered the nothing from the Aventine, and the day’s other business, two small loans and one repayment, in his slow square hand, under Hordeonius’s line. Then he closed the book.

On the pier by the stall the figures went down the white plaster in a column, four and five and seven and nine and sixteen. Hordeonius stood in front of them for a while before he put his shutter down, and then took a piece of charcoal from the dish and, under the sixteen, wrote the date for tomorrow, and left the space beside it empty.
