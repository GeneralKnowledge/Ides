# The Road Out

POV: Lucius Antonius · Perusia, the south gate · February 40

---

The army went out first, as had been agreed, and Lucius stood on the wall above the gate and watched it go.

They went out by cohorts. He had asked for that and the young Caesar’s people had allowed it, that they should go out in order, under their centurions, with their standards, and lay down their arms on the road below the gate in proper form, the way a Roman army lays down its arms, if a Roman army ever does. He had thought it mattered. Standing on the wall in the cold morning, watching them file out through the arch below him and down the hill, he was no longer sure what it was he had thought it would do.

They were very thin. He had known that. He had seen them every day. But he had seen them in the streets of the town, among the houses, where everyone was thin, and now he saw them on the open road going down the hill in the plain grey light, and against the bare slope and the brown fields beyond, with the young Caesar’s army drawn up on either side of the road below, he saw them as the young Caesar’s army would see them. They walked like old men. Some of them were helped. One cohort near the end had a man carried on a shield by four others, and the four others were not much better than the man.

At the bottom of the hill, where the road went through the gap in Agrippa’s palisade, they stopped, cohort by cohort, and laid their shields and their swords and their spears on the ground in heaps, and then went on through the gap, empty-handed, into the young Caesar’s lines.

And the young Caesar’s men gave them bread.

He saw it from the wall. He had not been told it would happen. As each cohort came through the gap, men came forward from the ranks on either side with baskets, and broke the loaves and put them into the hands of the men coming through, and the men coming through stopped, some of them, in the road, and ate, standing there, with the bread in both hands, while the cohort behind them waited.

---

He went down at the third hour, when the last of them had gone through.

There was no reason for him to stay on the wall any longer and no reason for him to stay in the town at all. The magistrate Vettius had said goodbye to him at the door of the house, courteously, with his hands folded, and had not said anything else, and Lucius had not been able to read his face, and had stopped trying. His officers had gone out with the army. The tribune with the horse look had gone out with the army; he had stopped at the door on his way and saluted, very correctly, and said *Consul*, and gone. There was nobody left to say anything to. He went down through the town alone with his two lictors, past the trench along the north wall, which he did not look at, and the forum, where people stood in the doorways and watched him, and out through the arch of the south gate onto the road.

The young Caesar’s soldiers were lining it.

They stood on either side of the road all the way down the hill, two deep, close, shoulder to shoulder, from the gate to the gap in the palisade at the bottom, a thousand paces of them, more. They had their shields grounded and their hands on the rims. They were not in battle order and they were not on parade. They were simply there, standing, in the cold, and they had been standing there since dawn to watch the army come out, and now they watched him.

He walked down between them.

He had walked through crowds all his life. He knew how a crowd sounded. He knew what it sounded like when it was with you, the warmth of it, the noise coming up off the stones; and he knew what it sounded like when it was against you, which was a different noise, sharper, with words in it. He had been ready for that. He had lain awake for two nights in Vettius’s house getting ready for that, the words they would shout, *traitor*, *Antonius’s little brother*, *baldy*, the things on the lead bullets. He had told himself he would walk through it with his head up as a consul should.

They did not shout anything.

They stood on either side of the road and looked at him and did not make a sound. He could hear his own footsteps, and the lictors’ behind him, and the wind going through the dead grass on the slope, and a long way off, in the camp, a mule. Nothing else. A thousand men, two thousand, and not one of them spoke.

He felt it go through him, that silence, and it took him a moment, halfway down the hill, to find the name for it.

Respect. That was what it was. They were soldiers, and he was a consul of the Roman people who had held a town for three months against the young Caesar and his whole army and Agrippa’s ditch, with nothing, with farm boys and colony men and old Antonians and not enough bread, and had come out on his feet. Soldiers understood that. Whatever the young Caesar had told them about him, they had stood in that ditch all winter and watched the walls, and they knew. He held his head up. He let them look.

Near the bottom, in the front rank on the left, there was a big man with a grey beard who was weeping. He made no sound. The tears went down into his beard and he did not wipe them, and he was not looking at Lucius at all; he was looking up the hill, past him, at the gate, where there was nobody now. Lucius thought it was the wind. The wind was cold enough.

At the bottom of the hill the young Caesar was waiting at the gap in the palisade, and he went down to him through the silence and thought it was the best thing that had happened to him since the steps of Saturn.

---

He walked the last of the way to the gap.

“Consul,” said the young Caesar, and held out his hand.

Lucius took it. The hand was cold and thin and very dry, a sick man’s hand, and it held his for exactly as long as was proper, and let go.

He found that his knees were not entirely steady. He found, too, under everything else, under the respect and the silence and the cold, something he would not have said to anyone and did not say to himself in words, which was that it was over. He would sleep tonight. He would eat. Nobody would come to him at the brazier with the number of the grain. He would not have to stand on the wall at dusk and count the fires on the hills above Fulginiae, and see them go out.

“You’ll want to eat,” said the young Caesar. “And rest. You’re my guest. Everything you need.” He turned, and a man came forward with a cloak, a thick one, wool, plain, the kind a household makes. “Here. It’s cold.”

Lucius let them put it round his shoulders over the toga. It was warm. It smelled of woodsmoke and somebody else.

Behind him the lines along the road had broken the moment he was through, and men were going down the slope on both sides toward the cohorts sitting on the ground with their bread, calling out names. He heard a noise begin among them that was not cheering. He was walking into the camp by then, beside the young Caesar, and he did not look back to see what it was.
