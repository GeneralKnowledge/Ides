# The Day Before the Kalends

POV: Laberius · the ditch below Perusia · night of 31 December 41

---

They came out of the town in the last watch of the last night of the year, and the first Laberius knew of it was the dog.

There was a dog that lived under the fifth tower. It had come up the valley with the army from somewhere, the way dogs did, and attached itself to the cohort, and it slept under the platform on a pile of old sacking and ate what the men threw it, and it had never once in a month made a sound at night. It made one now. It came out from under the platform with its hackles up and stood on the bank facing the town with its legs stiff, and it did not bark. It growled, very low, a sound like a cart a long way off.

Laberius was awake already. He had been lying in the tent with his eyes open, thinking about nothing, about the drains, about the cold. He was out of the tent before he had decided to be, with his sword-belt in his hand, and the dog was still growling, and then he heard it too, below the growl: feet. A great many feet, on frozen ground, coming down the hill in the dark, trying not to be heard, and being heard.

“Up,” he said. He did not shout. He went along the tent lines kicking the poles. “Up. Up. They’re out.”

---

It was very dark. There was no moon, and the fires on the wall above had been let go down, on purpose, he understood later, so that the men coming down the hill would not be seen against them. All along the bank the young Caesar’s men were coming out of their tents half-dressed and finding their shields by feel and their places on the bank by the voices of their centurions, and from up the line, from the towers further round the hill, horns had begun, one and then another, and somewhere to the left there was already fighting, he could hear it, the sound that is like no other sound, iron and wood and men.

He found his place by the fifth tower. He found it the way he had found his place in a line for twenty years, without looking, by the feel of the men either side. He had a shield. He did not remember picking it up.

The man on his left was tall. He knew that by the height of the shield rim against his own. He knew who it was a moment later, by the breathing, which had a catch in it on the in-breath, a thing Laberius had noticed in the Marcii’s orchard in the summer with that man standing two arms’ lengths off with his hand on his knife. The tall man. He had the scar on the side of his head now where the pitchfork had caught him, under the helmet, and he stood on Laberius’s left on the bank of the young Caesar’s ditch in the dark and did not say anything, and neither did Laberius.

Then the men from the town came over the far lip of the ditch.

---

He did not see much. Afterwards, when they asked him, as they did, because the fifth tower held and the sixth did not, he could not tell them much. He told them what he could.

It was dark and they came in a mass, down the slope of the ditch and across the bottom, where the water had frozen, and up the near side toward the palisade. They had ladders, some of them, short ones, and hurdles to throw across the stakes, and they were very quiet, until they were close, and then they were very loud. He had his shield up and the tall man’s rim against his and Vibius somewhere behind him on the platform shouting for the archers, and men came up out of the ditch in front of him against the palisade, and he stabbed at them through the gaps, low, the way you were taught, at whatever moved.

There was one who got through.

He came over the top of the palisade where a hurdle had gone across it, between Laberius and the tall man, almost on top of them, and Laberius put his shield into him and they both went down on the bank in the frozen mud, and the man was under him. He was not big. He was strong and he smelled very bad, of smoke and old wool and something sweetish under it that Laberius knew and did not want to know, the smell of a man who has not eaten properly for a month. He had lost his helmet. Laberius had his forearm across the man’s throat and his sword hand free, and the sword in it, and there was a fire somewhere along the bank that had been kicked up in the fighting, and in the light of it, for a moment, he saw the man’s face.

There was a gap in his teeth. At the front. The left side.

Laberius did not move.

It was a moment. It was less than a moment. The man under him was gasping and his eyes were very wide, and they were looking up at Laberius, and in the firelight with the gap in his teeth and the dirt on him and the starved hollow cheeks it could have been anybody, and it could have been Rufrius. It could have been Rufrius on the causeway at Philippi, with the shield rims touching, saying *Mule* through the gap. Laberius looked at the face and could not tell. He could not tell. He had known Rufrius for twenty years and he could not, in the dark, by the light of a kicked fire, with his forearm on the man’s throat, tell whether it was him.

He took his arm off the man’s throat.

He did not decide to. He did it the way he had stepped between the boy and the tall man in the orchard. He took his arm away and got his knee off the man’s chest and shoved him, hard, with the flat of his shield, back toward the gap in the palisade where the hurdle was, and the man rolled and scrambled and went through the gap and over the lip and down, into the ditch, into the dark.

And the tall man on his left stepped to the lip of the ditch and put his spear down into it, once, with his whole weight, the way you put a spear into a boar in a pit, and pulled it out, and stepped back into his place with his rim against Laberius’s rim.

“He was going to get back up,” the tall man said.

---

It was over before the light. The sally failed. It failed all along the line; the men from the town were driven back up the hill to their gates with the horns going behind them, and those who did not get back up the hill stayed in the ditch.

At first light Laberius went down into the ditch below the fifth tower with the burial party. He did not have to. He went.

There were nine of them on that stretch. He looked at all of them. He found the one below the hurdle, lying on his face in the frozen water at the bottom with the spear wound in his back, and turned him over, and looked.

It was not Rufrius. It was nobody he knew. A thin man of thirty or so with a gap in his teeth and a Marsian look about the jaw, with a farmer’s hands, the nails broken, and an old bronze ring on one finger that had been made for a bigger finger and had a bit of wool wound round it to keep it on. A colony man, maybe, from some other plain. One of Lucius’s farm boys with a sword. Nobody.

Laberius stood over him in the cold.

The tall man was in the burial party too. He was on the other side of the ditch with a spade, and he saw Laberius turn the man over, and he saw Laberius look, and he went on digging. When they had finished, and were going back up the bank, he fell into step beside Laberius for a few paces, as if by chance.

“Know him?” he said.

“No.”

The tall man nodded, and went on to his own tent.

Vibius was at the fifth tower with the dog, which had gone back under the platform and was lying on its sacking with its head on its paws. Vibius had a cut over one eye and was holding a rag to it and looking very pleased with himself.

“Kalends of January,” Vibius said. “Happy new year, Centurion. They tell me you put one down on the bank with your bare hands.”

“Who told you?”

“The third cohort.”

“The third cohort,” said Laberius, “should mind its own drains.”

He went into his tent and sat down on his blanket. He took off his boots. They were Gaius’s boots, resoled twice since Mutina, the last time at Thessalonica, and there was frozen mud on them to the ankle, and on the left one, on the toe, something darker than mud. He sat and looked at it for a while, and then he took a handful of straw from the bedding and cleaned it off, carefully, the way he would have cleaned his own.
