# The Trench

POV: Lucius Antonius · Perusia · late December 41

---

They had dug the trench along the inside of the north wall, where the ground was soft and nobody lived, and it was already longer than Lucius had been told.

He walked the length of it in the early morning with the tribune who had served under Marcus in Gaul, the one with the horse look, because the tribune had asked him to and because he had found, in the last month, that he could not refuse that man anything he asked for in a quiet voice. It was cold. There was frost on the stones of the wall and on the grass along the trench lip, and the earth that had been thrown up was frozen in lumps, and the trench itself had a skin of frozen earth over it in places where they had covered it, and in places where they had not.

He paced it. He could not help pacing it. One hundred and ninety paces from the corner tower to where the digging had stopped for the night, and the spades stuck upright in the end of it, waiting.

“It was eighty at the Saturnalia,” the tribune said.

“I know what it was at the Saturnalia.”

The tribune did not answer. They walked on.

There were people on the far side of the trench, in the lane that ran along behind the houses. Not many. Some of them were looking at the trench and some were not. One was a woman with a cloth over her head, standing very still with her hands under her arms for the warmth, looking at a particular place in the trench, a place that had been covered. Lucius did not look at the place. He looked at her. She was a townswoman, not a slave, with good shoes, and when she felt him looking she raised her eyes and saw who it was and her face did nothing at all. Then she turned and went away up the lane.

---

The order had been his. He knew that. He would have known it even if the tribune had not been walking beside him in silence for a hundred and ninety paces as if to make sure he did.

It had been ten days ago, at the council in the magistrate’s house, with the brazier going and the officers round the table and the old magistrate Vettius at the end of it, courteous, with his hands folded. They had been through the grain again. They went through it every three days. The number got smaller every three days and the faces round the table got more careful about not looking at each other while it was said.

He had said that the army must be fed first, because the army was the only thing between the town and the young Caesar. Everyone had agreed with that. He had said that the citizens must be fed second, because they were citizens and their town had opened its gates to a consul. Everyone had agreed with that too, Vettius most warmly. Then there had been a silence, and in the silence somebody had said the word *slaves*, and Lucius had said, quite quickly, before anybody else could, that the slaves were to have no more rations from the public granaries, and that they were not to be let out of the gates either, because a slave let out was a slave who told the young Caesar’s men how many were left in the town and how much bread they had.

He had said it quickly so that it would be said. He remembered the feeling. It had been like pulling a tooth with your own fingers: the thing done all at once, before the hand could think.

Nobody had argued. Vettius had nodded and made a note. And that had been all.

He had not thought, then, about where the slaves would go. He had thought that their owners would feed them, out of the household stores. Some had. Most of the household stores were gone by the Saturnalia. After that the slaves had gone where there was anything to eat, which was nowhere; they had gone out into the lanes and the empty ground under the walls and pulled up the grass and eaten it, and the leaves off the vines in the gardens, and the bark of young trees. He had seen them do it. He had seen a man on his knees in the frost by the north gate pulling up handfuls of something brown and putting it in his mouth, steadily, without looking round, the way an ox eats.

And they had begun to die, and the question had been what to do with them.

“They’re asking again about burning,” the tribune said. He said it to the trench.

“No.”

“The ground is going to freeze hard in a week. They won’t be able to dig.”

“Then they dig faster.” Lucius stopped. “If we burn them the smoke goes up over the wall and every man in the young Caesar’s ditch sees it and counts it. They’ll know. They’ll know to the day how long we’ve got.”

“They know already,” said the tribune.

Lucius turned and looked at him.

The tribune looked back. He had very pale eyes in a face that had been burnt dark by twenty summers in Gaul and Spain and Macedonia, and there was nothing in them, not contempt, not pity, nothing, only the patience of a man who has been in a great many sieges, on both sides of the wall, and knows how they end.

“Everybody knows,” the tribune said. “The whole army knows. The town knows. That woman knows. The men on the young Caesar’s bank know because they can count the fires on our wall at night and they can see that there are fewer every night. They threw a bullet over yesterday that said so.” He paused. “I’m not saying burn them. I’m saying the smoke won’t tell them anything new.”

---

They went up onto the wall above the trench. From there you could see over the town one way and down into the valley the other, to Agrippa’s bank and ditch going round the foot of the hill, and the towers on it at every few hundred paces, and the smoke from the young Caesar’s camps going straight up into the cold still air in fifty places, because over there nobody was afraid to burn anything.

Lucius looked down into the town instead.

He could see the forum, and the streets going off it, and the people in the streets, not many at this hour. He could see Vettius’s house, his own house now, with the consul’s guard at the door. He could see the old temple of the town at the top of the hill, which they had told him was Vulcan’s.

They were loyal. He held on to that. They had opened the gates. They had given the consul their houses and their granaries and their sons for the wall. Nobody had come to him to complain about the slaves, not one householder, not Vettius, not anyone; they had borne it, as he had borne it; they understood that it was the war, and that the war was for them, for every town in Italy that had had its land taken and given to a soldier, and that when Marcus came and it was over, the people of Perusia would be remembered for this as the people of Praeneste were still remembered for standing by Marius.

The woman in the lane with the cloth over her head had not complained either. He had seen her face. Her face had done nothing at all.

“How many?” he said.

The tribune told him. It was the number from last night. There would be another number tonight.

Lucius nodded. He kept his eyes on the town, on the forum and the streets and the guarded door, and found that he was waiting, as he had been waiting for a month, for the sound of someone below calling his name.

Nobody did. In the lane behind the trench, where the woman had stood, a man had come out with a spade over his shoulder and was walking slowly along the lip toward the end where the digging had stopped, and Lucius watched him go the whole way, and pick his spade out of the frozen heap, and begin.
