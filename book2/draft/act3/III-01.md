# Lead

POV: Laberius · Octavian’s lines below Perusia · December 41

---

The first one came over in the morning while they were laying the turf on the bank by the fifth tower, and it hit the turf a pace from Laberius’s foot and went in up to its waist, and stayed there, grey, the size of an almond.

He dug it out with his thumb.

It was heavier than it looked. They always were. He had handled a great many of them in twenty years, picked them out of shields and out of turf and once, at Munda, out of the shoulder of a boy from Asculum with the point of a knife while the boy bit on a strap, and every time the weight surprised him, as if lead had a way of being more than itself. It was warm from the sling. It was cast in a two-piece mould, and the seam ran round it like a seam round a plum, and on one face, raised in small rough capitals, there were letters.

He turned it to the light.

He read slowly. He had learned late, in the army, from a clerk at Corfinium who had taught him in return for not being put on latrines, and he still read the way he walked a boundary, one stake at a time. *L. ANTONI*, he read. Then a word he had to look at twice. Then *PERISTI*. Then, underneath, smaller, crowded in where the mould had run out of room, *C. CAESARIS VICTORIA*.

It was one of theirs. One of the young Caesar’s, he meant, one of his own side’s, that had gone over the wall in the night and been picked up by somebody in the town and slung straight back. They did that. He had seen it at Mutina. You threw a man’s own lead back at him because it saved casting your own and because it was funny.

The word in the middle was *CALVE*.

He put his hand up to his head before he thought about it. He did it under the helmet, which made it worse, because he had to tip the helmet back to get his fingers in, and he felt the place at the crown, the smooth place, that had been the size of a denarius at Philippi and was the size of the palm of his hand now, and that nobody had mentioned to him since Gaius died. Gaius had mentioned it most days. Gaius had said it was where the brains were getting out.

*Lucius Antonius, baldy, you’re done for. Victory to Gaius Caesar.*

He stood on the bank with the lead in his palm and his other hand on top of his head, and then he understood that it was not about him, it was about the consul, who was bald too, everybody knew it, the consul had been bald since he was thirty and there had been jokes in the Forum about it for years. He took his hand off his head. He put his helmet straight.

He laughed. It came up out of him all at once, a short bark, and the two men laying turf beside him looked round, and he turned it into a cough and bent to pick up the next turf.

---

By the afternoon everyone was doing it.

The word went along the lines the way word does, from tower to tower, faster than any runner, and by the ninth hour every man on the bank who could read was reading lead and every man who could not was carrying it to someone who could. They came over from the town in handfuls now. It was not a fight. Nobody on the wall was aiming. They were swinging the slings from behind the parapet the way boys swing them at crows, high and lazy, so that the bullets came down out of the sky onto the bank and the ditch and the turf like hail, and men ran out under them laughing to pick them up.

Some were the young Caesar’s, coming home. Some were new. The new ones had been cast in the town, in moulds that somebody in there had cut overnight with a knife and a great deal of care, and the letters were rough and backwards in places, because whoever cut the mould had forgotten that it would come out the other way round.

A big man from the third cohort brought one to Laberius at the turn of the afternoon, because word had gone round that the centurion at the fifth tower could read, and held it out on his palm.

Laberius read it. He read it twice, to be sure.

“Well?” said the big man.

It was about the young Caesar. It was about a part of the young Caesar, and what the men on the wall would like to do to it, and it used a word Laberius had not seen written down since a latrine wall at Brundisium, spelled the way it sounded. He told the big man. The big man went away to tell everybody, and Laberius heard it go down the line, the roar of it, from the fifth tower to the sixth and on round the hill.

There was one about Fulvia. He did not read that one aloud. He held it in his hand for a while, turning it, and thought about the woman on the flat ground below the temple at Praeneste with her husband’s sword knocking on her knee, that he had heard about in the colony, and he put the bullet in his belt-pouch, and the next man who asked what it said he told it was about the consul’s hair.

There was one, late, that came down alone, after the others had stopped. A small one, cast badly, with the seam off true. It had two words on it, and they were not a joke, and he did not know at first what they were, and when he did, he did not want to.

*ESUREIS ET ME CELAS*.

You are hungry, and you hide it from me.

He did not know which side had cut it. He looked at it a long time. It could have been from the bank to the town, a jeer: we know you’re starving, don’t pretend. It could have been from the town to the bank, from some man on the wall who had been at Philippi with the man he was throwing at, and had watched the young Caesar’s ditch-diggers eating bread at noon for a month, and was saying something else entirely.

He put it in the pouch with the one about Fulvia.

---

Vibius came down off the platform at dusk with his helmet in his hand and sat on the bank beside him.

“I heard you laughed,” he said.

“I coughed.”

“That’s not what the third cohort says. The third cohort says the centurion at the fifth tower read the first bullet and put his hand on his head and laughed like a mule.” He looked sideways at Laberius. “Was it about you?”

“It was about the consul.”

“Is the consul bald?”

“The consul’s bald.”

“Ah,” said Vibius. He looked up at the wall, where they were lighting the night fires, one by one, along the parapet. “Then it was about the consul.”

They sat. Along the bank men were still going through their handfuls of lead in the last of the light, reading them out to each other, the filthy ones loudest. Somebody had begun a song made of them. Somebody else on the wall heard it and sang back a verse of his own, and the ditch laughed, and the wall laughed, and for a while there was a war going on between the two of them in which nobody was hurt at all.

Laberius put his hand into the pouch at his belt and felt the two bullets there, the one about Fulvia and the small one with the seam off true, warm from his body now, and heavier than they looked.

He did not take them out. After a while he got up and went to see about the drains.
