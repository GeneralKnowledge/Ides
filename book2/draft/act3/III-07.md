# Terms

POV: Octavian · Octavian’s lines below Perusia · February 40

---

Lucius had lost so much weight that his toga did not hang right, and the first thing Octavian thought, watching him come down the hill from the gate between his two lictors, was that somebody should have taken it in.

It was a stupid thing to think. He knew that. He thought it anyway, standing at the gap in the palisade where the road went through with Agrippa on one side of him and the guard behind, and he went on thinking it as Lucius came down: that the toga had been made for a man with a consul’s belly, and that the belly was gone, and the folds that should have lain across it lay across nothing and dragged, and the consul had to keep gathering them up on his left arm as he walked, as if he were carrying a child.

He had been asked to wait at the line. He had not waited. When Lucius was halfway down the hill he walked out through the gap in the palisade onto the road, alone, without the guard, and Agrippa said his name once behind him, sharply, and then did not say it again, because it was done.

They met on the bare ground below the ditch, where the dead of the night sally had lain until the burial parties came. There was nothing there now. Trodden earth and frost and the marks of feet.

“Caesar,” Lucius said.

“Consul.”

Lucius smiled at that. It was the old smile, the warm one that Octavian had seen across the Senate house and the Forum and, once, at a dinner in the Carinae before Mutina, when they had both been pretending to be friends. It looked strange now on a face with the bones showing.

“For a few more days,” said Lucius. “Isn’t it? Then I’m nobody. A man with a brother.”

---

They talked for a long time. Afterwards Agrippa told him it had been nearly an hour, the two of them walking slowly up and down on the trodden ground below the ditch, with the army watching from the bank on one side and the town watching from the wall on the other, and nobody able to hear a word.

Lucius did most of the talking. He always had.

He did not ask for his life. Octavian had expected him to, and had prepared for it; he had lain awake the night before going through what he would say, the words for it, the right amount of hesitation. Lucius did not ask. He said, almost at once, walking, with the folds of the toga gathered up on his arm, that he had made the war, not his soldiers, and not the people of Perusia, and not anyone else, and that if Caesar wanted somebody to punish, here he was, and he would be grateful if Caesar would take him and let the rest go.

He said it plainly. He said it without any speech in it at all, which in Lucius was so unusual that Octavian looked at him.

He said he had done it for the Republic. He said he had believed, and still believed, that the land of Italy should not be taken from the men who farmed it by a decree of three men in a tent, and that the consuls should govern as their fathers had, and that he had wanted his brother to lay down the triumvirate and had thought, if he forced the question, his brother would. He said he had been wrong about that. He said it as a man says he was wrong about the weather.

“And Marcus?” Octavian said.

Lucius walked several paces before he answered.

“I don’t know,” he said. “I don’t know what Marcus wanted. I never asked him.” He looked at Octavian sideways, with a kind of rueful honesty that was very winning and that Octavian thought he had probably used on juries. “That’s the truth. I wrote him a great many letters telling him what I was doing, and I never once asked him if it was what he wanted, and he never once told me. You can have that, if it’s any use to you. I don’t suppose it is.”

Octavian walked beside him and said nothing.

It was of use. It was of more use than anything else Lucius could have said. It meant that Marcus would be able to say, when he came, and he would come, that none of this had been his; that his brother and his wife had made a war in his name without his orders; and that he, Marcus, had no quarrel with his colleague the young Caesar, none at all. It meant that Marcus would need Octavian to agree with that. It meant that the man walking beside him with his toga gathered on his arm had just handed him, without knowing it or perhaps knowing it perfectly well, the whole of the next year.

He looked at Lucius’s face. He could not tell which.

---

He had decided before he came out through the gap. He had decided in the night. That was the thing nobody would ever know, not Agrippa, not Maecenas: that the hour on the trodden ground had changed nothing at all.

Lucius would live. He had to live. He was Marcus’s brother, and a dead brother would be a thing Marcus could not forgive in front of the legions, whatever he felt in private. Lucius would be treated with every honour, and sent away somewhere honourable, and forgotten.

The soldiers would live. Lucius’s soldiers, the Antonian veterans and the colony men and the farm boys with swords. They would be pardoned and taken into his army. They were the point of the whole thing. They were what Italy was made of now.

The townspeople were a different matter. And the men of rank in the town, the senators and knights who had come up from Rome to Lucius at Praeneste with their cooks and then gone on with him to Perusia, believing it would hold; the ones who had written letters, and been seen, and could be named. They were a different matter too.

He did not say any of that. He walked with Lucius on the frozen ground and listened to him talk about the Republic, and he nodded in the right places, and when Lucius said, at the end, with a catch in his voice that was either very good acting or not acting at all, *Spare the town, Caesar, they opened their gates to a consul, that’s all they did*, Octavian said that he would do what was just.

Lucius looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded, as if that were an answer.

---

Agrippa was waiting at the gap in the palisade.

“Well?” he said.

“He surrenders. Tomorrow. The army comes out and lays down its arms on the road. He comes out after.” Octavian pulled his cloak up round his chin. He was cold; he had been cold for an hour and had not felt it. “Have them fed. All of them, his and ours, the same rations, as they come out. Bread first. Not too much at once, they’ll be sick.”

“And Lucius?”

“Lucius is my guest.”

Agrippa looked at him. Then he looked past him, up the hill, where Lucius was going back toward the gate between his two lictors, slowly, with the folds of his toga gathered on his arm, and the faces along the wall above him watching him come.

“He thinks you’ve spared the town,” Agrippa said.

“He asked me to.”

“And?”

Octavian watched the gate open for Lucius, and close behind him.

“And I said I would do what was just,” he said, and went back through the gap toward his tent, where there was a fire, and a cup of warm water with honey in it, and the list of names that Maecenas had brought from Rome in December, which he had not yet read through to the end.
