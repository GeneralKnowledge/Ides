# Seals

POV: Nysa · Rome, the young Caesar’s house · January 40

---

The courier came on the eighth day and on the sixteenth and on the twenty-fourth, as near as the roads allowed, and by the middle of January Nysa could tell from the way he came up the Ring-makers’ Stairs what kind of bag he had.

He was a short bow-legged Umbrian who had been a muleteer, and he rode from the camp at Perusia to Rome by the Flaminian Way in four days if the bridges held, and he carried two bags. The big one was for the house. It had the young Caesar’s letters in it, to Glycon’s deputy and to his sister and to Maecenas when Maecenas was in Rome, and orders for things the camp needed that the house could send, and once a long letter to the old freedwoman with the bad eyes, about a cloak, which she had made Nysa read to her three times. The small bag was for the wives.

That had been nobody’s idea. It had happened. In December a woman had come to the door, a centurion’s wife from the Aventine, with a letter in her hand, and asked whether the young Caesar’s courier would take it to her husband in the camp, and Glycon’s deputy, who was a soft man, had said yes. Then there had been two women. Then there had been twenty. Now there was a basket inside the door on the courier’s day, and the women came up the stairs in the morning and put their letters in it and went away, and Nysa, because she was at the door and because she counted, had been told to keep a list of them so that nothing went astray.

She kept the list. She wrote down each letter as it went into the basket: the woman’s name, the man’s name, his cohort if she knew it. And when the courier came back eight days later with the small bag, she took the letters out of it and matched them against the list, the answers to the letters, and sent a boy round the city with them, and crossed the names off.

Some letters did not come back with answers. Some came back themselves.

---

The first one came back on the sixteenth day of December. She had not understood it, at first. It was the centurion’s wife’s letter, the one from the Aventine, the first one, and it came out of the small bag with its seal unbroken, the woman’s seal, a little cheap ring with a bird on it, still whole on the cord, and someone at the camp had written on the outside of it with a stylus, in a clerk’s hand, the man’s name and cohort and one word.

She had held it for a long time.

She took it to Glycon’s deputy. He looked at it and went white and said that he would take it to the woman himself, and he did, and he came back in the afternoon and went to his room and did not come out until the next day.

After that she did not take them to Glycon’s deputy. She took them herself.

She found that she could tell, now, before she opened the small bag. Not which ones, but whether. She could tell by the weight of it in her hand when the courier gave it to her, because the letters that came back unbroken were heavier than answers: they were the wives’ letters, written on good wax in good tablets that the wives had bought for the purpose, and the answers from the camp were on whatever the men could find, scraps of board, half a tablet, once a piece of bark. A bag with a lot of answers in it was light. A bag that was heavy had good tablets in it, coming home.

She did not tell anyone this. It was not a thing to tell.

---

By the middle of January she had a column for them. She kept it on the back of the list, the other side of the tablet, where nobody else looked, and she kept it the way she kept every count, in Greek, in rows of ten. There was no name in it, only a stroke. One stroke for each letter that had come back with its seal whole. She had not decided to keep it. She had found herself doing it the first time, with the centurion’s wife’s letter in her other hand, and had gone on.

The night sally had been on the last day of the year. Everyone in Rome knew about it by the fourth of January, because the courier had come in early with the news, riding his mule half to death, and the young Caesar’s letter in the big bag had been about it. The young Caesar had written that the enemy had come out in force in the night and had been driven back everywhere with great loss, and that the wall had held, and that there was no cause for concern in the city. Glycon’s deputy had read it aloud in the atrium to the household. The women had cheered.

The small bag that came with that letter had been the heaviest she had held.

She had counted it in her room, after, by the lamp. She had laid them out on the bed. Then she had put them back in the bag, and gone out with the boy at first light, and been round the city all morning, the Aventine and the Subura and the Esquiline and down to the river, and in each house she had put the letter into a woman’s hands and said nothing and watched the woman see the seal.

Twenty-three strokes, at the middle of January. She knew the number the way she knew her own name.

---

The young Caesar did not write for nine days.

It had never happened. His letters came in every big bag, short, in his small careful hand, to Glycon’s deputy and to his sister. They were about the house, mostly: the accounts, the lamps, a tunic he wanted sent, warm, not new, one of the ones the women had made. He wore what his household made. Everyone said so; it was a thing about him, like his not eating, and the women in the wool room were proud of it in a way they did not say. When he wanted a tunic he wrote for it.

On the twenty-fourth day the courier came up the stairs with the big bag and there was nothing in it from the young Caesar at all.

There was a letter from Agrippa to Glycon’s deputy, about mules. There was a letter from Maecenas, who was in the camp, to someone else. There was nothing in the small careful hand.

Nysa stood at the door with the big bag in one hand and the small bag in the other, and she weighed the small one without meaning to, and it was light, and she felt the relief go through her and then, at once, behind it, something else.

The young Caesar had been ill at Philippi. He had been ill at Brundisium, and the whole of Italy had heard that he was dead. He had been ill in the autumn when she counted the storeroom. He was always ill. And there was a fever in the camp at Perusia, the courier said so, the men in the ditch were going down with it in the wet; and he had not written.

She did not say anything to anyone. She went on with the day. She sorted the small bag and sent the boy round with the answers and crossed the names off the list. In the afternoon the old freedwoman with the bad eyes asked her whether there had been anything about the cloak, and she said not this time, and the old woman nodded and went back to her wool.

That night she lay in her room in the dark and did a count she had never done before, a count of something that had not happened, and could not stop. The house. The people in it. What would happen to the house if. To the women in the wool room. To Glycon’s deputy and the cook and the litter-men and the porter. To herself, who owed him obedience under the law as her patron and had nothing else in Rome but a door to sit at and a stool. The house of a dead man’s heir. She had been in one of those before. She knew how fast the masks came down off the walls.

---

The letter came on the twenty-seventh day, three days late, by a different rider, who had been held at the bridge at Ocriculum by the floods.

It was short. It was in the small careful hand. It said that the young Caesar was well, and that the rains had brought the river up and cut the road, and that he wanted the brown tunic sent, the old one with the darned elbow, because the camp was cold and the new one was too good to sit in a wet tent in. It said nothing about the sally, or the dead.

Glycon’s deputy read it aloud in the atrium and the women laughed at the darned elbow, and the old freedwoman with the bad eyes, who had darned it, sat up very straight on her bench and did not laugh, and was pleased.

Nysa went to find the brown tunic.

It was in the chest in the wool room, folded, where she had put it herself in the autumn. She took it out and shook it and looked at the darn on the elbow, which was very neat, and folded it again to go in the big bag. Then she sat down on the chest with the tablet on her knee and turned it over to the back, where the strokes were, twenty-three of them in rows of ten, and looked at them for a while; and she did not add anything to the column, because there was nothing to add, and she did not rub any of them out.
