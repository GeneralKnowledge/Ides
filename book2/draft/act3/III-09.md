# Vulcan’s

POV: Laberius · Perusia · March 40

---

The smoke went up first from the top of the town, near the old temple, a single thick column, and Laberius saw it from the gate and thought: that’s started, then.

He had been told it would. Everyone on the bank had been told, for three days, since the army came out and the consul went into the young Caesar’s tent: that the town had held out against the triumvir, and that the triumvir’s soldiers had dug in the cold all winter, and that a town which holds out is a town which is given to the soldiers who took it. That was how it went. Nobody had said it to Laberius in words. Nobody needed to. He had been in four towns that were given to the soldiers who took them, in Spain and in Gaul, and he knew what it smelled like before it started.

So when he came up through the south gate that morning with the third cohort, under orders to hold the street from the gate to the forum, and saw the smoke going up from the top of the town, he thought it was the looting. Somebody had got in early. Somebody always did. They had broken into a house and fired it to cover what they had taken, or for no reason, the way men fire things in a town that has been given to them.

---

The fire came down the hill.

He watched it come. He stood at the corner of the street below the forum with his men behind him and watched it come down from the top of the town the way he had watched the stubble burn on his father’s fields in Picenum when he was a boy, in the autumn, when the wind was right: not fast, not all at once, but steady, house to house, roof to roof, as if it knew where it was going. There was a wind off the hills. It came down the slope of the town from the north and carried the fire with it, and the old houses were built close, wall against wall, with timber roofs, and there had been no rain for a week.

He knew which way it would go. That was the thing he could do. He could not tell good soil from bad, but he could look at a fire and a wind and a slope and know which streets would go and in what order, as he could look at a ditch and know where the bank would slump. It would come down the north side of the forum and jump the street and take the west side. It would miss the east side for a while, because the street there was wider and the houses were stone to the first floor. It would take the gate in the end. It would take everything.

Except the temple at the top. He looked up through the smoke at the old temple on the crown of the hill, where the fire had started, and saw that it was not burning. It stood up out of the smoke with its pediment and its columns, black against the black, with the fire on every side of it and not on it. He did not understand that. He looked at it for some time.

Then a man came running down the street from the forum with his tunic singed and his hands black, one of the young Caesar’s men from another cohort, and Laberius stopped him with a hand on his chest.

“Who started it?”

“Some old fool.” The man was gasping. “One of the townsmen. Rich one, big house up by the temple. Calls himself the Macedonian. Cestius. He set fire to his own house. Stood in the doorway and set fire to it himself, with a torch, and then went in.” He wiped his face with his black hand and made it worse. “Didn’t want the soldiers to have it, they’re saying. Wanted to go with it. And the wind took it next door, and next door again, and now look.”

Laberius let him go.

---

There was a house on the west side of the street, below the forum, with its door open and smoke coming out of the top of it, and an old woman in the doorway.

She was not doing anything. She was standing in the doorway with her hands at her sides, looking out at the street, at the soldiers, at the smoke coming down, as if she were waiting for someone to come along the street and tell her what to do. Behind her, inside the house, he could hear the fire, a sound like rain on a roof, getting louder.

He went across the street. He did not decide to. He went across it the way he had gone into the Marcii’s orchard, his legs doing it, and he took the old woman by the arm.

“Out,” he said. “Come on, mother. Out.”

She did not move. She was very light. He could feel the bones of her arm through the wool, like the bones of a bird. She looked up at him with eyes that had gone pale with age and said something in a language he did not know, the old language of the town, the Etruscan, soft, with a lot of breath in it, and pulled her arm out of his hand and went back inside.

He went in after her.

The atrium was full of smoke. He could not see the roof. He found her by the household shrine, in the corner by the door, a little painted niche in the wall with a roof like a tiny temple, and she was reaching up into it with both hands. She was taking something down. He did not wait to see what. He picked her up, the whole of her, the way he would have picked up a man who had fallen in a river crossing, over his shoulder, and she weighed nothing at all, and he carried her out through the door into the street with the smoke coming after them.

He set her down on the far side, by the stone houses, against a wall. She sat down on the ground. She was holding something against her chest with both hands, and when she had got her breath, she held it out to him.

It was a bronze figure, the length of his hand. A young man in a short tunic, dancing, with a cup in one hand and a horn in the other, the way they always were. A household god. The paint had worn off it long ago and the bronze had gone dark and smooth with being handled, and one foot was missing.

He did not take it. She held it out, and said something in the soft breathing language, and held it out further, and pushed it at him, and when he still did not take it she put it into his hand and closed his fingers over it with her own, which were dry and very cold, and then she let go and turned her face to the wall.

---

The house went at noon. He watched it go from the stone side of the street. The fire came down the west side exactly as he had known it would, and took the house with the painted shrine, and the house next to it, and the next, and in the end, in the afternoon, it took the gate.

By evening the whole town was burning except the temple at the top. He could see it from the bank when they had been pulled back out of the streets: Perusia on its hill against the dark, all one fire from the walls to the crown, and on the crown, standing up out of it, the old temple, untouched, with the light of the burning town on its columns. Someone on the bank said it was Vulcan’s. The god of fire. Someone else said that was a joke, and someone else said it was not a joke, it was a sign, and they argued about that for a while, and Laberius did not join in.

He did not know what had happened to the old woman. When they were pulled back out of the streets she had not been by the wall. He had looked.

Vibius found him sitting on the bank with his pack open beside him.

“You were in there,” Vibius said. “The third cohort says you went into a house.”

“I went into a house.”

“What for?”

Laberius had the bronze figure in his hand, inside the pack, wrapped in his spare tunic. He could feel the shape of it through the wool, the cup and the horn and the place where the foot was missing.

“Nothing,” he said. “There was nothing in it.”

He did up the pack. Vibius sat down beside him, and they watched the town burn.
