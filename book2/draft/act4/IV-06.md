# The Name

POV: Octavian · the Appian Way, south of Capua · August 40

---

He said the name on purpose, at Capua, to see.

They were drawn up in the old colony forum outside the town, the men he had called out of their farms in July, five or six thousand of them, veterans of Philippi and of Mutina and of the ditch at Perusia, standing in their cohorts on the trodden ground in the heat with their shields grounded. He had come out to speak to them before the march south. Agrippa had told him not to. Maecenas, who was in Rome arranging things, had written to tell him not to. He had come out anyway, on a horse, because he had found in the last year that there were things he needed to see for himself.

He told them the facts. He was good at the facts. He told them that a fleet had come across from Greece, two hundred ships, and was at Brundisium; that the people of Brundisium had shut their gates against it, as was their right; that the fleet had brought with it Domitius Ahenobarbus, who had been condemned for the murder of Caesar and had been a pirate on the Adriatic since Philippi; that Sextus Pompeius was raiding the coast of Italy from Sicily, and had landed at Thurii and Consentia, and was starving Rome. He gave them the numbers. He did not raise his voice.

Then he said: “The fleet is Marcus Antonius’s.”

He was watching their faces when he said it. He had placed himself so that he could, with the sun behind him, high on the horse, looking down along the front ranks.

The name went along them the way a wind goes along standing grain.

He saw it. He saw it in the front rank and he saw it go back into the second and the third, a shifting, a turning of heads toward the man beside, a sound that was not a sound so much as a lot of men breathing out at once. He saw a centurion in the second cohort look down at his feet. He saw a big grey-bearded man near the end of the first rank close his eyes for a moment, as if something had hurt him. He saw three or four men in the fourth cohort look at each other and then, very carefully, at nothing.

Then it was gone, and they were standing in their ranks again in the heat, looking up at him, waiting for the rest.

He gave them the rest. He told them that Antonius had made an alliance with the murderer of his father and with the son of Pompey, and that the city of Brundisium was being besieged by a Roman triumvir for the crime of shutting its gates, and that he, Caesar, was going south to relieve it, and that he expected every man who had been given land in Italy by Caesar to come with him and defend it.

They cheered at the end. They cheered properly, the way soldiers cheer when they have been told to, with the centurions starting it. It went up off the trodden ground and the sun was very hot and he rode back to the town with Agrippa beside him and his head aching.

---

“Well?” said Agrippa, in the house, when the door was shut.

“They’ll come.”

“They’ll come,” Agrippa agreed. He was standing by the window. He did not sit; he hardly ever sat now. “They’ll march to Brundisium. They’ll dig. They’ll stand in a line. I’m not sure they’ll fight.”

Octavian sat down on the couch. He was tired; he had been tired since the spring and could not shake it, and there was a heat in him behind the eyes in the afternoons that he did not tell anyone about.

He thought about the faces in the front rank.

He had seen fear in them. That was what it had been, when the name went along the line: fear. They had stood in the ditch at Perusia all winter and watched what happened to an army that had followed the name Antonius into a war without Antonius, and now Antonius himself had come, with two hundred ships and the East behind him, and they were afraid of him. That was natural. He was afraid of him too. Everyone in Italy over the age of twenty had seen Antonius at the head of an army and remembered it. Fear was useful. Frightened men dug deep ditches and stood close in line. He had made a war with frightened men at Perusia and won it without a battle.

“They’re afraid,” he said.

Agrippa turned from the window and looked at him for a long moment, with the expression he had sometimes, which was not quite a smile and was not quite anything else.

“Yes,” said Agrippa. “Some of them.”

He did not say what the others were. He turned back to the window, and Octavian did not ask.

---

They married him to Scribonia the week before he left Rome.

It had been quick. Maecenas had arranged it in the spring, through Libo, with a great many letters and one long afternoon in the small room off the atrium with figs that nobody ate; and in June she had come to the house above the Ring-makers’ Stairs with her women and her chests and her own coverlet and had moved into a room that had been painted yellow for her. She was thirty. She had a long, intelligent, rather tired face and a dry voice, and she had looked round the yellow room on her first evening and said, to nobody in particular, that it would do; and the household, which had been terrified of her for a month, had relaxed.

He had married her because her brother was Sextus’s father-in-law. Everybody knew that. She knew it. On the wedding night she had sat on the end of the bed in the yellow room and said, quite kindly, that she hoped her brother’s son-in-law would stop taking the grain ships now, because she had been married to two men before for less useful reasons and would like this one to be worth it. He had laughed. He had not expected to.

Sextus had gone on taking the grain ships. Sextus had landed at Thurii. Sextus was, the last letters said, talking to Antonius.

He did not think much about Scribonia on the road south. He thought about the faces in the front rank.

---

They were on the Appian Way below Beneventum, in the hills, on the fourth day, when he had to get down from the horse.

He did it as if he wanted to. He told the escort he would walk for a while, to see the column, and he got down and walked by the roadside in the dust with his hand on the horse’s neck, and the escort walked their horses behind him, and he kept his face toward the column so that they could not see it.

The heat behind his eyes had come down into his chest. It did that. It had done it at Philippi and at Brundisium and in the spring of the year before, and each time he had been carried, and each time the story had gone round the army that he was dying, or dead, and each time he had got up. He could feel the sweat on his back, cold. He kept walking. The column went past him on the road, cohort after cohort, the men he had spoken to at Capua, in their dust, with their shields on their backs and their eyes on the road ahead.

He watched their faces as they passed. He could not help it now.

At the end of one cohort, near the back, there was a centurion walking beside his men who was not looking at the road. He was looking at Octavian, steadily, as he came level, a broad grey man in his forties with a scar down one forearm and the stubborn walk of a man who has marched in the same boots for a long time. He did not salute. He looked, as a man looks at a horse he has been told is sound and has decided to judge for himself. Then the cohort went on, and he went on with it, and did not look back.

Octavian stood by the roadside with his hand on the horse’s neck until the heat in his chest went down. It went down. It always did.

When he got back on the horse his hands were steady, and he rode on south toward Brundisium at the head of an army he had told himself was afraid, and did not look again at the faces in the column.
