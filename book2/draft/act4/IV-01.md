# Luggage

POV: Fulvia · Brundisium · March 40

---

Antyllus had packed his own chest, and she found out at Brundisium what was in it.

It had been his idea. At Praeneste, the night the news came about Perusia, with the house turned over and the women running and Manius shouting in the court for mules, the boy had come to her in the room over the court and asked, very formally, with his hands at his sides, whether he might pack his own things. He was seven. He had a little chest of his own, cedar, with a bronze lock, that Marcus had given him before he went east. She had said yes because she had no time to say anything else, and had not thought about it again for nine days of roads.

Now the chest was on the quay at Brundisium with the rest, in a heap by the steps down to the boats, in the wind, and the porters were taking things down to the boat in no order she could see, and one of them picked up the cedar chest by its handle and the lid came open and everything in it fell out on the stones.

There was a wooden sword. There was a toga, the little bordered one that was too warm, folded very badly. There was a top and a whip for it. There was a bag of knucklebones. There was a clay lamp in the shape of a foot, broken, that she had thrown out at the Carinae a year ago and that he must have taken out of the rubbish. And there was a lead sling bullet, grey, the size of an almond, with letters on it.

She picked that up herself before the porter could.

She knew what it was. Everybody in Italy knew what they were by now. They had been coming out of Perusia in men’s pouches and belts since the surrender, and the soldiers sold them in the colonies for a drink, and the ones that sold best were the ones about her. She turned it to the light. It was not about her. It was about Lucius’s head, and his hair, and it said that he was done for.

“Where did you get this?” she said.

Antyllus was standing by the chest with his face scarlet, holding the knucklebones.

“A soldier,” he said. “At the gate at Praeneste. He gave it me. He said it was a lucky one.” He looked at the bullet in her hand. “He said Uncle Lucius came out alive, so it was a lucky one.”

She gave it back to him.

---

Plancus was on the quay. That was the other thing.

He had come down from Spoletium with a few hundred horse, without his legions, which he had left behind him in Umbria to be taken over by whoever wanted them, and he had come to Brundisium by a different road and got there a day before her and found a ship. He was very charming about it. He had come down the quay to meet her litter with his hands out, in a good cloak, with his hair oiled, and said that he was at her service and that his ship was hers and that he would see her safe to her husband if it was the last thing he did in Italy.

It was the last thing he did in Italy. She did not say that. She thanked him and let him hand her down.

She did not ask him about Fulginiae, and he did not mention it. She watched him not mention it. He talked instead about the wind, which was fair, he said, a good north-westerly that would take them across to Greece in three days if it held, and about the ship, which was a good one, Rhodian, fast, and about Athens, where Marcus was said to be, or to be going, or to have been. He talked very well. He had always talked very well. She stood on the quay with her hands under her cloak and listened to him and thought that she had called him a coward at the corner of the wall at Praeneste with the fig tree growing out of it, and that it had been a comfortable thing to think, and that she did not have the energy to think it now.

He had left his army to come here. He had left his legions in Umbria for the young Caesar to pick up like a purse in the road. She did not know what that made him. She did not have the energy for that either.

---

Claudia came down the quay with the nurse and Iullus. Claudia was carrying her own bundle, the one she had brought back from the young Caesar’s house, the small one tied with a strap. She had refused to let anyone else carry it all the way from Praeneste. She stood by the steps and looked at the ship and at the water, which was grey and choppy in the wind, and at the long low line of the harbour mole going out into it.

“Are we going to Father?” Iullus said. He was nearly three and asked everything twice.

“We’re going to your father.”

“Across the sea?”

“Across the sea.”

He looked at the sea. He looked at it with his father’s chin and the nurse’s way of standing, holding the nurse’s hand with both of his, as he held everything.

Fulvia looked at it too.

She had always liked the sea. That was a thing nobody knew about her, because nobody had ever asked. As a girl she had been taken to Baiae in the summers and had stood on the terrace of her mother’s friend’s villa looking at the bay, and had thought that the sea was the only thing in the world that did not want anything from her. She thought it now. Three days, Plancus said. Three days with nothing to do but sit on a deck in the wind, and nobody to send for, and no courier coming up the road with a horse he had ridden to death, and no letters. At the end of it, Marcus. She would stand in front of him and find out.

It seemed to her, standing on the quay, that once she was on the water, nothing in Italy could reach her. Not the young Caesar. Not the story that was already going round about the Ides and the altar and the three hundred, which she had heard on the road at Beneventum from a muleteer and did not believe and could not stop thinking about. Not Lucius, who was alive, in the young Caesar’s camp, a guest. Not anything.

---

She went down the steps to the boat first, because the children would not go until she did.

Halfway down she had to stop. It happened sometimes now. It had begun in the winter, at Praeneste, and she had told nobody, not her woman, not Manius, not Claudia: a heaviness in the chest when she climbed a stair, and a shortness, as if someone had put a hand flat on her breastbone and pressed. It went if she stood still. She stood still on the steps with her hand on the wet stone of the quay wall, and looked at the boat below her, and waited for it to go.

“Mother?” said Claudia, above her.

“The steps are wet.”

It went. She went on down. The boatman handed her in, and she sat in the stern, and the nurse came down with Iullus, and Claudia with her bundle, and last of all Antyllus, holding his cedar chest in both arms with the lid shut and his chin on the lid, refusing the porter, and they put off across the grey water toward Plancus’s ship.

She did not look back at Italy. She looked at the ship. It was a good one, as Plancus had said, Rhodian, long and low, with a red eye painted on the bow, and men on the yard getting the sail ready, and Plancus in the stern of it already, waving.

Beside her Antyllus had opened the lid of his chest a crack and was looking in, as if to make sure that everything was still there.
