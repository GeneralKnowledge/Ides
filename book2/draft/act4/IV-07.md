# Sicyon

POV: Fulvia · Sicyon · summer 40

---

The light came off the gulf in the afternoons and filled the room, and she had stopped asking them to close the shutters against it.

It was a good room. The house was on the terrace below the old town, where the rich men of Sicyon had built their villas to look north across the water to the mountains on the other side, and her room was at the end of the house, with a window on the gulf and a door onto a small garden with a well, and a vine on a trellis that made a green shade over the door in the mornings. In the afternoons the sun came round and the vine did not help, and the light came in off the water, very bright, and lay across the floor and up the bed and over her hands on the blanket.

She lay and looked at her hands in it.

They had got thin. She had noticed that in Athens, and then stopped noticing, and now she noticed it again. The keys were not on them. She had given the keys to her woman at Athens, because they were heavy, and her woman had them now on her own belt, and Fulvia could hear them sometimes in the passage outside, going about the house.

---

Marcus had gone twelve days ago.

He had not come to say goodbye. She had known he would not. He had sent his steward the evening before, a polite Greek, to say that the triumvir was sailing at first light for Corcyra with the fleet, and then for Italy, and that the lady was to remain at Sicyon, where the air was good, until she was recovered, and that every provision had been made. The steward had stood at the foot of the bed with his hands folded and said it beautifully. She had thanked him.

At first light she had heard the fleet go. You could hear it from this house, down on the water, the horns on the ships and the oars all together, and the shouting, and then the horns again further off. She had lain and listened to it go up the gulf until she could not hear it any more. Then her woman had come in with the water and opened the shutters, and the gulf had been empty and bright.

He would come back. That was what she had thought then, lying there, listening to the horns. He had gone to Italy to settle with the young Caesar, and when it was settled he would come back for them, or send for them, and they would go to Alexandria, or to Rome, or wherever he was. He was angry, still. It would pass. It was weather.

She had thought that for several days. She had not thought it for some days now.

---

Claudia came in the afternoons. She came with her sewing and sat on the stool by the bed, in the light, and sewed, and sometimes talked and mostly did not. She had been doing it since Athens. She had not been asked.

“Is it hot?” Claudia said.

“It’s light. It’s not hot.”

“Do you want the shutters?”

“No.”

Claudia sewed. She was making a tunic for Iullus, who had grown out of everything on the ship, a small one, plain, in a brown wool she had bought from a woman in the market at Sicyon with her own money, with the coins she had kept in the bundle from the young Caesar’s house. Fulvia watched her hands. They were her own hands at sixteen, quick and narrow, and the needle went in and came out.

“They’re saying in the market,” Claudia said, without looking up, “that there was a battle at Brundisium. Or that there wasn’t. Or that there’s going to be.”

“There won’t be.”

Claudia looked up then.

“The soldiers won’t,” Fulvia said. “His or the young Caesar’s. Not against each other. Not now. They’ve done it once and they didn’t like it.” She moved her hand on the blanket, into the light. “They’ll make a peace. Pollio and Maecenas and some others. They’ll sit in a room and make a peace and the soldiers will stand outside and wait for it, and when it’s made there’ll be a wedding. There’s always a wedding.”

“Whose?”

Fulvia looked at her daughter.

She had not said it to anyone. She had not said it to herself in words. There had been a great many things she had not said, all her life, because they were weak or because they were useful to somebody else or because she did not need to; she had been the wife of Clodius and of Curio and of Marcus Antonius, and she had learned early what not to say. She lay in the light and found that there was nothing left that it would cost her to say, and nobody left to use it.

“His,” she said. “When I’m dead.”

Claudia’s needle stopped.

“He’ll marry the young Caesar’s sister,” Fulvia said. “Octavia. She was widowed this year. Marcellus died in the spring. He’ll marry her, and the young Caesar will be his brother, and that will be the peace.” She was quiet for a moment. “I’d have done it. If I were them. It’s what I’d have done.”

“Mother.”

“I was afraid of it,” Fulvia said. “All last year. That’s why I did all of it. Praeneste. The sword. Not for the land. Not for the farmers. Lucius did it for the farmers, or he thought he did. I did it so that Marcus would have to come back to Italy, and come back to me.” She looked at the light on her hands. “And he came back to Italy.”

---

Claudia held the needle still for a long time. Then she put the sewing down on the stool and got up, and went to the window, and stood in the light with her back to the bed, looking out at the gulf.

Fulvia watched her. She was tall now. She would be a tall woman. She had her father’s height and his eyes and nothing at all, Fulvia thought, of either of her stepfathers, which was something.

“You never sent the doll,” Claudia said, at the window.

“What?”

“When I came back. From his house. I left the doll there, the bone one. It was supposed to go to the Lares. I told the woman to leave it. And I thought you’d send for it, after, because you’d know. And you didn’t.”

Fulvia lay and looked at her daughter’s back.

“Your father sewed its tunic,” she said.

Claudia turned round.

“The winter you were four. I bought it at the Sigillaria and he laughed at the price, and then he sat up half the night sewing it a tunic out of a scrap of red wool, because you asked him to. Badly. He couldn’t sew. He pricked himself and swore and you laughed at him.” Fulvia moved her hand on the blanket. “I forgot that. For thirteen years. I remembered it the day you came back. I should have sent for it. I thought it was too late.”

Claudia stood at the window. Then she came back and sat down on the stool and picked up the brown tunic for Iullus, and looked at it, and did not sew.

“It’s probably still there,” she said. “Nobody will have known whose job it was.”

---

The light went round. It went off the bed and up the wall and turned gold, and then red, and then the gulf outside went grey and the mountains on the other side went dark, and her woman came in with the lamp, and the keys at her belt, and set the lamp on the stand.

“Shall I close the shutters?” said her woman.

“No. Leave them.”

She could hear the sea. It was very quiet at this time of the evening, just a sound on the stones at the bottom of the terrace, coming and going.

She had told Claudia, and there was nothing left that she had not said, and she was surprised by how light it was. She had always thought it would be heavy, the last of it. She had thought there would be something to hold on to. There was nothing to hold on to, and it was light, like the room in the afternoons.

Claudia had picked up the tunic again. The needle went in and came out.

“Count the silver,” Fulvia said to her woman, from habit, and then heard herself, and almost laughed; and her woman, who had been with her since Clodius and had counted everything for her for twenty years, stood by the lamp with the keys at her belt and said, “I have, mistress. It’s all there,” and did not move to go.
