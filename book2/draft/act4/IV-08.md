# Across the Ditch

POV: Laberius · the lines outside Brundisium · September 40

---

The ditch at Brundisium was Antonius’s ditch, not theirs, and that was the first thing that was wrong with it.

Laberius saw it from the hill on the day they came down out of the Apulian country with the young Caesar’s army. Antonius had landed in the summer and found the town shut against him and had put a wall across the neck of land the town stood on, from the inner harbour to the outer, to keep it shut in; a good wall, well sited, with a ditch in front of it, the kind of work Laberius would have been glad to have had his own name on. Now the young Caesar’s army had come and was digging its own lines facing it across the flat ground outside, to shut in the men who were shutting in the town. So there were two ditches, and two walls, and between them a strip of open ground a few hundred paces wide, and on the other side of the strip, on Antonius’s wall, men in the same helmets.

He looked at it for a long time from the hill, with his pack on his back. Vibius stood beside him.

“Well,” said Vibius. “There’s a thing.”

---

They dug for nine days. It was hot. The ground was sand under a skin of dry grass, and it would not hold a bank at any pitch worth having, and Laberius spent the nine days walking the line telling men to revet with brushwood and being ignored, because nobody believed the bank would ever need to stand.

Nobody believed anything would ever need to stand. That was the thing he noticed, in the nine days. In every camp he had ever been in, before a fight, there was a sound the men made, a tightness in the way they talked and laughed and cleaned their kit, like a rope taking strain. He had heard it at Munda and Mutina and Philippi and at Perusia on the last night of the year. He did not hear it at Brundisium. The men dug and talked and laughed and went down to the shore in the evening to swim, and some of them had begun to go out onto the open ground between the lines in the evenings too, at first only a little way, to the first rise, to look; and then further.

On the third day a man from the Antonian wall came out onto the open ground with his hands empty and walked halfway across and stood there. After a while a man from their own line went out to meet him. They stood in the middle and talked. Laberius watched from the bank. The officers on both sides watched. Nobody stopped them.

By the sixth day there were fifty of them out there every evening, from both walls, standing in knots on the open ground in the long light, talking. Somebody had brought a skin of wine. Somebody on the Antonian side had brought figs.

---

On the eighth day, in the evening, he was walking the bank by the fourth tower checking the brushwood, and somebody on the open ground below shouted, “Cordus!”

He stopped.

He did not turn round at once. He stood on the bank with his hand on a stake and looked at the brushwood in front of him, the dry stems woven through the uprights, and he heard the name again, closer, and he knew the voice. He had known it for twenty years. There was only one man in the world who called him that.

He turned round.

Rufrius was standing in the middle of the open ground with his helmet under his arm. He had got older. His hair was grey all through, and he had a new scar across the bridge of his nose, and he was thin still, with the winter on the hill not yet gone out of his face; but he stood the way he had always stood, with his weight on his back foot and his chin up, as if waiting to be told something he would not like. And when he grinned, which he did now, looking up at Laberius on the bank, there was the gap in his teeth, the front, the left side.

“Mule,” Rufrius said.

Laberius went down off the bank.

---

They stood in the middle of the open ground. Around them in the long light the knots of men from both walls were talking, and laughing, and passing the skin of wine, and nobody looked at them.

“I saw you digging,” Rufrius said. “At Perusia. From the wall. All winter. On the wrong side.”

“Which side was that?”

Rufrius considered. “The one with the bread,” he said.

“You came out with the rest. In February. They gave you bread too.”

“They did.” Rufrius grinned. “I ate it. Then I walked north to Ventidius with forty others, the night after, before anybody wrote our names in the young Caesar’s book.” He jerked his head back at the Antonian wall. “And here’s Ventidius, and here’s me.” He counted on his fingers, slowly, the way he had always counted anything. “Ilerda. The marsh at Forum Gallorum. Perusia. Here. Four times I’ve been the other side of a line from you, Cordus.”

“And once on my left.”

“You would count that.”

He looked down. He looked at Laberius’s feet in the dry grass, at the boots, the way he had looked at them on the causeway at Philippi standing in the water. Laberius waited for him to say it.

“Those are still Gaius’s,” Rufrius said.

“Resoled.”

“Three times?”

“Twice. Since Mutina.” He did not say anything about Thessalonica, or about the night before the Kalends, or the frozen mud, or the straw. “They’ll do.”

Rufrius nodded slowly. He looked at the boots a while longer. Then he looked up.

“You got your land?”

“North of Capua. Fourth line from the Volturnus road.”

“Good land?”

“Beans.” Laberius thought about it. “I can’t tell. Everybody says so. There’s a man on it who knows.”

“A man on it.” Rufrius’s eyebrows went up. “Still? You didn’t put him off?”

“He’s my tenant.”

Rufrius stared at him for a moment. Then he laughed, the old bark, loud enough that men in the knots round them looked across; and he went on laughing, with his hand on Laberius’s shoulder, until he had to wipe his eyes with the back of his wrist.

“Your *tenant*,” he said. “Cordus. Oh, Cordus. Twenty years I stood next to you in a line, and you were the worst man in the army for knowing what was good for you, and you’ve gone and got a tenant.” He shook his head. “Does he pay you?”

“Beans.”

Rufrius laughed again.

---

The officers came out on the ninth evening. Not to stop it. Laberius watched them come, from both walls, a few at a time, tribunes and senior centurions, and stand among the knots of men on the open ground, and listen, and then talk. He saw a tribune from the young Caesar’s side, the one with one ear, from Teanum, who had come down with the army; he saw him in the middle of the open ground with three Antonian centurions round him, and all four of them talking at once, with their hands. He saw the young Caesar’s men and the Antonian men gather round that knot until it was a crowd. He heard the crowd’s voice go up, and he knew that voice too. He had heard it in the colony square at Capua and in the basilica at Teanum, and he had heard it from the wall of Perusia shouting down to the ditch.

It was saying no.

Not to anyone in particular. To the whole thing. To the two walls and the two ditches and the strip of open ground between. To being asked, again, the year after Perusia, to stand in a line facing a line of men who had stood beside them at Philippi, because of something between two men in two tents that none of them had been told about and none of them had agreed to.

“That’s the peace,” said Vibius, beside him. He had come out too. “Right there. That’s it being made.”

“The triumvirs make the peace.”

“The triumvirs sign it.” Vibius nodded at the crowd round the tribune with one ear. “That’s where it’s made.”

Laberius did not argue with him. He did not think Vibius was wrong.

---

Rufrius found him again at dusk, at the edge of the crowd. He had a fig in each hand and gave him one.

“They’re sending envoys tomorrow,” Rufrius said. “Both sides. Pollio for ours. Maecenas for yours. And somebody else, a friend of both, Cocceius. To sit in a room in the town and agree what the army’s already agreed.” He bit his fig. “Then we all go home.”

“Where’s home? For you?”

Rufrius chewed.

“Don’t know,” he said. “Syria, maybe. The Parthians are in Syria, they say. Somebody’ll have to go and get them out.” He looked across at the Antonian wall, at the fires being lit along it. “Or here. If there’s land. If they give us land in Italy, like they gave you. With a farmer on it.” He grinned, with the gap. “I’d have to put him off, mind. I’m not you.”

“No,” said Laberius.

They stood on the open ground between the two walls and ate the figs, and the light went, and along both walls the fires came up, one after another, until there were two lines of them facing each other across the dark with nobody behind either of them who meant to cross.

“Cordus,” Rufrius said.

“What?”

“Nothing.” Rufrius threw the fig stalk away into the dark. “Only it’s good. You. Being on the other side of a ditch from me again, and nothing happening.” He put his helmet back on and did up the strap. “I’d like to see these beans.”

“Come and see them.”

“I might,” said Rufrius, and went back across the open ground toward the Antonian fires; and Laberius stood and watched him go until he could not tell him from the others, and then went back to his own bank, and checked the brushwood at the fourth tower again, though nobody would ever need it to hold.
