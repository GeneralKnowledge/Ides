# The Beans Again

POV: Laberius · the plain north of Capua · April 40

---

The beans were up again. That was the first thing he saw from the road, before the house or the dog or anyone: the field from the boundary stone to the second stake green from end to end, knee high, in flower, white and black, the way they had been the morning he woke under the cart with the boy looking at him.

He stopped on the road and looked at them for a while.

He had worked it out on the way down, walking down from Perusia with the legion, which was going into quarters at Capua, and then on alone with a month’s leave on a tablet in his pack. The beans had been in by the autumn. He had promised Aulius until the beans were in, the morning after the twine, and the beans had been in, and then he had stood at the door with the old man asleep inside and said *until I come back*. Somebody had sown these in the autumn, after he left, and kept them through the winter, and here they were in April, flowering. Aulius had sown them. On Laberius’s land, that the surveyor had given Laberius, with his name burnt into the stakes.

He counted the stakes. He could not help it. They were all there, from the boundary stone to the second stake and along the line to the ditch, except the one he had moved to the hedge gap in the spring. That was there too. Somebody had woven new withies round it.

---

The dog barked when he came up the track. Then it stopped, and came out to the end of its rope, and stood there with its tail going slowly, as if it were trying to remember something.

Aulius came out of the house.

He came out quickly, wiping his hands on his tunic, and stopped in the yard when he saw who it was, and Laberius saw his face do several things one after another, too fast to read. Then Aulius came across the yard with his hand out.

“Centurion,” he said. “You’re back.”

“I’m back.”

Aulius took his hand and held it. His grip was hard and he held on longer than he needed to. Then he turned and shouted at the house, and his wife came to the door, and the children, all three of them, the two small ones and the eldest, the boy who had held the pitchfork in the Marcii’s orchard, who was taller now, nearly as tall as his father, and stood in the door behind his mother with his long arms hanging and stared.

“Come in,” said Aulius. “Come in, come in. You’ll eat. She’ll make something. Have you come from the north? We heard about the north. We heard all about it.” He was talking fast. He did not usually talk fast. “Sit down. There’s wine. Not good wine. Sit down.”

Laberius let himself be brought in.

He sat on the bench in the room where the old man had slept the night he left. Aulius’s wife put bread in front of him, and a bowl of beans cooked with oil and some green thing, and a cup of wine that was, as Aulius had said, not good. The children stood along the wall and watched him eat. Aulius sat across from him and talked: about the winter, which had been wet; about the beans, which he had sown in the month after Laberius left, because the ground was there and it would have been a sin to let it lie; about the colony, which had half emptied in the autumn when the young Caesar’s men came round with their chests, and was filling again now with men coming back from the north.

Laberius ate. He had not eaten like this since the autumn. The beans were very good. He thought that Aulius was glad to see him, and he was surprised by how much he liked that, and he ate more slowly to make it last.

---

The old man was not on the bench.

He noticed it halfway through the beans, and looked at the place by the door where the bench was, and it was empty, and there was a blanket folded on it.

“Your father,” he said.

Aulius stopped talking.

“In the winter,” he said. “At the Saturnalia. He went in his sleep.” He looked at the folded blanket. “He was old. He’d had his time. He was glad to go in his own house.” He said the last part carefully, looking at Laberius, as if he had prepared it.

Laberius put down the spoon.

He thought about the old man on the bench the morning after the twine, in the sun, with his stick, asking *Have you eaten?* He thought about him saying the bread was too heavy and his wife had made it lighter. He thought about *My father’s still alive. You can’t hold him to it*, which Aulius had said at the well, about the dog.

“His own house,” Laberius said.

“His own house.” Aulius was still looking at him. “Because you said. Until you came back. He knew that. I told him, the night you went, and he said, *Then I’ll go before he comes back, and that’s the end of it*, and he laughed.” Aulius smiled a little, not much. “He was like that.”

The room was quiet. One of the small children shifted against the wall.

Laberius understood then, sitting on the bench with the spoon in front of him and the wife at the hearth with her back very straight, what the hand in the yard had been, and the fast talking, and the beans and the bread and the bad wine. It had not been gladness. Or not only. They had been waiting for him all winter. They had sown his field and buried their old man in it, or near it, and kept the stake in the hedge, and all winter every time the dog barked they had thought it was him, coming back to say that the time was up. And when he came up the track, Aulius had come out of the house with his face doing several things at once, and one of them had been fear, and he had put his hand out anyway.

“Where did you bury him?” Laberius said.

---

Aulius took him out to it. It was at the edge of the field, by the road, by the boundary stone, where the family’s dead had always been buried, Aulius said, since his grandfather bought the land from the colony. There was a little mound with a flat stone on it and no letters. The beans grew right up to it.

They stood by it for a while.

“The colony men came,” Aulius said. “In the winter. Twice. With stakes. The tall one, the one you hit, he wasn’t with them; he’d gone north. Others.” He looked down at the stone. “I told them what you said. That the centurion had left his land in the care of his tenant, and the centurion would be home. The first time they laughed. The second time one of them said, *Laberius from the fifth tower? The one who reads the lead?* Because they’d heard. Up there. And they went away.” He was quiet. “I didn’t know what the lead was.”

“Sling bullets,” Laberius said. “With writing on.”

He did not say any more about it. Aulius waited, in case he would, and then did not ask.

They walked back along the line of the stakes, slowly, the way Laberius had walked it the morning after the twine. At the hedge gap he stopped and looked at the stake with the new withies round it.

“The boy did that,” Aulius said. “In the winter. The old ones rotted.”

The boy was standing at the corner of the house, watching them, with his long arms.

---

That night he did not sleep under the cart. Aulius would not hear of it. They gave him the bench where the old man had slept, with the old man’s blanket, and he lay on it in the dark with the family asleep in the back room and listened to the house: the fire settling, the dog shifting outside, somebody breathing.

He had not told them anything about Perusia. Aulius had asked once, at the meal, carefully, what it had been like in the north, and Laberius had said *Cold*, and Aulius had nodded and not asked again. He had not told them about the ditch, or the night before the Kalends, or the man with the gap in his teeth, or the town burning, or the road on the morning of the Ides. He did not think he would tell them. He did not think he would tell anyone.

In his pack, under the bench, at the bottom, wrapped in his spare tunic, there was a bronze god the length of his hand with one foot missing.

He thought about it for a long time in the dark. He thought about the painted shrine by the door of this house, in the corner, a little niche in the wall with a roof like a tiny temple, like the one in the house in Perusia, with Aulius’s family’s gods in it, two of them, old, clay, and a lamp.

In the morning, before anyone was up, he took the bronze god out of the pack and unwrapped it and stood for a while in front of the niche with it in his hand. Then he wrapped it again and put it back in the pack, at the bottom, and went out to see to the water.

When Aulius came out to the well, Laberius was there, with the bucket, and he drew it, and set it on the stone, and stood back from it.

“Have you eaten?” he said.
