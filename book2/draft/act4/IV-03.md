# A New Wife

POV: Nysa · Rome, the young Caesar’s house · April 40

---

They took out Claudia’s room first, though Claudia had been gone a year, because nobody had told anyone to take it out until now.

Glycon came back from the north at the end of March, thinner, with a cough, and walked through the house on his first morning with a tablet, and stopped at every door. At Claudia’s door he stood for some time. It was as she had left it: the bed with the blue coverlet, the chest, the stool, the little shelf by the window, and on the shelf the bone doll in its red tunic, where Claudia had told her to leave it the morning she packed the bundle. Nobody had taken it to the Lares. Nobody had known whose job it was.

“Out,” Glycon said. “All of it. The bed can go to the wool room. The coverlet to the fuller’s. The chest to the storeroom.” He wrote. Then he looked at the shelf. “And that.”

“It goes to the Lares,” said Nysa. “She said so.”

“Then take it to them.” He wrote again. “Then the room is to be washed, floor and walls, and the walls painted. Not red. Something a woman would like.”

“What would a woman like?”

He looked at her over the tablet.

“Ask her,” he said, “when she comes.”

---

Nysa did not know who *she* was for three days, and then she knew from the cook, who knew everything first, because everything came through the kitchen door before it came through the front.

Her name was Scribonia. She was older than the young Caesar, ten years older, the cook said, perhaps more. She had been married twice already. She had children by the second husband, grown or nearly. She was the sister of a man named Libo, and Libo was the father-in-law of Sextus Pompeius, who had the sea.

“So there it is,” said the cook, chopping. “He marries the sister of the father-in-law of the man who’s starving us. And the man who’s starving us stops starving us. That’s a wedding. That’s what a wedding is, up there.” He jerked his knife at the ceiling, meaning the senators, meaning everyone who was not in a kitchen. “Maecenas arranged it. Maecenas arranges everything. He came in the back way last week with a man from Libo’s house and they were in the small room all afternoon, and I sent in figs, and they didn’t eat one.”

Nysa sat on the stool by the bread table and thought about the room.

It would need painting. Glycon had said not red. The walls in there were a dark old red that had been the orator’s, and it would take three coats at least to cover. She thought green, perhaps, or the pale yellow that had been fashionable in the Carinae the year before Mutina. She thought about the coverlet. A woman who had been married twice and had grown children would bring her own coverlet, and her own chest, and her own women, and her own ways of doing things, and the house would have to make room for all of it. The wool room would have to make room for her women. The kitchen would have to learn what she ate.

That was what a wedding was, to Nysa. The rest of it was the cook’s business, and the senators’.

---

There were other departures that month.

She counted them at the door, because she was at the door again, now that the women had stopped coming with their letters for the camp and the people with their deeds had begun, slowly, to come back. She counted who came up the Ring-makers’ Stairs and who went down, and she wrote it on the tablet the way Glycon liked, and she heard the talk on the steps while she did it.

A man in a good cloak who came to see Glycon said that Tiberius Nero had gone. Nero had been with Lucius at Perusia and had got out somehow, and had gone south, and now he had gone from Italy altogether, to Sextus in Sicily, with his young wife and his little son, by night, from Naples, and the boy had cried on the shore and nearly given them away. The man in the good cloak thought this was very funny.

A woman selling eggs at the bottom of the steps said that the old Julia had gone too. Antonius’s mother. She had gone to Sextus as well. Everybody was going to Sextus. Sextus had the sea and the sea was the only place left in the world where the young Caesar was not.

Nysa wrote down the egg-woman: *A woman, eggs, did not come up.* She did not write down Julia.

---

She had known about Julia for five days before the egg-woman.

The steward of the Carinae had come to the door. Antonius’s steward, the one who had sat in the Carinae kitchen and looked at her as if she were a jar he was trying to remember the contents of; the one she had given the true count to at the Capena gate the winter before last, when the young Caesar came home sick, and who had turned the tablet face down. He had come up the Ring-makers’ Stairs at the end of a long day, alone, in a plain cloak, and had not asked for Glycon. He had asked for her.

She had gone out to him on the steps.

He had not wasted words. He never had. He had said that the master’s mother was leaving Rome. He had said she was leaving in the morning, for the coast, and from the coast for Sicily, and that nobody in the young Caesar’s house knew yet. He had said that she would be on the Appian Way by first light and at the sea in three days.

“Why are you telling me?” Nysa had said.

“Because they’ll find out in a week,” the steward had said. “Somebody will tell them. Somebody always does. I’d sooner it was somebody who could count to three before she did.”

She had looked at him. He had looked back, with no expression at all, a man who had been Antonius’s steward for twenty years and had watched every kind of person come and go through the Carinae gate and had turned a tablet face down once for her.

“Three days,” he had said, and gone back down the stairs.

She had counted them. She had sat at the door for three days and counted the people who came up the steps and the people who went down, and she had said nothing to Glycon and nothing to anyone, and on the fourth morning she had gone to Glycon in the small room and said that she had heard on the steps that the old Julia, Antonius’s mother, had left Rome.

Glycon had written it down. He had asked her where she had heard it and she had said on the steps, which was true. He had sent a boy to the Carinae and the boy had come back and said it was true, the old lady had gone, three days since, nobody knew where. Glycon had written that down too.

Then, the next day, the egg-woman.

---

The painters came on the Ides. Nysa had them paint Claudia’s room a pale yellow, in the end, three coats over the orator’s red, and it took four days, and the room smelled of lime for a week afterwards. She put a new coverlet on the new bed, a plain one, cream, from the wool room. She did not put anything on the shelf by the window.

Glycon came and looked at it on the last day.

“Yellow,” he said.

“They had yellow in the Carinae,” said Nysa. “The year before Mutina. All the women liked it.”

Glycon looked at the walls for a while. Then he looked at her.

“Did they,” he said, not quite a question; and she understood, from the way he said it, that he had been told something about the old Julia, by someone, from somewhere, and had decided not to ask her about it, and that this was what he was not asking.

“It’ll fade,” she said. “Yellow does. In a year she can have it whatever she likes.”

“In a year,” said Glycon, “we’ll see.” He wrote something on his tablet. Then he went out, coughing, and she stood in the yellow room and smelled the lime, and found that she was counting the boards in the floor.
