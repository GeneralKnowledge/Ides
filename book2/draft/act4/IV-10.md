# The Count

POV: Nysa · Rome · autumn 40

---

The veil was flame-coloured and it was the wool room’s, and that was the young Caesar’s doing.

He had written from Brundisium. Glycon read the letter in the atrium to the household, standing, with his cough, and it said that the triumvir’s sister would be married to Marcus Antonius in Rome before the Kalends of November, by leave of the Senate, which had been asked to set aside the months of her mourning and had agreed; and that the bride’s veil was to be made in the young Caesar’s house, by his household, as his own clothes were. That was all it said about the veil. It said a great deal more about the arrangements, which Glycon did not read aloud.

The old freedwoman with the bad eyes wept. Nobody had asked her to weave anything for a bride in thirty years. She had done the last one, she said, for a girl of the Claudii in the year of Catiline, and she sat at the loom for nine days with her nose nearly on the warp and would let nobody else touch it. Nysa did the hem. She did it by the lamp in the evenings, one lamp, the household’s one, in very small stitches, a thread the colour of a fire just catching, and when she had done it she folded the veil in a cloth and Glycon took it to the house of the triumvir’s sister himself, carrying it in both arms the way he had carried the deeds in from the door, the year before last, like firewood.

---

She heard about Fulvia from the cook.

The news had come with the fleet, or ahead of it, by a merchant ship from Corinth. The wife of Marcus Antonius was dead at Sicyon. She had been ill since the spring, it was said, and the triumvir had left her there when he sailed for Italy, and she had died in the summer, with her children and her daughter by her, in a house above the gulf.

“There’s your wedding,” said the cook. He was not chopping anything. He sat on his stool with his hands on his knees. “There’s why there can be a wedding. You can’t marry a man to the young Caesar’s sister while he’s got Fulvia.” He was quiet. “She used to come down to the kitchen at the Carinae. You remember? At the Saturnalia. With the children. To give out the presents herself. She gave me a knife once.”

“I remember,” Nysa said.

She did remember. She remembered Fulvia in the Carinae kitchen at the Saturnalia, quick and short with everyone, handing out the presents from a basket as if she were paying wages, and the children behind her, and the steward behind the children with the tablet, writing down who had had what.

She remembered a girl of fifteen with her hair done high in a style from the Carinae, standing on the top step of the young Caesar’s house looking down at the people sitting along the wall.

She went to the household shrine that night, in the corner of the atrium, where she had put the bone doll in its red tunic in the spring, beside the household’s own gods, because Claudia had said that was where it went. It was there. She stood in front of the shrine for some time and then went to bed.

---

Salvidienus came to the house nine days before the wedding.

He had been sent for from Gaul. Nysa had seen the letter go, alone in the bag, under the young Caesar’s own seal, which was not how the bag usually went. He came up the Ring-makers’ Stairs at the second hour with a dozen men behind him, and he was bigger than she had thought from hearing about him, and louder, and on the top step he laughed at something one of his men said so that the street below turned round. The porter opened to him without asking his name. Nysa wrote him down. *A general, twelve with him, in at the second hour.*

The young Caesar was in the small room with Maecenas. Salvidienus went in to them and the door was shut.

He did not come out by the front. She sat at the door until the eighth hour and wrote down everyone who came and went, and he was not one of them. His twelve sat on the steps until a centurion came up from the Forum and spoke to them, and then they went down the Stairs together without looking back.

Two days later the Senate declared him an enemy of the Roman people, and a few days after that he was dead. The porter said he had opened his veins. The litter-men said the Senate’s men had saved him the trouble. They argued about it in the kitchen yard for most of an evening. The porter also said, and nobody argued with this, that it was Antonius who had given him up: that Salvidienus had written to Antonius in the summer, when the armies were at Brundisium, offering to bring his legions over, and that Antonius had told the young Caesar so at dinner, after the peace, the way you pass a man the salt.

---

On the wedding day the young Caesar’s household went to the Carinae.

They did not go to the wedding. The wedding was at the triumvir’s sister’s house, the feast and the taking of the auspices and the joining of hands, and the household of the young Caesar had no place in it. They went to see the procession. The bride would be taken at dusk from her house to the house of her husband, and her husband’s house was on the Carinae, the house that had been Pompey’s, that Antonius had bought at auction and lived in and filled with his cooks and his actors and his best silver and his wife, and that Nysa had lived in for three years.

So she stood on the Carinae in the dusk, at the corner of the street below the house, with the women from the wool room and the cook and the porter and Glycon, and counted.

She had not been asked to. Nobody had told her to count. Glycon had not said a word about it. But there was a crowd, and she was in it, and she counted it, as she had counted every crowd she had ever been in since she was a girl at the Domus, in rows, then tens, in Greek, because it was quicker.

The street was full from wall to wall. The roofs had people on them. There were boys up the lamp-posts and women at every window with lamps, and men from the colonies who had come to Rome for the wedding because they had heard there would be wine, and freedmen and shopkeepers and slaves from every house on the Carinae, and soldiers, a great many soldiers, from both armies, from Brundisium, standing together in the street in knots, as if there had never been anything between them. She counted them. She counted the windows and the roofs. She counted the boys on the lamp-posts, and stopped at forty, and went on.

The torches came round the corner at the bottom of the street. She heard them before she saw them: the flutes, and the boys singing the old wedding songs with the filthy verses, and the crowd taking up the cry, *Talasio*, the way crowds had taken it up at weddings since before anyone could remember why. Then the light. Then the torches, five of them, carried by boys. Then the bride.

She was walking between two boys in bordered togas who held her hands. Her face was hidden by the veil. Nysa could see the veil very clearly in the torchlight, the colour of a fire just catching, and the hem of it, which she had done, in very small stitches, by one lamp; and she could see the bride’s hands in the boys’ hands, and the way she walked, steadily, neither fast nor slow, through the shouting, with her head up under the veil.

One of the boys holding her hand was Antyllus. Nysa knew him at once, though he was bigger than when she had last seen him, in the Carinae kitchen, at the Saturnalia, holding his mother’s skirt. He was holding his new stepmother’s hand and walking very upright with his face very solemn, as if he had been told how to walk and had practised.

And behind them, coming up the street at the head of his friends, with his arms open to the crowd and the torchlight on him, laughing, enormous, in a Roman toga with a wreath on his head: Antonius.

The crowd’s noise doubled. Nysa felt it go through the street like a wind. She went on counting.

---

The torches went up to the door of the house. There was the business with the doorposts, the wool and the oil, and the lifting of the bride over the threshold, which she could not see from the corner but could hear from the noise, and then the door shutting, and then the crowd, which had been a single thing with its face toward the house, beginning to come apart into people again, and turn, and talk, and go.

Glycon was beside her.

He did not ask. She saw him not ask. He looked at her, and at the crowd going away down the street, and then at the house with its door shut and the torches guttering on the steps; and she knew that if he asked she would tell him, and that he would write it down, and that it would go to Brundisium in the next bag, or wherever the young Caesar was, and be a number in a column on a tablet in a small careful hand. She waited for him to ask.

He did not. He coughed, behind his hand, and said that it was late and the women should go home before the streets got rough, and went to see to it.

She stood at the corner.

She had the number. She had it the way she had every number she had ever counted, entire, in Greek, the rows and the tens and the ones left over, and she found that she did not know who it was for. The young Caesar had not asked for it. Glycon had not asked for it. The steward of the Carinae was somewhere inside the house behind that shut door, seeing to the wedding feast, and would not come out tonight to ask her anything. Fulvia was dead at Sicyon. Fulvia’s woman, who had once asked her for the true number at the games, was somewhere across the sea with the keys.

It was over, she thought. That was what a wedding was. The war was over and the soldiers had stood in the street together and the young Caesar and Antonius were brothers now, and there was nothing left to count for, on either side, because there were no sides. She stood with the number in her head and thought that it was the first count she had ever made that belonged to nobody but her.

At her feet, on the bottom step of the house at the corner, a boy was sitting. He was perhaps six, a shopkeeper’s boy, with a torch-end in his hand that he had picked up out of the gutter and was blowing on to keep it alight. He had been there the whole time. He looked up at her.

“How many?” he said.

She looked down at him.

“People,” he said. “You were counting. I saw your mouth. How many?”

Nysa told him.

He thought about it, frowning, the torch-end forgotten in his hand. It was too big a number for him; she could see him try to hold it and fail.

“Is that a lot?” he said.

“Yes,” said Nysa. “That’s a lot.”
