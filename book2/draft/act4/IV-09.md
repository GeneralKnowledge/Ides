# Spain

POV: Lucius Antonius · Corduba, Further Spain · autumn 40

---

The news of the peace came up the Baetis on a grain barge, three weeks old, and his brother’s letter came with it, and the letter was about horses.

Lucius read it in the courtyard of the governor’s house, where he read everything, because it had a fountain and a lemon tree and was the only place in Corduba where the heat let go of you in the afternoons. He read it standing up. Then he sat down on the edge of the fountain and read it again.

Marcus wrote that he was well. He wrote that the peace had been made at Brundisium, that Pollio and Maecenas had made it, with Cocceius running between them, and that the soldiers had made it first, standing about between the lines eating figs and refusing to fight, which Lucius would have enjoyed seeing. He wrote that he was to marry the young Caesar’s sister, Octavia, in Rome before the winter, and that she was a fine woman, and that the Senate was waiving the months. He wrote that he had the East and the young Caesar had the West, and that Lepidus had Africa, which was more than Lepidus deserved.

Then he wrote, for most of a page, about a horse he had bought in Cappadocia, a grey, with a mouth like silk, that would make Lucius forget the grey he had eaten at Perusia.

That was the only time the word was in the letter. It was in the sentence about the horse.

At the end Marcus wrote: *Govern well. Don’t write me long letters. Your brother.*

---

He had been in Spain since the summer.

They had sent him from Perusia by stages, as a guest, with a guest’s good cloak and an escort of four quiet men who were always somewhere near, down through Italy to Puteoli without passing through Rome, and from Puteoli by sea. The young Caesar had told him himself, in the camp below the burnt town, a few days after the Ides, in a small tent with a brazier and a cup of warm water at his elbow and Maecenas by the tent-pole examining his rings. The province of Further Spain needed a governor. It was a great province, rich, with the legions settled and quiet. An honourable post, the kind of post given to a consular of good family. Everything he needed would be sent after him. His staff was already chosen.

“My brother agrees?” Lucius had said.

“Your brother will,” the young Caesar had said.

He had been right about that, as it turned out. He was right about most things, the young Caesar, in a way that Lucius had come to find more tiring than anything else about him.

---

He had a good house in Corduba. He had a staff that had been chosen for him, four of whom slept in the rooms nearest his and went where he went. He had two legions in the province, settled and quiet, whose officers were very correct with him and reported, he assumed, to someone else. He had the assizes to hold, and the roads, and the tax-farmers, and a quarrel between two towns on the Singilis about a bridge.

And he had land to measure. That had surprised him. There were veterans in the province from Caesar’s wars, settled at Hispalis and Urso and here and there in the valley, and their boundaries had been badly done, twenty years ago, by surveyors in a hurry; and every week someone came to the governor’s house with a quarrel about a stake. He had sent for a chain and a groma in his second month. He had walked the fields outside Urso in the white heat with the chain-man calling the numbers back to him, the way he had walked them in Campania in the year Caesar died, and he had found that he still could, and that nobody in Spain had ever heard of the land board of seven or of Cicero’s jokes about it.

Nobody in Spain had heard of anything. That was the thing about it.

---

He sat on the edge of the fountain with Marcus’s letter on his knee.

He had read every letter from Italy since the summer for the word. The officers’ letters, the tax-farmers’ letters, the letters from friends in Rome who were still writing to him, fewer every month. Not one of them had said *Perusia*. Not one had said *the siege*, or *the winter*, or *the town*. Not one had said *Fulvia*, even when the news came, in the summer, that she was dead at Sicyon; his friends had written that his brother’s wife had died, and that it was very sad, and nothing else. They wrote about the peace, and the weather, and the Parthians in Syria, and the wedding.

He had taken it for a kindness. He still did, mostly. They knew what he had done and what it had cost, and whose fault the starving had been, and the trench along the north wall, and the town burning, and they were not saying so. It was the tact of men who had all made mistakes in the last ten years and had agreed not to bring them up at table.

And now Marcus. A page about a grey horse, and *Govern well*.

He waited, sitting there, to feel the insult of it. A consul of the Roman people, who had stood on the steps of Saturn and on the Rostra with the whole Forum calling his name, who had held a town against an army for a winter; and he had been sent to the far end of the world with four quiet men, and his brother wrote him about horses and told him not to write long letters.

What he felt instead, sitting on the edge of the fountain in the empty courtyard, was something he did not say to himself in words and would not have said to anyone: a loosening, very deep down, like a knot that has been pulled tight for a year being picked open. There would be no grain to count. Nobody in Spain would know what the trench along the north wall had been for. Nobody would call his name in a Forum and wait for him to tell them what they wanted to hear. Marcus would be in the East with his new wife, and the young Caesar would be in Rome, and he would be here, at the edge of the map, measuring land.

He folded the letter and put it inside his tunic.

One of the four quiet men had come to the door of the courtyard and was standing there, not looking at him, the way they did. Beyond him, in the passage, there were voices: the deputation from the towns on the Singilis, come about the bridge.

Lucius put his hand in the fountain. The water was cold. He left it there a moment longer, and then dried it on his toga and got up to hear about the bridge.
