# Spain

POV: Lucius Antonius · Brundisium · autumn 40

---

His brother embraced him in front of everybody, and then talked to him about horses.

It was in the courtyard of the house in Brundisium where the peace had been signed, the morning after, with the whole of both staffs standing about in the sun pretending not to watch. Lucius had come in from the young Caesar’s side, where he had been since February, a guest, with a guest’s good cloak and a guest’s escort of four quiet men who were always somewhere near. He had not seen Marcus for three years. He had stood in the gateway of the courtyard and looked across it and seen him, by the fountain, talking to Pollio, enormous and brown, with grey in his beard and a Roman cloak on, and he had stopped in the gateway because his legs had stopped.

Then Marcus had looked up and seen him, and come across the courtyard in four strides, and put his arms round him and lifted him off the ground the way he had when Lucius was twelve.

“Look at you,” Marcus said, putting him down. “You’re thin. They starved you.” He held him at arm’s length and looked at him. “Gods, you’re thin. Did you eat the horses? I heard you ate the horses.”

“Some of them.”

“Which ones? Not the grey? Tell me it wasn’t the grey.”

“It was the grey,” Lucius said.

Marcus put his hand over his eyes. Then he laughed, the laugh that went across a courtyard and made everyone in it laugh too without knowing why, and put his arm round Lucius’s shoulders and walked him over to the fountain, telling him about a horse he had bought in Cappadocia that would make him forget the grey, a horse with a mouth like silk, and Pollio stepped back to make room for them, smiling, and the staffs of both triumvirs went on pretending not to watch.

---

Nobody said *Perusia*.

He noticed it at the fountain and he went on noticing it all morning. He was introduced to people; he was greeted by people he knew; he was brought wine and sat on a bench in the shade beside his brother, and men came up to pay their respects to both of them, Antonian officers and the young Caesar’s, and every one of them said something warm, and not one of them said *Perusia*. Not one of them said *the siege*, or *the winter*, or *the town*. Not one said *Fulvia*. They talked about the peace, and the weather, and the crossing from Corcyra, and the Parthians in Syria, and Marcus’s horse with the silk mouth, and the wedding.

The wedding was the thing. Everyone was talking about the wedding. The young Caesar’s sister, Octavia, widowed in the spring; and the Senate would have to waive the months for her, and would, of course it would; and she was beautiful and good and everyone liked her, and Marcus would marry her in Rome before the winter, and then the two triumvirs would be brothers, and that would be the end of it.

Lucius sat on the bench in the shade and listened to it go round, and thought that it was a kindness.

That was what it was. They were being kind to him. They knew what he had done and what it had cost, and they knew whose fault the starving had been, and the trench along the north wall, and the town burning, and they were not saying so. They were letting him sit here in the sun next to his brother as if he had come in from a long journey. As if Perusia were a place nobody had been. He felt it as a warmth, a kind of tact, the tact of men who had all made mistakes in the last ten years and had agreed not to bring them up at table.

He held on to that. It was a comfortable thing to hold.

---

The young Caesar sent for him in the afternoon.

He was in a small room at the back of the house with Maecenas and a secretary, very pale, with a cup of warm water at his elbow; he had been ill again on the march, Lucius had heard, and had not entirely got over it. He was courteous. He was always courteous. He asked Lucius to sit and asked how he was and whether his rooms were comfortable, and then, without any change in his voice, he told him about Spain.

The province of Further Spain needed a governor. It was a great province, rich, with the legions there settled and quiet; an honourable post, the kind of post given to a consular of good family. The young Caesar and his colleague had agreed that Lucius Antonius should have it. He would leave before the winter, from Puteoli, with a suitable staff, which was already chosen. He would not need to return to Rome first. Everything he needed would be sent after him.

Lucius sat on the stool.

He had been a consul of the Roman people. He had stood on the steps of Saturn and on the Rostra with the whole Forum calling his name. He had held a town against an army for a winter. And he was being sent to Spain with a staff that was already chosen, from Puteoli, without going back to Rome, and the young Caesar was telling him so as kindly as a man tells a child that it is bedtime.

“My brother agrees?” he said.

“Your brother proposed it,” said the young Caesar.

Lucius looked at him. The young Caesar looked back with his pale, patient, unreadable face,, and Maecenas, by the window, examined his rings.

“Then I’m grateful,” Lucius said, “to you both.”

---

He went out into the courtyard. Marcus was not there; he had gone into the town with Pollio. The staffs had gone. There was nobody by the fountain but a slave sweeping the leaves.

He sat on the edge of the fountain where Marcus had sat.

Spain. He turned it over. He could see it from here, already: the long voyage, the province, the governor’s house at Corduba or wherever it was, the legions that were settled and quiet, the staff that had been chosen for him, four quiet men who would always be somewhere near. A great province. Rich. Far. He would govern it well. He was good at figures and he was good with crowds and he had been a surveyor once, with the chain and the groma, and there would be land to measure in Spain.

He waited to feel the insult of it.

What he felt instead, sitting on the edge of the fountain in the empty courtyard with the slave sweeping, was something he did not say to himself in words and would not have said to anyone: a loosening, very deep down, like a knot that has been pulled tight for a year being picked open. There would be no grain to count. Nobody in Spain would know what the trench along the north wall had been for. Nobody would call his name in a Forum and wait for him to tell them what they wanted to hear. Marcus would be in the East, and the young Caesar would be in Rome, and he would be in Spain, measuring land, at the far end of the world from both of them.

He sat there for a long time.

The slave finished sweeping and went away. The water ran in the fountain. Lucius put his hand in it, and it was cold, and he left it there.
