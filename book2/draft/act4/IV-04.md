# White Shoes

POV: Fulvia · Athens · May 40

---

He was wearing Greek shoes. White ones, the soft kind with no hobnails that the young men of Athens wore to walk to the gymnasium, and she looked at them before she looked at anything else, because they were the first thing she saw when she raised her eyes from the floor of the courtyard, and because she had been married to him for six years and had never once seen him in anything on his feet that a man could not march in.

She had been waiting in the courtyard of the house below the Acropolis for most of the morning. It was a good house, borrowed from someone, with a fountain in the middle and a fig tree and a colonnade round three sides, and she had been given a chair in the colonnade and a cup of something cold and told that the triumvir was at the Odeion and would come when he came. She had sat. Antyllus had sat beside her in his bordered toga, very upright, for an hour, and then had been allowed to go and look at the fish in the fountain. Iullus was with the nurse inside. Claudia had stayed at the ship.

She had heard him come in. Everyone in the house had heard him; he came into a house the way weather came into it. Voices in the street, and the door, and his voice above the others, laughing at something, and then the voices going quiet one by one as someone told him who was in the courtyard.

Then the white shoes on the stone in front of her chair.

---

He had changed. She saw that when she looked up from the shoes. He was browner, and heavier, though he had always been heavy, and there was grey in his beard that had not been there at Mutina; and he was wearing, over a fine linen tunic, a Greek cloak, a long one, square, pinned at the shoulder in the Greek way, of a blue so deep it was nearly black, with a border she did not recognise. He looked like a Greek. He looked like a rich Greek of good family, a magistrate of some city in Asia, the kind of man who had been coming to the Carinae for years to ask Marcus for things.

He looked at her for a long moment without saying anything. She saw him see that she was thinner. She saw him see the boy at the fountain, who had turned round and was standing quite still with his hands at his sides, the way he had been told to stand when his father came, and had been practising for three months.

Then Marcus said, “Well. Here you are.”

---

He did not shout. That was what she would remember, afterwards. She had heard him shout at a hundred men, at soldiers and at senators and at his brother and at her, and she had been ready for it on the ship and on the road from Piraeus and all the morning in the colonnade. She had her answers ready for the shouting. He did not shout.

He sat down on the edge of the fountain, in his blue cloak, with his elbows on his knees, the way he sat in the Carinae at the end of a long day, and he talked to her quietly, looking at the water, and he told her what she had done.

She had made a war in his name. She and Lucius and Manius. Without his orders, without his knowledge, without one word from him. She had raised soldiers in Italy against his colleague, with whom he had sworn a compact at Bononia and again at Philippi. She had given out watchwords. He had heard about the watchwords. He had heard about the sword. Everyone had heard about the sword. They had been laughing about the sword in Alexandria, at dinner, at a table he had been sitting at, and he had had to sit there and laugh too.

She had lost him Italy. That was the sum of it. She had lost him the veterans, who had all gone over to the young Caesar at Perusia with bread in their hands. She had lost him Pollio’s legions and Plancus’s legions and Ventidius’s legions, all standing about in the north without orders, which the young Caesar would pick up one by one at his leisure. She had lost him Lucius, who was the young Caesar’s guest. She had lost him his mother, who had had to run to Sextus Pompeius by sea. And she had given the young Caesar the one thing he had never had and could not have got for himself, which was a war he could win.

“And Manius,” he said. “Manius. Who told you he had my authority? Did he show you a letter? He has no letter. I never wrote him one.”

“He said you would want the colonies slowed.”

“He said.” Marcus looked at the water. “He said. Well. I shall talk to Manius.”

She knew what that meant. She had been married to him for six years. She found she did not care very much.

---

She did not give him her answers. She had had them ready for three months and she sat in the colonnade with her hands in her lap and found that she did not want to give them.

She could have said that he had not written. That she had written to him every month, by every ship, and that he had answered two letters, one about the price of silver at Ephesus and one about a fleet. That she had held Italy for him the only way there was to hold it, with soldiers, because the young Caesar was taking it away field by field with surveyors while he sat in Alexandria at a table laughing at a joke about her sword. That she had asked him, in every letter, what he wanted, and that he had never once told her.

She could have said: *Tarsus. The barge. The queen.*

She did not say any of it. She sat and looked at the white shoes and let him talk, and when he had finished talking he sat for a while on the edge of the fountain with his elbows on his knees and looked at the fish.

Then he looked up, and across the courtyard, at the boy.

Antyllus was still standing by the fountain where he had been told to stand. He had not moved. His face was white and very solemn and his hands were at his sides, and he was looking at his father with an expression that Fulvia had seen on the faces of soldiers on parade, waiting to be noticed by the general.

Marcus looked at him for a moment. Then he held out his arm, without saying anything, and the boy came across the stone at a run and was gathered up against the blue cloak, and Marcus put his face down in the boy’s hair and kept it there.

She watched them.

It would pass. She was nearly sure of it. She had seen him angry before, at her and at others, and it had always passed; it was weather, it came in and it went out. He would be angry for a week, or a month, and talk to Manius, and then one evening he would come to her room with a cup in each hand and sit on the end of her bed and talk about something else, and it would be over. That was how it had been. He would take them all to Alexandria, or back to Italy in the spring with his fleet, and there would be a settlement with the young Caesar, there was always a settlement, and she would be the triumvir’s wife again.

She sat in the colonnade and believed that, for the length of time it took him to put the boy down.

Then he stood up, and straightened the blue cloak on his shoulder, and said to the steward of the house, not to her, that the lady would be given rooms on the garden side, where it was quiet, and that he himself would be at the Odeion until late, and should not be waited for.

He went out. She heard him in the street, his voice going away, laughing at something again.

She stayed in the chair. There was the pressing in her chest that came now when she had been sitting still too long and then thought about standing up, the flat hand on the breastbone, and she waited for it to go before she tried. Antyllus came back from the fountain and stood beside her chair with his hands at his sides, and after a while she put her hand on his head.

“Did he like me?” the boy said.

“Yes.”

“He didn’t say.”

“No,” said Fulvia. “He doesn’t.” She took her hand away. “Go and find your brother. Tell the nurse we’re to have the rooms on the garden side.”
