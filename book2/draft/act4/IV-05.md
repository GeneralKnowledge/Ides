# Sphinx

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Janus · June 40

---

Calenus died in Gaul in the spring, of nothing anyone could name, in his tent beside the Rhodanus with his legions round him, and his son handed all eleven of them to the young Caesar within the month, and Hordeonius heard both pieces of news on the same morning and thought first, before anything else, of the plate.

He was ashamed of that. He was ashamed of it for most of the morning. Then he got the private tablet out of the strongbox, the one with the cipher, and opened it at the place.

It was three years old, the entry. The month of the lists. He had written it himself in the cipher only he could read: the date, the sum, *Calenus’s steward: lent*, three thousand denarii in worn silver, secured on the consular’s plate, every piece listed by weight on a second tablet. And after it, small, one more word.

He looked at the word for a while. He had not looked at it in a long time.

Three thousand, and not a coin of it paid back. Not the principal and not the interest. He had not asked for it. He had not sent Sosia. Every quarter-day for three years he had opened the day-book and seen the line for Calenus’s household, owing, and closed the book again, and had told himself that a consular with half the Falernian country in his name was good for it whenever he chose to be, and that there was no hurry. That had been true. It had also not been the reason. He knew what the money had been for, the month it was lent. He had known at the board. He had written *clean* after it because of what it was for, and a man does not send his collector after a loan like that. He lets it sit.

And now Calenus was dead in Gaul, and the son had given the legions to the young Caesar, and the estate would go wherever estates went when the man who owned them died on the wrong side of a civil war that had just changed sides under him.

---

The steward did not come. That was the first surprise. Hordeonius had half expected him, the grey careful freedman with the soft hands, coming the long way round past the board with the face of a man doing sums he did not want seen. Nobody came for ten days.

On the eleventh day a different man came.

He was short and Greek, with a careful beard and ink in the creases of his knuckles, and a cough that he kept behind his hand. He wore a plain good tunic of household wool, nothing fine, and he came straight down the arcade from the Forum end without looking at the other stalls, as if he had been told exactly which one and had no interest in any of the rest. He stopped at the board and took out a tablet.

“Lucius Hordeonius Felix,” he said. It was not a question.

“I am.”

“You hold a loan against the household of Quintus Fufius Calenus, consular, deceased. Three thousand denarii, lent three years ago, in the month the lists went up.” He did not stumble over that. He had the date pat, as if he had been reading it off something all the way from wherever he had come from. “Secured on plate.”

Hordeonius did not answer at once.

“I represent,” said the Greek, “the household’s present affairs.” He coughed behind his hand. “The young Fufius has gone into the young Caesar’s service with his father’s army, as you will have heard. His father’s estate has debts. The young Caesar has undertaken that the debts of men who have come over to him will be honoured in full, so that nobody can say otherwise. I have been asked to settle them.” He laid the tablet on the board. “This one is yours. Principal, and interest at your usual rate, for three years. I have the sum.”

Hordeonius looked at the tablet.

It was sealed. The seal was new to him. It was not Calenus’s, which he knew, a head of Mercury, worn; and it was not the son’s, which he did not know but which would surely be something with Fufius on it. It was a sphinx. A small crouching sphinx with a woman’s face, very finely cut, pressed deep into green wax.

He had seen it once before. It had been on a letter that came through the Janus in the winter, from the camp at Perusia to some dealer in the commissariat, that the dealer had shown round the arcade because he was proud of it. *The young Caesar’s own ring*, the dealer had said. *His father had it. He seals everything with it.*

---

He broke the seal. He read the tablet. It said what the Greek had said, in a good secretary’s hand: the sum, the interest, *at the creditor’s usual rate*, and an undertaking that the household of Calenus was now under the protection of Caesar, son of the god, and its obligations would be met.

Principal and interest. Three years. At his usual rate.

He sat with the tablet in his hand and felt the thing that every lender feels, which is the pleasure of being paid, and under it, rising, something larger and lighter that he did not have a word for at first and then did. Luck. It was luck. He had lent three thousand to a frightened consular’s steward in the worst month of the proscriptions, for a reason he had chosen not to ask about, and had never been repaid, and had stopped expecting to be, and had written *clean* after it in a cipher for nobody’s eyes but his own. And now, three years later, the young Caesar, who had won everything, was paying it, with interest, under his own seal. He had been right. For once in his life he had made a loan for a reason that was not money and it had come back as money anyway.

He did not say any of that.

“Your usual rate,” said the Greek, watching him. “One by the month, they tell me at the Basilica. I have counted it at one.”

“One,” Hordeonius agreed.

The Greek nodded and put a bag on the board. It was a heavy bag. Hordeonius did not open it. He did not need to; he could tell from the way it sat. He reached under the board for the second tablet, the one with Calenus’s plate listed on it by weight, every piece, the security, and pushed it across.

The Greek looked at it without picking it up.

“The plate,” Hordeonius said. “It was security. It is released.”

“Ah.” The Greek turned the tablet round with one inky finger and read it, the weights and the descriptions, the salvers and the bowls and the great wine-cooler with the hunting scene. He read the whole of it, slowly, as if it interested him. Then he looked up.

“You never sent for it,” he said. “In three years. A consular’s plate, and the loan in default from the first quarter-day, and you never once sent for the plate.” He coughed. “They say at the Basilica you are a careful man.”

“I am a careful man.”

“Yes.” The Greek put the tablet of the plate under his arm. “I’ll note that. That the creditor was careful.”

He went away up the arcade the way he had come, without looking at the other stalls, coughing behind his hand.

---

Sosia came at the ninth hour and found the bag in the strongbox and the line for Calenus’s household closed in the day-book, *paid in full, with interest, by the hand of the young Caesar’s freedman*, and whistled through his teeth.

“Calenus,” he said. “After three years. Who’d have thought it.”

“Not I.”

“Paid by the winner.” Sosia shook his head. “You always said, back the winner. Bucco’s been backing the loser for a year and look at him. And here’s Calenus, who backed the loser, dead, and his debts paid by the winner.” He looked at Hordeonius with something like admiration. “That’s luck, that is.”

“That is luck,” said Hordeonius.

He had the private tablet in the strongbox, under the bag, with the cipher entry in it and the word after it. He did not take it out. He had thought, earlier, of writing something under it, *cleared*, or *paid*, the way you close an account. He had found that he did not want to. He did not want the word in the same column as the young Caesar’s sphinx.

He thought instead about the Greek with the inky knuckles reading the list of plate, slowly, every piece, and saying *I’ll note that*; and he found, sitting on his stool in the warm afternoon with Sosia whistling beside him, that he could not decide whether he had just been paid or just been written down.
