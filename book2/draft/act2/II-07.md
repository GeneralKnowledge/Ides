# Boots

POV: Nysa · Rome, the young Caesar’s house · late 41

---

They knocked like men who did not expect to have to knock twice, and the porter, who had been told by Glycon to open to nobody, looked at Nysa across the atrium with his mouth open and did nothing at all.

She went to the door herself. She did not open it. She put her face to the grille.

There were nine of them on the steps. She counted them before she thought about them, the way she would have counted anything on those steps, in a row, then the two at the back who were standing apart. They were soldiers. They had shields slung and helmets on and the mud of the Praeneste road dried grey up their legs, and their boots were the old kind, the heavy army boots with the iron in the soles, worn down at the outer edge from years of marching on the camber of a paved road. They were not new boots. Nobody on those steps had bought his boots this year.

In front of them, nearest the grille, was a centurion with a vine stick under his arm and a face she knew.

She did not know his name. She had never known it. She had seen him at the back gate of the Carinae a dozen times in the year of Mutina and the year after, coming for Antonius’s orders or for his wages or for his dinner, a square grey-haired man with a voice that filled a yard, who had once given her half an apple across the gate because, he said, she looked as though she were counting the pips. She had not seen him since Philippi.

He looked at the grille. He saw her eyes through it, and she saw him find her.

“It’s the Greek girl,” he said. “From the master’s house.” He did not say whose master. “Open up, girl. We don’t want to break it, it’s a nice door.”

---

She opened it. She did not know, afterwards, why she did it so quickly. The porter made a noise in his throat behind her and she paid him no attention.

The centurion came in alone. He left the others on the steps. He stood in the narrow atrium with the orator’s masks looking down at him from the wall and his helmet under his arm, and looked round at it all, the plain floor and the small pool and the cheap cushions on the bench, and his eyebrows went up.

“This is it?” he said. “This is his house?”

“This is the house.”

“Antonius has better stables.” He said it without malice, as a fact. He looked at her then, properly. “You’re in his house now. The young one’s.”

“Yes.”

“He took you in.”

“I came,” she said. “He let me.”

He nodded, as though that were a distinction he had heard before and respected. Then he put his helmet down on the bench, and took a tablet out of his belt, and opened it, and she saw that it was written on, a list of some kind, and that there was a space left at the bottom.

“The consul wants to know who’s in the houses,” he said. “The young Caesar’s friends. Their houses. Who’s in them, how many, whether there are arms. Whether the master’s coming back, and when, if anybody’s been told.” He held out the tablet. He held it the way the old man from Cremona had held out his deed at the door, in the spring, as if it settled something. “Nobody wants trouble. The consul says, no trouble. We count, we go. You count. You always counted.”

---

What she told him she did not write down anywhere, and when she went over it afterwards she found that she could not entirely remember it, which had never happened to her before with a number.

She remembered how the centurion stood while she talked, with his weight on one leg and the vine stick tapping against his boot. She remembered that he did not write while she was speaking but afterwards, slowly, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth, and that he asked her to say one figure again. She remembered the porter behind her in the passage to the kitchen, breathing.

She remembered thinking, while she spoke, that he was one of Antonius’s men and that she had been one of Antonius’s household, and that the young Caesar had gone out of the city by the Flaminian gate before dawn on the day the consul came in, without telling her or anybody in the wool room where he was going, and that Glycon had gone with him. She remembered thinking that she owed the young Caesar obedience because he was her patron by law, and that she owed this man half an apple.

She remembered the nine pairs of boots on the steps outside, the iron in their soles, the outer edges worn down on the camber of the road.

And she remembered, very clearly, the storeroom: the sacks on their planks and the jars in the sand and the low door at the back with its bar and its second lock, and the key to it on the ring at her own belt, under her shawl, where she could feel it against her hip the whole time she was speaking.

---

The centurion closed the tablet.

“That’s all, then,” he said. “That’s everything?”

“That’s what there is.”

He looked at her for a moment longer. She thought he was going to say something about the apple. He did not. He picked up his helmet from the bench and put it on and did up the strap under his chin, and he was a soldier again and not the man at the back gate.

“You want to watch yourself,” he said. “In here. When he comes back.” He looked round the narrow atrium once more. “If he comes back.”

“Will he?”

“The consul says not.” He shrugged. “The consul says a lot.”

He went out. She heard him on the steps, the voice that filled a yard, telling the others to form up, and then the boots going down the Ring-makers’ Stairs together, and then the noise of them going away across the Forum, and then nothing.

The porter came out of the passage.

“What did you tell him?” he said.

“How many we are.”

“That all?”

“He asked how many we are,” Nysa said. “I told him.”

The porter looked at her, and then at the door, and then at her again. He was not a clever man and he had never liked her. She watched him decide whether to ask again, and decide that it would be safer not to know, and go back to his stool by the door and sit down on it with his arms folded and his back very straight, as if somebody might come and ask him.

She went down to the storeroom. She did not need anything there. She stood on the bottom step with the lamp and looked at the low door at the back with its bar across it, and counted the sacks again, because they were there, sixty, she made it, she had not been wrong; and then she went up again to the wool room, where the old freedwoman with the bad eyes was holding a skein up to the light from the roof, trying to see what colour it was.

“Blue,” said Nysa, and sat down beside her and took the other end.
