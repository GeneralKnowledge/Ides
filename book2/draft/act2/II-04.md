# The Storeroom

POV: Nysa · Rome, the young Caesar’s house · autumn 41

---

Glycon gave her the key to the storeroom on the morning the young Caesar took to his bed again, and he gave it to her the way a man hands over a cup he has been told is poisoned.

“Count it,” he said. “All of it. Grain, oil, wine, the salt, the dried things. Then the room at the back.”

“What’s in the room at the back?”

“Count it,” Glycon said, “and you’ll know.”

The storeroom was under the house, down eleven steps from the passage behind the kitchen, cut back into the hill so that the far wall was rock with the marks of the picks still on it. It was cool and smelled of earth and of the sacks, and the light came from one slit high up in the street wall and from the lamp she carried. She set the lamp on the bench by the door and stood for a while with her back to it, letting her eyes learn the dark.

There was less than she had expected. She saw that before she had counted anything. In the Carinae the storerooms had gone back and back under Antonius’s house like a second house, jars to the ceiling, and the steward had needed a boy with a ladder to count the top rows. Here she could see the far wall from the door. There were jars of oil along the left side in a single row, sunk to the shoulder in sand. There were the grain sacks on their planks on the right, stacked four high. There was wine in a corner. There were strings of onions and garlic from the beams and a crate of dried figs and a tub of salt with its lid tied down, and that was all.

She began on the left, with the oil, because it was nearest.

---

She counted in Greek and in tens and wrote in Latin, because the tablet was for Glycon and Glycon had told her he did not trust a column of figures in a language his master’s enemies spoke better than his master did. She did not think that was the reason. She thought Glycon could not read Greek numerals quickly and did not want to say so. She wrote in Latin.

Thirty-four jars of oil. Twenty-one full, by the weight when she rocked them, nine half, four empty and not yet taken out. She lifted the lids of the full ones and smelled them. One had gone.

Sixty-two sacks of grain. She did not open them. She pressed each one with her hand as she went, high and low, the way the old housekeeper in the Domus had taught her to press a sack, to find where it had settled or been got at. Two had been got at, low down, by something with teeth. She wrote that.

It was while she was pressing the sacks that she began to work it out, and she did not want to, and she did it anyway, because it was what she did.

The house was, as near as she could count it, forty-three people. The young Caesar. His sister when she came, which was not often now that she was married. Glycon, the cook, the cook’s two boys, the porter, the six litter men, the women in the wool room, the bath slave, the secretaries, the old freedwoman with the eyes, the grooms, the boys who ran messages, herself. A man ate a measure of wheat in a few days. She did not know the exact figure, nobody had ever asked her to know it; but she knew what the Domus had gone through in a month, and the Domus had been two hundred, and she could divide.

Sixty-two sacks, less what the mice had had. Forty-three people.

She stood in the dark with her hand on the last sack and did not finish the sum. She did not need to. It was not a store for a siege. It was a store for a household that bought its bread in the market every few days like everybody else and had never thought it would have to do anything different.

---

The room at the back had a door of its own, low, with a bar across it and a second lock. The second key was on the same ring. She had to stoop to go in.

There were spears in it, standing in racks along both walls, the long ones with the leaf heads, perhaps sixty. There were shields stacked on their rims like plates in a kitchen, oval, plain, with no device painted on them. There were three chests that turned out, when she lifted the lids, to hold swords, the short kind, greased and wrapped in cloth, and a fourth chest full of sling bullets of lead, cold and heavy, like a chest of grey eggs.

She counted them. Of course she counted them. She knelt on the rock floor with the lamp beside her and took the swords out one at a time and laid them in rows on the cloth and counted the rows, and put them back, and wrote down the number. She had never counted swords before. It was the same as counting anything else. That surprised her more than the swords did.

There were also, in the corner behind the shields, four helmets of the old kind with horsehair crests gone grey and moth-eaten, and a cuirass that had been made for a smaller man than any she had seen in the house, very fine, with silver on the shoulders. She looked at it for some time. She thought it had been the orator’s, and that nobody had known what to do with it, and she wrote it down at the bottom of the tablet by itself: *One breastplate, silvered, small, not anyone’s.*

---

She took the tablet up to Glycon at the eighth hour. He was in the small room off the atrium where the young Caesar worked, which was empty, because the young Caesar was in his bed with the door shut and the doctor going in and out with a face like a closed box.

Glycon read the tablet through standing up. He read it twice. Then he put it on the table and laid his hand flat on it.

“How long?” he said.

She told him. She had not finished the sum in the storeroom but she had finished it on the stairs.

He nodded slowly, as if it were the number he had expected and he had been hoping to be wrong.

“If the city is closed,” Nysa said.

“If the city is closed.”

“Will it be?”

Glycon looked at her. Then he looked at the shut door across the atrium where the young Caesar was lying.

“The consul is at Praeneste with his brother’s wife and three or four thousand men,” he said, “and Praeneste is a day’s march from the Esquiline gate. Our soldiers are at Capua and in the colonies, and Salvidienus is in the north with six legions he will need for Gaul. The man in that room knows all of that.” He took his hand off the tablet. “He asked me this morning how much grain we had. That’s all he asked.”

“Is he very ill?”

“He’s ill.” Glycon picked the tablet up again. “He was ill at Philippi. He was ill at Brundisium. He was ill in the spring. He’s always ill when it matters, and then it passes, and he gets up, and nobody can tell from his face what he decided while he was lying down.” He said it without any warmth and without any malice, as a man describes a road. “Get the grain sacks off the floor. Put them on the empty jars if you have to. And tell the cook he buys flour at the market from tomorrow, every day, a little more than we need. Not a lot more. A little.”

---

She went to tell the cook. On the way she had to pass the door of the young Caesar’s room, and it was open a hand’s width, and she did not mean to look in and did.

He was not lying down. He was sitting on the edge of the bed in his tunic with a blanket round his shoulders, very pale, with his feet on the cold floor and a tablet on his knee, writing. The doctor was not there. The lamp was by his elbow. He was writing slowly, in small letters, and as she passed he stopped and looked at what he had written and rubbed something out with the flat of the stylus and wrote it again.

He did not look up. His face had nothing on it at all. It was the face of a man copying out a list of names he had already decided on, and it made her feel, going down the passage to the kitchen, safer than she had felt all day.

The cook was making the evening bread. She told him about the flour and he threw up his hands.

Then, because he was a talker and liked her, he told her what he had heard in the market that morning: that the consul’s men at Praeneste had a watchword now, and that it had been given out by the consul’s wife herself, at dawn, in her night plait, and that the word was *Philippi*.

Nysa stood by the bread table.

She had given Fulvia’s woman the true count once, at the games, in the Carinae, in the year Cicero died. Nobody in this house knew that. She had never told Glycon and Glycon had never asked, and the young Caesar had taken her in off the street the day after the Capena gate and set her at his door to count, and she had counted, and for half a year that had seemed to be the end of it. She thought now of Fulvia’s woman at Praeneste with her tablet, the one with the column of names on it, and wondered what was written in that column now, and whether there was a line for a freedwoman in the young Caesar’s house who knew how many sacks there were under it.

“Too much salt,” said the cook, about something, and she found she had put her hand in the bread.
