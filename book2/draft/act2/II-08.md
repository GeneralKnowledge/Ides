# Markers

POV: Octavian · the hills round Perusia · late autumn 41

---

Agrippa had painted the stakes white again. Octavian saw them from the litter as soon as the road came over the last ridge, a line of white points going away along the valley floor below the town and up the far slope and over it, and for a moment, with the fever still in his joints, he was at Philippi and it was the year before and he was being carried along a marsh.

He put his hand out through the curtains. The bearers stopped.

“There,” said Agrippa, riding up. He was pleased. He pointed with his whip. “And there. And round the back, where you can’t see it, down to the river. It’s all marked. We start digging tomorrow.”

Perusia stood on its hill above them in the thin afternoon light. It was an old town, older than Rome, with walls of great squared blocks that the Etruscans had put up when Rome was a village of huts, and gates with arches, and above the gates heads carved in the stone, worn by rain to the shape of faces in a dream. There was smoke going up from inside the walls in a dozen places. Lucius was in there. Lucius had come into it four days ago with his army behind him, at a run, with Agrippa on one side of the road and Salvidienus on the other, like a dog driven into a yard.

“How far round?” Octavian said.

“Fifty-six stades. More or less.” Agrippa said it the way another man would have said the name of a horse he had bought. “Ditch and bank and palisade, with towers. All the way round. Both sides of the hill. Nothing gets in, nothing gets out.”

“How long?”

“To dig?” Agrippa looked at the line of white stakes, measuring it with his eye. “With the men we have, a month. Less, if it doesn’t rain.”

It would rain. It was the end of the year. Octavian did not say so.

---

They had argued about it the night before, in the farmhouse he was using below the ridge, Agrippa and Salvidienus and Maecenas and he, round a table that the farmer’s wife had scrubbed white for them and then gone away from, crying, because they had taken her house.

Salvidienus had wanted to fight. Salvidienus always wanted to fight. He was a big loud man who had started life minding sheep, it was said, and had risen by being good in a battle and saying so, and he had turned round on the road to Gaul with six legions when Octavian sent for him and come back down Italy at a pace that had made even Agrippa respect him. He had his legions, he said. Lucius’s men were levies and colony men and farm boys. Two days, he said. Give me two days in front of the walls, and an assault on the third, and the consul will be in chains by the Kalends.

Agrippa had said nothing to that. He had looked at Octavian.

And Octavian had sat at the scrubbed table with his cloak up to his chin and his hands round a cup of warm water, and had thought about two days in front of those walls. He had thought about who was in the town: Antonian veterans, some of them, the men who had walked through Cassius’s camp at Philippi while he lay in the reeds. He had thought about who was outside it, his own men, half of them the same kind, settled on the same plain, who had sat in the basilica at Teanum and laughed when Lucius made a joke. He had thought about what those men would do if they were ordered up a ladder against men they had shared a tent with, and about Pollio and Ventidius and Plancus somewhere to the north and east with their legions, waiting to see which way it went.

He had thought about Philippi, about what had happened there to the part of the line that had been his, and about the fact that everybody in the room knew it, and that nobody would say it.

“We’ll dig,” he had said.

Salvidienus had stared at him. “Dig?”

“We’ll put a wall round them and wait. They came in four days ago with what they could carry. The town has whatever it had in its granaries for the winter. That’s all they have.” He had looked at Agrippa. “How many people in a town that size?”

“Twenty thousand. Thirty, with the army.”

“Then they’ll be hungry by the new year.” He had put his cup down. “Nobody will be ordered up a ladder. Nobody will have to kill anybody. They’ll come out when they’re hungry enough, and we’ll be standing at the gate, and that will be the end of it.”

Salvidienus had said something then that he should not have said, about generals who preferred spades to swords, and Maecenas had laughed lightly and poured him more wine and asked him about the roads in Gaul, and the moment had gone past. But Octavian had heard it, and he had seen Agrippa hear it, and he had written it in his mind on the list where he kept such things.

---

Now he got down from the litter and stood on the ridge. The wind came across the valley and went through his cloak, and he let it.

Below him the first cohorts were coming up the road with their tools on their shoulders, picks and mattocks and the baskets for carrying earth, and centurions walking beside them. They were in good order. They were singing something, a marching song with a filthy verse about the consul, and the centurions were letting them. He could see their faces from where he stood, the near ones, sunburnt and cheerful, men who had been told they were going to dig and not to fight, and were glad.

“They like it,” he said.

“They like being alive,” said Agrippa. “It’s not the same.” He was watching the cohorts too. “It’s a good choice. I’d have made it.”

“Salvidienus wouldn’t.”

“Salvidienus would have got a thousand men killed under those walls and been given a triumph for it.” Agrippa swung down off his horse and stood beside him. He was a head taller than Octavian and twice as broad, and he stood on the ridge in the wind as if it were not there. “This way nobody gets killed. Except in there.” He nodded at the town. “When the food runs out.”

“That’s their choice.”

“It’s Lucius’s choice.” Agrippa was quiet for a moment. “The slaves will go first. Then the townspeople. Then the army. That’s how it goes in a siege. The soldiers eat last, but they eat.”

Octavian looked at the smoke going up from inside the walls.

He had not thought about the townspeople. He had thought about Lucius, and about the soldiers, his and Lucius’s, and about the generals in the north, and about what Rome would say. He had not thought about whoever had lit those dozen fires in there, the people whose town it was, who had not asked to have a consul run into it with an army at his back and the young Caesar close behind. He had thought about how many there were. He had asked Agrippa for the number and Agrippa had given it to him. Twenty thousand. Thirty, with the army.

He found he did not want to think about them now. He put them away. He was good at putting things away. He had been doing it since he was eighteen and a letter came to Apollonia from his mother.

“How soon can the ditch close the road?” he said.

Agrippa looked at him sideways, with the expression he had, sometimes, which was not quite a smile.

“The road’s closed already,” he said. “I closed it this morning. Before you came over the ridge.” He pointed with his whip, down to where the road from the south went in under the old Etruscan gate, and Octavian saw that there was a line of men across it, and a cart turned on its side, and a white stake. “I knew you’d say dig.”
