# The Granaries

POV: Lucius Antonius · Perusia · late autumn 41

---

The town’s granaries were in the rock under the citadel, a row of cellars cut into the hill by the Etruscans, and the magistrate who kept the keys took Lucius down to them himself, carrying the lamp, going ahead of him down the steps one at a time like an old man going down to bathe.

His name was Vettius, or Vettienus, Lucius had not caught it, and he had been very courteous for four days. He had given the consul his own house. He had given the consul’s officers the houses of his neighbours. He had stood beside Lucius on the wall on the first evening and pointed out the hills and named them, and the roads, and where each one went, and had said, with a little bow, that the people of Perusia had never in their history closed their gates to a consul of the Roman people and did not intend to begin. He had said nothing about the young Caesar’s army coming up the valley behind the consul, or about the white stakes that had appeared along the valley floor by the second morning. He was the kind of man who did not say things.

At the bottom of the steps he held the lamp up.

“The public granary,” he said. “The private stores are in the houses. Each household keeps its own, as is our custom. These are the town’s.”

Lucius looked.

There were eleven cellars. He walked the length of them with the old man and the lamp, and in each one the light went over the heaps and the sacks and the big sealed jars, and in each one the old man said a number, quietly, the way a priest says the name of a god he is not sure of. At the end he did the sum aloud, in the Etruscan way, which Lucius could not follow, and then turned it into Latin.

Lucius stood in the last cellar with the lamp-smoke going up past his face.

He was good at figures. That was a thing Marcus had never understood about him, or had understood and found funny: that the younger brother who made the long speeches could also sit down with a surveyor’s tablet and add up the iugera of a whole district in his head and get it right. He had done it on the land board, in the year Caesar died. He did it now.

The town. The army. The slaves and the camp-followers who had come in with the army because they had nowhere else to go. Divide.

“How long?” said the old man, who had done it too.

Lucius told him a number. It was not the right number. It was a longer one.

The old man looked at him for a moment in the lamplight. Then he bowed, and lowered the lamp, and they went up the steps again into the light.

---

The letters came that evening. There were three of them, which in four days of waiting was more than he had let himself hope for, and they came together, by one rider, a boy from Pollio’s horse who had come round the back of the hill by the river path before Agrippa’s men closed it. The boy was white with cold and pride. Lucius gave him wine himself and sent him to be fed, and took the three tablets into the room in the magistrate’s house that he had made his own, and shut the door, and sat down with them on the bed.

Pollio’s first. Pollio wrote a good letter. He was a man of letters; he wrote tragedies, Lucius had heard, and had a way of putting a sentence together that made you feel you had been taken by the arm and walked round a garden. He wrote from Ravenna. He wrote that he had heard with the greatest concern of the consul’s situation, and that his legions were ready, and that he was in daily communication with Ventidius, and that the two of them were considering together, with the utmost seriousness, the best and safest way to bring help to the consul in a manner that would be in accordance with the wishes of his brother.

Lucius read it twice. It was a good letter.

Ventidius’s second. Ventidius wrote a short letter, in a big hand, like a muleteer’s, because he had been a muleteer, everyone knew it, before Caesar made him a soldier and a senator and a laughing-stock for Cicero. He wrote from Ariminum. He wrote that he was coming. He wrote that Pollio was coming. He wrote that they were waiting for Plancus.

Plancus last. Plancus wrote a long letter about the roads.

He read it three times. It was about the roads in Umbria at this time of year, and the bridges, and the rains, and the state of the country between Spoletium and the Tiber, and the difficulty of moving legions across it in safety, and how much better it would be, from every point of view, to wait for clearer word from the consul’s brother, who would surely by now have had the news and would surely have sent orders. It was a very reasonable letter. At the end it wished the consul health.

Lucius put the three tablets side by side on the blanket.

They were coming. That was what the letters said, if you read them together. Pollio was ready, and Ventidius was coming, and they were only waiting for Plancus, and Plancus was only waiting for the roads, and for Marcus. Three generals, eleven legions between them, a few days’ march to the north and east. It was what he had said to the senators at Praeneste. It was what he had said in the Forum. Choose your ground and let them come. He had chosen his ground. It was a hill with walls round it, old walls, the best walls in Etruria, and the generals were coming, slowly, carefully, the way men come who have a great deal to lose and want to arrive on the right side of it.

He felt the door open in his chest, the way it did, and the wind come in.

---

He called his officers in that night and read them Pollio’s letter aloud. He read it well. He gave them the sentences that took you by the arm. They cheered at the end of it, not loudly, because it was a small room, and the young officer who had said *Yes, consul* all the way from Rome to Praeneste and from Praeneste to here said it again, *yes, consul, yes*, with his face lit.

He did not read them Plancus. He told them Plancus was coming by Spoletium.

One of the older men, a tribune who had served under Marcus in Gaul and had a way of looking at Lucius as though he were a horse that might be sound, asked about the granaries.

“There’s enough,” Lucius said.

“For how long?”

“For long enough.”

The tribune looked at him with the horse look. Then he nodded, and did not ask again. Lucius saw him not ask, and saw him decide not to, and was grateful, and did not like being grateful.

When they had gone he sat on the bed with the lamp. He did not lie down. Outside, in the dark below the walls, he could hear the sound that had been going on all day and was going on still, by torchlight, which was the sound of a great many men digging. It was not loud. It was a steady low sound, picks in earth and stones and the knock of baskets being emptied, and voices, and every now and then a man laughing. It went all the way round the town. He had stood on the wall at dusk and listened to it come round the hill from one side and go away round the other.

He took a tablet and a stylus and wrote, at the top, *Grain*.

Then he wrote the old man’s number under it, the true one, in figures, small. He looked at it for some time. Then he rubbed it out with the flat of the stylus, smoothing the wax until there was nothing there, and wrote instead, in the same place, *To my brother, Marcus Antonius, triumvir, from Lucius, consul, at Perusia*, and began a letter.

He did not mention the grain in the letter. He mentioned Pollio and Ventidius and Plancus, and the walls, and the spirit of the men, and the loyalty of the people of Perusia, who had never closed their gates to a consul and did not intend to begin. He wrote that he was holding Italy for his brother and would hold it until his brother came. He wrote for a long time. Outside, the digging went on.
