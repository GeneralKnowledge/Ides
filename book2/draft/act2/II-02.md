# Plate

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Janus · September 41

---

She came at the hour when the arcade was emptiest, just after noon, when the moneychangers along the row had gone to eat and left their boys on the stools, and she came on foot, with one porter behind her carrying a basket covered with a cloth.

Hordeonius knew her before she reached the stall. He knew her by the walk, which was quick and narrow and kept to the wall, and by the hands, which she held folded in front of her at the waist the way women hold their hands in a temple. He had seen those hands two winters ago laid flat on this board while their owner asked him, in a pleasant voice, what his rate was by the month on a sum she did not name, and he had told her, and she had thanked him and gone away and not come back. He had seen them once before that, though she would not know it. He had seen them in his mind, in the spring of the year Cicero died, every time he wrote to the Carinae calling in the mistress’s paper.

He did not get up.

“Good day,” she said. “You lend against plate.”

“I lend against most things.”

She turned her head and the porter put the basket on the end of the board and stood back. She did not lift the cloth. She looked at Hordeonius, and he looked at her, and he saw her wait to find out whether he knew who she was. He gave her nothing. He had a face for that, which Sosia said was the face of a man reading an inscription in a language he did not much like.

“Whose plate?” he said.

“My mistress’s.”

“And your mistress is?”

She told him, without lowering her voice, so that the boy on the next stool heard it and dropped something. Hordeonius did not drop anything.

“Then let us see it,” he said.

---

She lifted the cloth herself.

There were eleven pieces in the basket, packed in straw. Two wine-mixing bowls, one with handles like swans’ necks. A tray with a hunting scene round the rim, chased, very good, Greek work of the last generation. Four cups. A pair of small jugs for oil. A ladle. A salt-cellar on three lion’s feet that was the best thing there and that she had put at the bottom, under the straw, where a careless man would not find it.

He took them out one at a time and set them on the board in a row and did not say anything about any of them. He looked at the undersides, where the weight was scratched. Most of them had a weight. Two had a name as well, an old one, scratched in a hand that was not the silversmith’s, and he turned those two face down without reading the name aloud.

Then he got out the scale.

It was the good scale, the one with the bronze pans that his father had bought in Delos, and he set it on the board and put the weights out beside it in their order, and he weighed every piece. He did not trust the scratches on the undersides. Nobody did. A household that was selling its plate had usually been selling it for some time, a cup here and a jug there, and the scratches were from the day it was bought.

She watched his hands. He was aware of her watching. She watched them the way he would have watched hers, not the scale but the fingers, to see whether he put a thumb on the pan or set the weight down with a click to make it bounce. He did neither. He had not done either in thirty years and he did not see why he should begin for Fulvia’s woman.

“Forty-one pounds and a little,” he said at the end. “The salt-cellar is the best of it.”

“It was her mother’s.”

“Then she will want it back.”

“She will want all of it back.” The woman said it without any expression at all. “That is why it is a loan and not a sale.”

He began to put the weights away.

“How much does she want?”

She told him.

He did not answer at once. It was more than the silver. Not by much, a fifth or so, the kind of fifth a lender gives against plate when he knows the house, and wants the house’s goodwill, and believes the house will be there to redeem it in a year. He sat with the last weight in his fingers and turned it over.

He thought about the Carinae in the spring of the year Cicero died. The Senate had voted Antonius an enemy, and everyone in Rome who had lent to his house had written that week to call it in, every one of them, he had been neither the first nor the last; and he had sat at this board and written his letter in the cool of the morning and sealed it and given it to Sosia, and had not thought about it again until he heard, in the summer, that the mistress had been taken to court over a farm and Atticus had stood surety for her. He had thought about it then, briefly. Then Antonius had come back over the Alps with Lepidus’s legions behind him and every man who had written that week had found himself reading a list in the Forum, hoping.

His name had not been on the list. He had wondered since then whose kindness that was. He had decided it was nobody’s, that he had been too small to put on a board. It was a comfort, being too small. He had cultivated it.

“The rate,” he said, “is two by the month.”

The woman did not move. “You told me one, two winters ago.”

He looked at her then. It was the first time either of them had admitted the other winter.

“Two winters ago,” he said, “the consul was not at Praeneste with two hundred men, and your mistress was not with him.”

The whole Janus knew where Fulvia had gone. The boys on the stools had been arguing all week about whether a consul who left Rome was still a consul, and had settled it, as far as Hordeonius could tell, by betting on it.

“She is in a house at Praeneste because the air is good,” the woman said, and her husband is the master of the East, and in the spring he will be in Italy. Then she will redeem her mother’s salt-cellar, and you will have had two by the month on it for the winter, and you will be glad you lent.” She paused. “Or you can lend at one, and be gladder.”

“I can lend at two,” said Hordeonius, “or I can not lend.”

She took a long breath through her nose, the only sign she gave.

“Two,” she said.

---

Sosia came back at the ninth hour, from a collection on the Aventine that had not gone well, and found the strongbox open and the eleven pieces of silver inside it in their straw, and stood looking down at them with his hands on his hips.

“Whose is that?”

Hordeonius told him.

Sosia was quiet for some time. He was not quiet as a rule. He was a big soft-voiced man with the patience of a cart-horse, and Hordeonius had once seen him sit outside a debtor’s door from dawn to dusk without moving except to eat an onion, and he had never seen him at a loss for something to say about a loan.

“She’s at war,” Sosia said. “The mistress. With the young Caesar.”

“Her brother-in-law is at war. She is at Praeneste.”

“With the consul.”

“With the consul,” Hordeonius agreed. “And her husband has the East. And her plate is silver, and silver is silver whoever wins.” He closed the lid of the strongbox. “It weighs the same in the young Caesar’s hand as in hers. If she does not redeem it, I sell it by weight in a year, and I have lost nothing but the workmanship. The salt-cellar alone would cover the interest.”

“And if the young Caesar’s men come to the Janus and ask who lent money to Antonius’s wife for her war?”

“Then I lent against plate,” said Hordeonius, “to a woman I had never seen before, who did not give her name.” He turned the key. “I have a terrible memory for faces. You know that. Everyone at the Janus knows it.”

Sosia looked at him. Then he went and sat down on the stool by the end of the board and took an onion out of his tunic and began to peel it, with his thumbnail, very slowly, all the way round.
