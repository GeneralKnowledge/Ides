# The Ditch

POV: Laberius · Octavian’s lines below Perusia · December 41

---

The ground here was red. That was the first thing he noticed when he put the mattock in, the morning he came up from the south with the draft from Capua: a red clay under a hand’s depth of black, heavy, wet, that came up off the blade in slabs and stuck to everything.

The men either side of him in the ditch talked about it. They were colony men, most of them, from the new plots round Capua and Beneventum, men who had been farming for one season and wanted everybody to know it. They picked up the clay and squeezed it and smelled it. One of them said it would grow wheat. Another said it would grow nothing but bricks. A third said his father in Picenum had had ground like this and grown beans on it that you could hang a cloak on.

Laberius did not join in. He had been a farmer for one season too, and he still could not tell good soil from bad by looking. He had tried. Aulius had shown him, in the summer, kneeling in the beans and crumbling a clod in his fingers and talking about it as if it were a person, and Laberius had knelt beside him and looked at the dirt and seen dirt. What he could tell was whether a bank would stand. He could look at a ditch-side and know how steep you could cut it before it slumped, and how long it would hold in rain, and where it would go first. Twenty years of camps had taught him that. He did not know what the clay would grow. He knew it would hold a bank at a good steep pitch if you got the water off it.

“Drain,” he said, to the man on his left. “Every twenty paces. Or the whole side goes the first time it pours.”

The man looked at him. Then at the vine stick that was not in his hand, because he did not have one any more, and then at the scars on his forearms, and then he said “Centurion,” and went to tell somebody.

---

He had come up with the second draft from Capua. That was what he told himself and it was true. A man from the young Caesar’s paymaster had come round the colony in the month after Vibius went, with another chest, and this time he had not stood in the square. He had come to the farms. He had come to Aulius’s farm on a grey morning and stood in the yard with the yellow dog sniffing his boots, and asked for the centurion, and said the young Caesar had need of men who knew how to make a camp. Three hundred, silver. And a centurion’s pay, and a centurion’s place, and the rent of the plot paid while he was gone.

Laberius had said yes before the man finished. He had said it standing in the yard. He told himself afterwards that it was the silver, and he still thought so, mostly. Three hundred was a great deal. With three hundred a man could pay a farmer something, at the end of it, when the land question was settled, whichever way it was settled. With three hundred a man could hold his head up at the colony forum and not be the centurion who had hit a man from his own old cohort with a pitchfork for a farmer’s boy and was sleeping under a cart in his own field.

He had not told the paymaster’s man about the cart. He had not told him about Aulius.

He had not told anybody, either, what he had said to Aulius the night before he left, at the door of the house, with the old man asleep on the bench inside and the children asleep in the back and Aulius’s wife standing behind Aulius holding a lamp. He had said that the plot was in Aulius’s care until he came back. He had said it in front of the wife, so that it would be heard. He had said that the beans were in, and that he had promised until the beans were in, and that now he was promising until he came back, and that if anyone came from the colony with a stake, Aulius was to tell them the centurion had left his land in the care of his tenant, and that the centurion would be home.

He had not called Aulius his tenant before. Aulius had not called himself one. Aulius had looked at him in the lamplight for a long time and then said *All right*, and that was all, and his wife had said nothing, and Laberius had gone back to the cart and lain under it and not slept.

He did not think about it in the ditch. He thought about the clay.

---

At the turn of the day the centurion of the draft came along the line and stopped above Laberius on the lip of the ditch, and squatted, and looked down at him.

“You’re the one,” he said. “Laberius. From Capua.”

“That’s me.”

“There’s a man asking for you. Up at the fifth tower.” He jerked his head. “One of the new legion. Says he’s your optio. I told him you weren’t anybody’s anything, you were in my ditch. He said tell you anyway.”

Laberius put down the mattock.

He found Vibius at the fifth tower, which was not a tower yet but four posts and a platform, sitting on the platform with his legs hanging over the edge, eating an apple. He had a helmet on that did not fit him. It came down over his eyebrows and made him look like a boy wearing his father’s.

“Well,” Vibius said. “Look what’s crawled up the valley.”

“Look what’s sitting down.”

“I’m on watch.” Vibius took another bite of the apple. “This is how you watch, in the new legion. You sit up high and look at the wall and if anybody comes over it you shout.” He looked at Laberius’s legs, which were red to the knee with the clay. “I heard they’d given you a spade. I said they’d be sorry. I said you’d have the whole cohort digging drains by nightfall.”

“Every twenty paces.”

“I know. I could hear you from here.” He held out the apple. Laberius took it and bit it and gave it back.

They looked at the wall.

It was close. That was the thing nobody had told him, coming up the valley, that he saw now from the platform: how close it was. Four hundred paces from the ditch to the foot of the hill, and the hill steep, and the wall on top of it, old, great blocks with grass growing between them, and along the top of it men. He could see the men. He could see them well enough to see how they stood, how they held their shields and leaned on the parapet, the way men lean on a parapet when they have been on it all day and have stopped expecting anything to happen. Some of them were watching the digging. One of them, near the gate, was eating something too.

“Our old lot,” said Vibius. “Some of them. I saw our old standard yesterday. And some of Antonius’s old men from Gaul. And a lot of boys from the farms that Lucius gave a sword to and told it was a war.”

Laberius looked along the wall.

He was looking for a face. He did not know which face. He looked at each man on the parapet as far as he could see, the near ones, the way he would have looked along a line on parade, and he was looking for somebody he would know. The tall man from the Marcii’s orchard had come north with the first draft for the young Caesar and was somewhere behind him in this ditch. That he knew. But the old third tent had split three ways at the colony forum, and the legion had split, and he did not know who had gone with the man on the trough.

“Rufrius?” he said.

“Not that I’ve seen.” Vibius threw the core of the apple down into the ditch. “You keep asking about Rufrius.”

“Do I?”

“Every time we see a wall.”

Laberius said he did not, and Vibius let it go.

On the wall, near the gate, the man who had been eating had stopped. He stood up from the parapet and stretched, with his arms over his head, the way a man stretches after sitting too long, and then he cupped his hands round his mouth and shouted something down the hill. It was too far to hear the words. But Laberius heard the voice, the pitch of it and the shape, and it was a voice that had been shouting down lines on parade grounds for twenty years, and it was shouting at the ditch, and for a moment, with the apple in his mouth, he thought he knew it.

Below him, along the red clay, the men in the ditch stopped digging and straightened up and shouted something back, and then they were all laughing, both sides, the wall and the ditch, at whatever it had been.
