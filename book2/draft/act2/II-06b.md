# The Consul’s Seal

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Janus · late 41

---

On the third day of the consul’s Rome a young man came to the stall with a tablet sealed in red, and Hordeonius knew before he broke it that it was going to be the largest sum anyone had ever asked him for, and that he was going to say no.

He knew it from the young man. He had the look. He was well born and well fed and he had ridden hard that morning, there was mud on the inside of his knees, and he stood at the board with his weight forward on his toes the way young men stand when they have been told a thing is urgent and believe it. He had the ring of an officer and a face that had said *Yes, consul* a great many times in the last three months and had begun to wonder what it would be like to say something else.

“From the consul,” he said, and put the tablet on the board.

Hordeonius looked at the seal before he touched it. It was a good seal, deep and clean, a head of Pietas with her hair bound up, and round it the consul’s name in small square letters. It had been cut recently. The edges of the letters were still sharp in the wax; nobody had yet had time to wear them down by pressing it into a hundred letters. He thought about that. He thought it was the newest seal he had seen on a consul’s business in thirty years.

He broke it. He read the tablet. The sum was what he had expected, give or take. The security was the public revenues of the Roman people for the coming year, as pledged by the consul in his office, and the private word of Lucius Antonius, and the private word of his brother, the triumvir, to be confirmed on his return.

He read it a second time, slowly, with his lips pressed together. He knew the young man was watching him and he did not hurry. It was the only power a lender had, in the end, the power to read slowly.

---

The Janus had been full since the morning the consul came in. Not with business. With talk. The arcade under the arch had been a crowd since dawn on the first day, men who had come up from the Forum after the speech at the Rostra with their deeds still in their hands, standing in knots between the stalls and telling each other what the consul had said, and then telling each other what it meant, and then asking the moneylenders what it meant, as though the moneylenders were augurs.

Hordeonius had told them he did not know. He had not known. He had sat on his stool on the first day with his shutter half down and watched the crowd and listened, and what he had listened for was not what the consul had said but what the young Caesar had done, which was nothing. The young Caesar’s house above the Ring-makers’ Stairs had its door shut. The young Caesar’s soldiers had gone out of the city. Lepidus, who had charge of Rome for the triumvirs, had gone too, quickly and without any speech, and nobody at the Janus could say in which direction.

On the second day the price of wheat in the market by the river had gone up by a fifth.

That was what Hordeonius had noticed. Not the speech. The wheat. A city that is about to be fought over buys bread, and the dealers by the river knew it before the Senate did, and when the wheat went up he had drawn his shutter the rest of the way down and gone home early.

---

“Well?” said the young man.

“It’s a great deal of money.”

“It’s for the soldiers.” The young man leaned on the board. “The consul has three cohorts in the city and four thousand men at Praeneste and more coming in every day, and they have to be fed, and some of them have to be paid, and the treasury is spoken for. It’s a loan against the revenues. In a year it comes back with the revenues. It’s the safest paper in Rome.”

“Who told you that?”

“The consul.”

“And did the consul say who would be collecting the revenues next year?”

The young man looked at him. He had very blue eyes, Hordeonius noticed, and they went, for a moment, entirely blank, the way a boy’s eyes go when his tutor asks him a question that is not in the book.

“The consul,” he said.

“Next year,” said Hordeonius, “there will be two new consuls. That is how consuls work. They are elected, and they last a year, and then they are somebody else.” He turned the tablet over on the board, face down, the way he did with paper he had not agreed to. “The revenues of the Roman people in the coming year will be collected by whoever holds Rome in the coming year. Do you know who that will be?”

“The consul holds it now.”

“He does. He has held it for three days.” Hordeonius folded his hands on the tablet. “I would like to see him hold it for thirty.”

---

The young man went down the row.

Hordeonius did not need to watch him go but he watched anyway. He went past four stalls, where the moneychangers looked at him and looked away, and stopped at the fifth, at the end, where the light came in from the Forum, where Bucco had his stool.

Bucco was there. Bucco was always there. He was a fat soft man with a voice like oil going into a lamp, and he had been Antonius’s agent at the Janus since before Mutina, and had gone east with the money after Philippi and come back in the winter with a new ring and a great deal of silver, and had spent the spring and summer lending it to veterans against land they had not yet been given. Hordeonius had watched him do it from his own stool, sixty paces away, all year. He had lent against the same tablets himself, now and then, at a third. He had not lent as much as Bucco. Nobody had.

Bucco read the consul’s tablet. He read it once, quickly. He did not turn it over. Hordeonius saw him look up at the young man and smile, and saw his hand go to the strongbox under the board before the young man had finished speaking, and that was when Hordeonius felt it, the small cold thing at the back of the neck that had kept him alive at the Janus through Sulla’s lists and Caesar’s war and the year of the proscriptions.

Bucco knew something.

He had to. Bucco had been in the East all winter. He had sat in Antonius’s antechambers at Ephesus and been paid out of Antonius’s chests. If the consul’s paper was good, Bucco would know it was good, because Bucco would know whether Antonius was coming. He would not open his strongbox for Lucius Antonius on the third day of a city that might be lost on the fourth, unless he had reason to think it would not be lost.

Hordeonius sat on his stool and watched Bucco count out silver into the young man’s bag, a great deal of silver, more than Hordeonius had in the house, and felt for a moment the old tightness in his chest that comes of watching someone else take the trade.

A month ago he would have lent. He knew that. A month ago, with the consul at Praeneste and the woman with the narrow hands across his board and her mistress’s salt-cellar in his straw, he had lent at two by the month and gone home and slept. He had not told anyone that it was the first loan in years that he had made because he liked the look of the side it was on. He did not tell himself so now.

Sosia came up beside the stall and leaned on the post.

“Bucco’s lending,” Sosia said.

“So I see.”

“A lot.”

“So I see.” Hordeonius took the consul’s tablet, the one he had turned face down, and slid it under the board where it would not be seen. “Bucco has been in the East. Perhaps he has heard from his master.”

“And you?”

“I have not been in the East,” said Hordeonius. “I have been here. Watching the price of wheat.” He looked down the row at Bucco tying the bag for the young man, with his fat fingers, double knots. “Bucco may know that Antonius is coming. I know that the young Caesar has gone out of the city with his soldiers, and that Salvidienus has turned round on the road to Gaul, and that wheat is up a fifth. I know that men who leave a city with soldiers usually come back with them.”

Sosia considered this.

“So Bucco knows something,” he said, “and you know something else.”

“Bucco knows what he has been told by people who want to be right,” said Hordeonius. “I know what the bakers are doing.”

That afternoon Hordeonius sat on his stool with the consul’s tablet under the board and watched Bucco tie the double knots, and thought that for once in his life he was the cautious man who was going to be proved right, and found, to his surprise, that it did not feel like anything much.
