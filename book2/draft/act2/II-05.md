# Teanum

POV: Octavian · Teanum Sidicinum · autumn 41

---

The officers had put the table in the middle of the basilica, under the roof-opening, so that the light fell on the tablets and not on anybody’s face, and Octavian thought that whoever had decided that had been to more of these than he had.

There were forty or fifty of them round the walls. Tribunes, and senior centurions from both armies, and the men the colonies had sent, who were not officers any more and stood among the officers anyway with the stiffness of men who have been somebody’s superior for twenty years and have just been reminded of it. They had asked for this. They had sent to him at Capua and to Lucius at Praeneste in the same week with the same letter, and the letter had said, in a soldier’s Latin that he had read three times with real pleasure, that the army which had won at Philippi did not intend to fight itself over who should give it its farms, and would the two of them come to Teanum and be made to agree.

So he had come. He had come in a litter, because he was not yet well enough to ride, with Agrippa on a horse beside the curtains and Maecenas behind talking to everybody. And Lucius had come, on a horse, at a canter, with forty riders and his lictors running to keep up, and had swung down in the square outside so that every soldier in Teanum could see the consul of the Roman people arrive at a gallop.

Now Lucius was at the far end of the table, and Octavian was at the near end, and between them the oldest tribune present, a grey man with one ear, was reading out the terms.

---

They were good terms. Octavian had seen them in draft the night before, at the house where he was lodged, and he had sat up with them past the second watch with the lamp turned low and a cup of warm water and honey at his elbow, going through each line for what it would cost.

The consuls were to govern according to the custom of the ancestors, and the triumvirs were not to hinder them. That cost nothing; it was a sentence. Land was to be given only to those who had fought at Philippi. That cost him the men he had been enlisting since the summer with silver in their hands, who had not been at Philippi, who would now have to be paid in something else. The money from the sale of the proscribed men’s estates was to go to the soldiers who were owed it. That cost him more. Antonius’s soldiers in Italy were to have their land from Antonius’s own agents, and not his, so that each man would know whom to thank. Manius would have that. Manius had probably written it.

And the young Caesar was to send no more troops through the passes to the north without the consul’s knowledge, which meant Salvidienus and his six legions were to stand still on the road to Gaul until Lucius said they could move.

He had read that one several times. Then he had put the tablet down and turned the lamp out and lain on the bed in the dark with his hands on his chest, and thought about how much he had already promised that he could not give back.

He had promised the men in the colonies their land, all of it, now, before Antonius could come home and give it them himself. He had promised the men he had been enlisting their silver, and some of it had come out of the money set aside for the farmers, which had been promised to the farmers. He had promised the farmers, through Lucius’s pledge on the steps of Saturn which he had been forced to say he agreed with, that no man would be put off unpaid. He had promised the Senate, in a letter, that he had no quarrel with the consul. He had promised Agrippa, in his tent, that there would be no war. Every one of those promises was in somebody’s hands as a tablet with his seal on it. He had lain there in the dark and tried to add them up and had found that they did not add. They had never added. He had simply never before had to lie still long enough to notice.

---

The tribune with one ear finished reading. He put the tablet down and looked along the table, first at Lucius and then at Octavian, with the patient look of a man who has divided up the plunder of a great many towns.

“Well?” he said.

“I agree to it,” said Lucius, at once, warmly, before anybody else could speak. “All of it. I’d have written most of it myself.” He smiled down the table. “I think I did write some of it. The first part, about the consuls.”

Some of the officers laughed. Octavian watched which ones. They were mostly the Antonians, the men on Lucius’s side of the room, and one or two of his own. He made a note, without moving, of the one or two.

“Caesar?” said the tribune.

“I agree to it,” he said.

He said it quietly, so that they had to lean in, and he did not smile. Then he held out his hand for the stylus.

There was a moment, while the tribune passed him the stylus and the tablet, when he looked round the walls of the basilica and saw them all looking at him, forty or fifty of them, the faces of the army that had won the world for three men in a tent; and it seemed to him that they were his. Not all of them. Enough of them. The colony men, who had their land from his surveyors. The centurions he had paid since the summer. The tribunes who had served with Agrippa. They had made this meeting because they did not want to fight, and the man they did not want to fight for was Lucius, who had nothing to pay them with but speeches. He could see it. He was nearly sure he could see it. He signed his name, small and clear, under the terms.

Then he passed the tablet back, and while the tribune was reading the signature aloud, Octavian read the bottom of the tablet upside down, and saw what was not on it.

There were the names of the two parties, his and Lucius’s. There were the names of the tribunes who had witnessed it, nine of them. There was no name for Fulvia. There was no name for Manius. And there was no name for any of Antonius’s generals in the north, not Pollio, not Ventidius, not Plancus, who between them had more legions than he and Lucius together, and who had been asked to send someone to Teanum, and had not.

He looked down the table at Lucius. Lucius was shaking the tribune’s hand.

---

Agrippa found him afterwards in the yard behind the basilica, sitting on a mounting-block with his cloak up round his chin, waiting for the litter.

“Well, that’s that,” Agrippa said. He did not sound as if he thought so.

“Who sent the tribune with one ear?”

“Nobody sent him. He came from the colonies at Beneventum. He was in the Seventh.” Agrippa sat down on the edge of the trough. He was a big young man and the trough tipped. “Why?”

“He looked at Lucius first.”

“He looked at the consul first. Lucius is the consul.”

“Yes,” said Octavian.

He thought about the one or two of his own who had laughed. He thought about the names that were not on the bottom of the tablet. He thought about Salvidienus standing still on the road north with six legions, because the consul said so, and about how long Salvidienus would stand still if the consul told him to and the young Caesar did not.

“It won’t hold,” Agrippa said.

“It won’t hold.” He pulled the cloak tighter. “But they’ll remember that I signed it. And they’ll remember who didn’t come.”

The litter came round the corner of the yard with the bearers half-running, and Agrippa got up to see to it.

“How much have you promised them?” Agrippa said, with his back to him, quite casually, the way he asked about the weather.

Octavian looked at the back of his friend’s neck, which was burnt red by the summer, and at the bearers setting the litter down, and at the gate of the yard where two of the officers from the meeting had stopped to talk and were looking his way.

“Enough,” he said, and got into the litter, and drew the curtains.
