# The Paymaster

POV: Laberius · the colony forum north of Capua · late summer 41

---

The colony had a forum now, because the surveyor had said it should have one, and so there was a square of trodden earth at the crossing of the two main tracks with a stake at each corner and a cart in the middle, and on the cart a chest, and on the chest a man in a good tunic with his hand flat on the lid.

Laberius came in from the south track at the third hour with Vibius and a sack of Aulius’s beans to sell, and saw the chest from the edge of the square, and stopped.

There were two of them. That was the first thing. The man on the cart with the chest was one, and across the square, standing on an upturned trough under the plane tree where the colony had started meeting because it was the only shade, there was another, a younger man with a loud voice and no chest at all. Each had a crowd. The crowd round the cart was bigger.

“That’s the young Caesar’s,” Vibius said. “The one with the box.”

“How do you know?”

“Because he’s got a box.”

Laberius put the sack down by the corner stake. He had a tooth on the right side at the back that had been going since Thessalonica, and he chewed on the left now, and when he was thinking he worked his jaw on that side without knowing he did it. Gaius used to say you could tell what Laberius thought of an order by how long he chewed it. He stood by the stake and chewed nothing and watched the cart.

The man in the good tunic was not making a speech. He was talking to the men at the front, one at a time, quite quietly, and every so often he lifted the lid of the chest a hand’s width and took something out and put it in a man’s palm and closed the man’s fingers over it with his own. Then the man went to the end of the cart, where a clerk with a tablet on his knee wrote something down. Then the man in the good tunic took the next one.

The man on the trough was making a speech. Laberius could hear it from where he stood. It was about the consul, and the consul’s brother, and the land of Italy, and how no veteran of Philippi would be put on a farmer’s field by a decree of the young Caesar’s when Antonius came home in the spring and settled it properly, the old way, with the farmers paid and the soldiers given land that was nobody’s. He had a good voice. He said *Antonius* the way you say the name of a man who is buying the next jug.

“What’s he got?” Laberius said.

“Him?” Vibius looked across at the trough. “A letter, he says. From the consul. Says the consul’s at Praeneste with an army and his brother’s coming with another.” He scratched his neck. “Says he’ll write your name down for Antonius if you want it written.”

“And the box?”

“Two hundred and fifty a man.” Vibius said it without looking at him. “Silver, now, today, in your hand. For your name on the young Caesar’s list for the winter. They’re making a legion out of men who’ve been discharged. You keep your plot. They pay the farmer’s rent on it while you’re gone, he says. The clerk writes that down too.”

Laberius chewed.

---

He knew most of the men in the crowd round the cart. That was the thing that kept him standing there, by the stake, with the beans at his feet. He had expected to know some of them; the colony was half his old cohort. He had not expected to see them like this, quiet and orderly, waiting their turn in a line nobody had drawn on the ground, with their hands at their sides and their eyes on the chest. He had stood at the head of lines like that at every pay-day for twenty years. They had never been so quiet for him.

The tall man was in it. He had a dirty linen pad tied over the side of his head where the pitchfork shaft had caught him, and he was near the front, and when he got to the cart the man in the good tunic said something to him and laughed, and the tall man laughed too, and put his hand out.

Ninnius was in it. Ninnius had sold the mule.

The man with the scar through his lip was not in it. Laberius looked for him, and found him in the end on the other side of the square, under the plane tree, at the back of the crowd round the trough. He was not listening to the speech. He was leaning on the tree with his arms folded, the way he had stood in the Marcii’s orchard, and he was watching the cart.

Across the square the young man on the trough said *the spring*, and *Antonius*, and *the old way*, and his crowd made a noise for him. It was not a big noise. Half of them were watching the cart over their shoulders while they made it.

“I’ll sell the beans,” Laberius said.

He picked up the sack. Vibius did not move.

“I’m going to put my name down,” Vibius said.

Laberius laughed. It came out of him before he had thought about it, short, through the nose, the way he laughed at Vibius twenty times a day. Vibius said things like that. He said them to see what your face did. He had said at Mutina that he was going over to Hirtius for the better bread, and at Philippi that he was going to sell his shield to the Thracians, and he had once told a tribune with a straight face that his mother was a priestess of Isis and he could not march on the Kalends.

Vibius did not laugh.

He was looking at the cart. He had his thumbs in his belt and his weight back on his heels, and he was looking at the chest the way he had looked at the gap in the line at Forum Gallorum, before he went into it, measuring.

“Two hundred and fifty,” Vibius said. “And the rent paid on mine. And I sleep in a tent with a roof on it and somebody else does the cooking.” He looked at Laberius then. “I’ve got nine iugera on the stony end by the colony boundary, Centurion, with a well I share with nine other men and a family of Oscans in a hut at the bottom who look at me like I ate their children. I’ve been there one season. I can’t plough. I never could. You know I never could.”

“You said you’d learn.”

“I said a lot of things.” He took his thumbs out of his belt. “I’m not asking you to come. I know you won’t.”

“Why won’t I?”

Vibius looked at him with something that was nearly pity and nearly temper and was mostly just tired.

“Because you’re going to stand in that farmer’s field until the beans are in,” he said, “and then you’re going to find another reason, and I’m not going to sit under the cart and watch you do it.”

---

He sold the beans to a woman at the south track for less than they were worth, because he did not want to stand in the square any longer bargaining, and she knew it and took them.

When he came back past the cart the line was shorter. The man in the good tunic had his hand on the chest lid and was talking to the clerk. Vibius was at the end of the cart. The clerk was writing.

Laberius did not go to him. He stood at the corner stake with the empty sack folded over his arm and watched Vibius bend to see what had been written, and point at something, and the clerk wipe it with the flat of his stylus and write it again. *Vibius*, whatever else. Laberius had never known the rest of his name. In twelve years he had never needed it.

Then the man in the good tunic opened the lid of the chest a hand’s width and took out a bag, small, the size of a fist, and put it in Vibius’s palm and closed Vibius’s fingers over it with his own.

Vibius came back across the square. He weighed the bag in his hand as he came, once, the way you weigh a stone you are going to throw.

“There,” he said.

“That’s done, then.”

“That’s done.” Vibius looked at the empty sack over Laberius’s arm. “What did you get for them?”

Laberius told him.

“She robbed you.”

“She did.”

They walked out of the square together by the south track. At the plane tree the young man had got down off the trough and was talking to three men at once with his hands, and the man with the scar through his lip had gone. The sun was high. The track went down between the new plots, past stakes and the start of hedges and a few huts going up in turf, and on either side men were working, or sitting, or standing in their doors looking at nothing.

“When do you go?” Laberius said.

“Kalends. They march us to Capua and then north.” Vibius was quiet for a few steps. “North, he says. He wouldn’t say where. Rome, maybe. Wherever the consul is.”

“The consul’s at Praeneste.”

“Then Praeneste, maybe.” He swung the little bag by its string. “Against Antonius’s brother. Against the old Second, some of it. That man on the trough had half the third tent listening to him.” He stopped swinging the bag. “I didn’t think of that till I was at the cart. I thought, I’ll be on a wall somewhere and look over and there’ll be Rufrius.”

“Rufrius went east.”

“Did he?”

“With the eagle. After Philippi.” Laberius did not know that. He thought it. He had not seen Rufrius since the camp at Philippi, after, and it seemed to him that Rufrius was the kind of man who would go where the eagle went, because it saved deciding.

“Well,” said Vibius. “That’s all right, then.”

At the bottom of the track, where it met the road, the farm with the yellow dog was in sight across the beans, and the old man was on his bench in the sun, and Aulius’s wife was at the door shaking something out. The dog saw them and got up, and stood, and did not bark.

Vibius stopped at the corner of the field.

“You could have had a third off Bucco’s man,” he said, “and I could have had a third, and we could have had a mule between us and a share of somebody’s well. That was there. That was in May.” He put the little bag inside his tunic. “I just want you to know I knew it was there.”

He went off up the road toward his own plot on the stony end, to get his things, and Laberius stood at the corner of Aulius’s field and worked his jaw on the left side for a long time, and then went in to tell Aulius the price of beans.
