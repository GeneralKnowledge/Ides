# Three Letters

POV: Fulvia · Praeneste · December 41

---

She knew the seals before her woman had them out of the courier’s bag, and she laid them on the table in the order she expected to like them, and was wrong about the order.

Pollio’s was a head of Apollo, very fine, cut by a Greek who had cut gems for half the Senate, and the tablet under it was thick. Ventidius’s was a plain ring with his name, deep and clumsy, as if he had pressed it with his whole fist. Plancus’s was a ship.

She had written to all three in the same week, at the beginning of the month, when the news came up the Labici road that Lucius had gone into Perusia and the young Caesar’s men were digging round it. She had written them three different letters. To Pollio she had written as one person of education to another, about duty and honour and what Marcus would remember. To Ventidius she had written as a soldier’s wife to a soldier, that the consul was shut in a town with Antonius’s veterans and that every day those men stood on a wall waiting for him was a day he would have to explain to them afterwards. To Plancus she had written about money, because with Plancus it was always about money, and had told him what Marcus would give, and where, and when.

She opened Plancus’s first, since she had put him last.

It was long. That was the first thing. Plancus had never in his life written a long letter to anyone he was going to obey. He wrote about the roads. He wrote about the rains, and the bridges over the Tinia, and the state of the country between Spoletium and the Tiber, and the risk to the legions of moving through it at this season with the young Caesar’s horse on every ridge. He wrote that he had the greatest respect for her judgement and for the consul’s courage. He wrote that he could not move without some word from Marcus, and that he was sure she would understand.

She read it twice. Then she put it down.

He was waiting. That was all it was. He was a cautious man who liked to be sure which way the wind was going before he put his sail up; she had known him for ten years, had sat at dinners with him, had watched him change sides twice and land on his feet both times. He would wait until it was clear, and then he would come, and when he came he would say that he had been coming all along. She thought she could live with that. She thought he would be at Perusia by the Kalends of February.

She opened Ventidius’s.

It was four lines. He had fallen back to Ariminum, he wrote. Pollio had fallen back to Ravenna. Plancus was at Spoletium and would not come. He would not fight the young Caesar without Antonius, he wrote, and without Plancus, and he was sorry for the consul. He did not wish her health.

She sat with the tablet in her hand for some time. Then she opened Pollio’s, and it was long, and beautifully written, and it said the same as Ventidius’s in two hundred words more, with a sentence near the end about the uncertain fortunes of war that she thought he had probably used before, in a play.

---

Her woman was standing by the door.

“Shall I send for the courier?” she said. “He’s in the kitchen. He’s going back by Tibur.”

“No.” Fulvia put Pollio’s letter down on top of the others, so that Ventidius’s four lines were under it. “Not yet.”

She got up and went to the window.

It was the same window. She had stood at it in the summer, the first night, with Lucius behind her telling her about two hundred and fourteen men and eleven senators, and she had looked out at the dark of the plain and thought about how much it would cost. Now it was winter and the plain was brown and there was frost on the road in the mornings, and there were not eleven senators in Praeneste any more but four, and the others had gone home to Rome by one road or another, quietly, with their cooks.

Lucius was ninety miles north. She did the sum without wanting to. Ninety miles, and a ditch and a wall all the way round him, and a valley full of the young Caesar’s soldiers digging. She had had one letter from him in a month, brought out by a boy who had come down the river at night on a raft. It had been a very cheerful letter. It had been about the walls, and the generals, and the loyalty of the people of Perusia, and at the end of it he had said that he was holding Italy for Marcus. He had said nothing at all about the grain.

That was how she knew.

---

Claudia came in at the ninth hour, with her sewing, as she did every day now. She did not ask to come. She came and sat on the bench under the window with her back to the light, the way Fulvia had taught her to sit when she was small, so that the work was lit and her face was not, and she sewed. She was making something grey. Fulvia had not asked what it was.

“Is it the letters?” Claudia said, after a while, without looking up.

“It’s the letters.”

“Are they coming?”

Fulvia looked at her daughter. Claudia was sixteen now, or nearly. She had come back from the young Caesar’s house in the summer with a bundle and without her doll and with a face like a shut door, and the door had not opened since, not to her mother, not to anybody. She sewed. She sat in rooms. She went where she was taken. She had stood at the edge of the flat ground below the temple in September in a grey cloak, while her mother gave the watchword and the men beat their shields, and had not looked at anything.

“No,” said Fulvia.

Claudia’s needle went in and came out.

“Will you tell Uncle Lucius?”

“I’ll tell him they are on their way.”

Claudia did look up then. She looked at her mother for a long moment with her father’s eyes, Clodius’s eyes, that had looked at Fulvia across a dinner table once when she was younger than Claudia was now and decided that she would do.

“Because he’ll stop eating,” Claudia said, “if you don’t.”

Fulvia did not answer that. She did not have to. Claudia went back to the grey thing in her lap, and Fulvia went back to the table, and sat down, and took a fresh tablet from the pile, and wrote to Lucius.

She wrote that Pollio was ready and waiting only for the roads. She wrote that Ventidius was coming. She wrote that Plancus was at Spoletium with his legions and would move as soon as there was word from Marcus, and that she had written to Marcus again, by the ship from Puteoli, and expected an answer in the spring. She wrote that the consul should hold. She wrote that she was proud of him. That part was true, and she found that it was the hardest part to write.

She sealed it and called for the courier.

Then she sat for a while with the three letters from the generals in front of her, the Apollo and the fist and the ship, and moved them about on the table with one finger, changing their order, as if there were an order in which they would say something else.
