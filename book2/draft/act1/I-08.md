# The Bundle

POV: Nysa · Rome, the young Caesar’s house and the Carinae · summer 41

---

Glycon came to the wool room in the middle of the afternoon, which he never did, and said, “Leave that. Come and pack.”

Nysa put down the tunic she was hemming. The old freedwoman beside her did not look up. She had stopped sewing a little while before and was sitting with her hands in her lap, and Nysa had thought it was her eyes.

“Pack what?”

“The mistress’s things.” Glycon looked at the old woman and then away. “All of them. Her woman will tell you what’s hers. Anything she doesn’t know, ask me. Don’t ask anybody else.” He paused in the doorway. “She’s going home to her mother.”

---

Claudia’s room was at the back of the house, on the garden, the best room in it after the young Caesar’s own, and it had the only good things in the house in it. A bronze mirror on a stand. A chest of citrus-wood with ivory on the corners. A couch with a coverlet of Milesian wool dyed the colour of the sea, which Nysa had seen through the door in the spring and thought must have come from the Carinae, because nothing in this house was that colour.

Claudia was sitting on the couch when Nysa came in. She was dressed for the street, in the pale blue stola and a dark cloak over it, and her hair was done, and her hands were in her lap. She looked at Nysa without interest, as if Nysa were a stool somebody had brought in.

Her woman was at the chest. She was the one with the basket who walked a step behind on the way to Vesta, a woman of fifty or so with thick wrists and a Greek accent from somewhere in the islands, and she did not look up either.

“Start with the clothes,” she said. “I’ll do the jewels.”

Nysa started with the clothes.

There were not many. Six stolas and their under-tunics, two cloaks, a palla for the temple, a pair of red shoes that had never been worn and a pair of grey ones that had been worn a great deal. Nysa folded each one and laid it in the bundle-cloth and counted it as she went, in Greek, and the Greek woman heard her counting and glanced up for the first time, and then down again.

There was a doll in the bottom of the clothes chest. It was a jointed doll of bone, the arms and legs held on with pegs, with a little painted face that had mostly worn off and a tunic of red wool that somebody had sewn for it very small. Nysa took it out and held it, and did not know which pile it belonged in.

“Leave that,” said Claudia, from the couch.

Nysa looked at her.

“Leave it.” The girl’s voice was quite flat. “It goes to the temple. I’m married. You give them to the Lares when you’re married.” She looked at the doll in Nysa’s hands and then at the wall. “I forgot.”

Nysa put it on the chest lid, by itself.

---

They were done before the ninth hour. It made two bundles and the citrus-wood chest, which two of the house slaves carried down to the door between them. The Greek woman took the jewel box under her own arm and would not give it up. Nysa took the bigger bundle on her back, as she had carried things all her life, with the knot across her chest and her hands free.

Glycon met them in the atrium. He had a tablet in his hand, sealed. He gave it to the Greek woman, not to Claudia.

“For the lady Fulvia,” he said. “From the young Caesar. With his respects.” He looked at the Greek woman steadily. “He asks me to say, as well, with the letter, in his own words, that he returns her daughter to her as he received her.”

The Greek woman’s face did not move. Claudia, behind her, looked at the floor.

“As he received her,” Glycon said again. “Those words.”

“I heard,” said the Greek woman.

Nysa stood with the bundle on her back and looked at the masks on the atrium wall, the dead orator’s family, and did not look at anybody.

---

They went across the Forum and up the Argiletum and along under the Esquiline in the heat, the four of them and the chest, and nobody in the streets looked twice at a girl in a blue stola walking with her women and her baggage, because girls went to their mothers’ houses every day. Claudia walked in front. She did not look back once. Nysa walked last, under the bundle, and counted the steps to keep from thinking, and lost count at the Subura corner, where a man selling hot chickpeas called out to her by the name of the Domus kitchen, the old one, *girl*, and she did not turn her head.

Then they were in the Carinae, and the lane, and the gate she had come out of in the winter with her own bundle on her back going the other way.

The porter was the same porter. He opened the gate for Claudia and stood back, and then he saw Nysa at the end of the line under the bundle, and his mouth opened, and Nysa looked at him until he shut it.

They went in by the front, because it was the daughter of the house coming home, and Nysa had never once in two years come in by the front. The atrium was as she remembered it and not. It was cooler. There was a smell of new paint and of something burned. There were men in it, a lot of men, soldiers and men who were not quite soldiers, standing about in twos and threes and talking low, and they stopped talking when Claudia came in and watched her cross to the inner door.

Fulvia was at the inner door.

Nysa had seen her a hundred times from the passage and the scullery and the end of the dining room with a jug, and had never once been looked at by her. Fulvia did not look at her now. She looked at her daughter. She looked at her for a long moment, all of her, from the done hair to the grey shoes, the way you look at something you have had returned to you to see if it has been damaged in the carriage.

Then she put out her hand, and Claudia went to her, and Fulvia took her in under her arm and turned with her, and they were gone through the inner door without a word.

The Greek woman gave the sealed tablet to the steward. He was greyer than he had been in the winter, and he looked at the tablet and at the seal, and then he looked past the Greek woman to the end of the line and saw Nysa, and stood quite still.

“Put it down,” he said at last. “The bundle. In the passage. It goes to her room.” He did not say her name. “You know the way.”

She did know the way. She went down the passage with the bundle on her back past the door of the dining room and the door of the silver room, and the silver room door was open, and the silver-woman was in it at her table with the scales.

The silver-woman looked up.

She was a narrow woman with clever hands who had been with Fulvia since Clodius, and she had once, in the winter of the lists, stood in this passage and asked Nysa how many people had been at the tribunal, and Nysa had told her the true number. Neither of them had said anything about it since. Neither of them said anything now. The silver-woman looked at the bundle on Nysa’s back and at the knot across her chest, and her eyes went over Nysa’s face the way they went over a dish on the scales, finding the weight, and then she looked down at her table again.

Nysa put the bundle on the floor of Claudia’s old room, which was exactly as it had been, with the same coverlet on the bed, and stood for a moment in the doorway.

Down the passage, from the kitchen, she could smell bread. It was the cook’s bread, the good one, the one with the fennel seed in it that he made on market days. She had not smelled it since the winter. She stood in the doorway of the room with her hands at her sides and breathed it, and did not go toward it, and after a while she went back up the passage to the atrium and out by the front gate, because she had come in that way, and the porter let her out.
