# The River

**Pilot scene I-04** · POV: Vettius (invented) · Trebia and Placentia, midwinter 218 BC
**Sources:** Pol. 3.71–74 (weather, the crossing, the Romans cold and unfed: 3.72, text; the break-through to Placentia and Mago’s ambush: to check)

---

The Numidians came before it was light, riding along the far bank and shouting at the camp. Vettius heard them first as laughter, high and quick, and then the trumpets answered and the whole camp began to get up in the dark.

He had gone to sleep with his feet wrapped in his blanket and the end of the blanket wrapped round the feet of Sabidius, who snored, and for a moment he thought the noise was Sabidius. Then the centurion was in the tent door with a lamp and no helmet, saying that the consul was sending the horse out after them and the foot after the horse, and that any man still lying down when he finished speaking could explain himself to the consul in person.

“What about eating?” said Sabidius, still lying down.

“Eat the snow,” said the centurion, and went on to the next tent.

There was snow, as it turned out, and sleet in it, coming sideways off the hills. Vettius put on everything he owned. He owned two tunics, so he put on both, and the mail over them, and the cloak over that, and he still felt the cold come through at the collarbone where the mail sat. The fires had gone down to ash in the night. Nobody lit them. The line of men going out of the gate was already moving when he got to it, and he fell in with his maniple by the sound of Sabidius complaining.

They went down to the river at a walk. It was dark enough that he could see the water only as a place where the snow stopped. The horse had already crossed; he could hear them somewhere ahead, and the Numidians somewhere beyond them, still shouting, farther off now. The consul wanted a fight. That much had been clear for days, from the way the officers talked at the latrines and the way the tribunes looked at the sky. The other consul was lying in the camp with a wound from the fight near the Po, and the soldiers said he wanted to wait for spring, and the soldiers also said that Sempronius did not mean to share the credit with a man in bed.

The first rank went into the Trebia and stopped as though they had walked into a wall. Then they went on, because the ranks behind them did not stop.

Vettius had grown up on a farm below the Alban hills with a stream at the bottom of it, and he had been in cold water before, but this was something else. It took his breath at the knees. At the thigh it took the feeling out of his legs, and at the groin he made a noise he had not known he could make. By the time it reached his chest he was holding his shield over his head with both arms and his javelins in his teeth, and the current was leaning on him like a drunk who wanted to be friends. The man on his left went under and came up again. The man on his right was Sabidius, who was saying something about his mother over and over in a voice that had gone very small.

On the far bank he could not feel his feet at all. He stamped on them and felt the stamping in his knees. His tunics were wet through under the mail and the wind found them. He looked at his hands and they were someone else’s, white with blue round the nails, and he tried to close one round the javelin shaft and watched it close slowly, as if it had to think about it.

It was light now, or as light as it was going to be. The snow came across the field in sheets, and between the sheets he saw the enemy.

They were drawn up on the flat ground beyond the river, a long line of them, not shouting and not moving. Smoke still went up behind them from a great many fires. Vettius looked at the smoke for a long time. He thought about what you would cook on a fire like that, at that hour, if you knew you had all morning, and his stomach turned over and told him he had last eaten at noon the day before.

“They’ve had breakfast,” said Sabidius.

“They’ve had breakfast,” Vettius agreed.

At each end of the enemy line, standing a little forward of it in the snow, there were elephants.

He had heard about elephants. Everyone had heard. A man from the next maniple claimed to have seen one in a triumph when he was a boy and said they were stupid and afraid of pigs. There were no pigs. The elephants stood with their trunks hanging and their great ears moving a little, and men sat on their necks, and they looked across the snow at the Roman line without any interest at all.

The trumpets went. The light troops ran forward and threw and came back with fewer of them, and then it was the turn of the hastati, and Vettius was a hastatus, and he walked forward with his maniple towards the line.

He threw his first javelin badly because his hand would not open in time and it went off to the left. He threw the second one better. Then the shields came together and there was no more room to think about his hand.

The men in front of him were Gauls. He knew because they were big and shouted in a language that sounded like gargling, and because some of them wore nothing above the waist in that weather, which nobody else on earth would do. A Gaul’s long sword came down on the rim of his shield and the shock went up his frozen arm to the shoulder and he could not tell whether it hurt. He pushed. The man behind him pushed. That was all a battle was, the old soldiers had told him, a crowd trying to get through a door, and for a while it was only that, the pushing, and the stabbing low under the shields at whatever was there, and the shouting, and the snow.

Then he heard a different noise behind and to the right.

He could not see it. Nobody in the line could see anything but the man in front. But the noise came from the river side, from the stream bed with the high banks and the brush on them that they had all marched past in the dark without looking at, and it was the noise of a great many men shouting at once who had not been shouting before. The rear ranks began to shift. Somebody behind him said a word he did not catch, and then everyone was saying it.

Off on the wings, the elephants had started forward. He saw one for a moment between the bodies, much nearer than before, with a man in a red cloak hanging off its tusk, and then the line closed again and he did not see it.

What happened next he worked out later from what other men said, because at the time it only felt like falling forward. The Gauls in front of him gave way. They did not run, exactly; there were simply fewer of them, and then fewer, and then there was a man on the ground and a space where he had been, and Vettius was in the space and there was another space beyond it. The maniple went through. Other maniples went through on either side. They came out of the back of the enemy line all together, breathing as if they had climbed a mountain, and found nobody in front of them at all, only snow and a few baggage mules and a boy holding them who dropped the ropes and ran.

They stood there. The centurion stood there with them. Behind them the battle went on; Vettius could hear it, and when he turned round he could see the backs of the Gauls, and beyond the Gauls, through the snow, a churning of men and horses and the dark shapes of the elephants, all of it going towards the river.

“Well,” said the centurion. He had lost his shield and had blood in his beard that was not his. He looked back at the way they had come and then up the field, east, towards where the town would be, and Vettius watched him decide.

“The camp is on the other side of that,” the centurion said, pointing with his sword at the battle, “and so is the river. Placentia is that way. Anyone who wants to go back and swim, go and swim.”

Nobody wanted to swim.

They went to Placentia in a column, ten thousand of them, men said afterwards, though at the time Vettius counted only the men in front of him and lost count at forty. Nobody followed them. It snowed the whole way. His feet came back to life about halfway and he wished they had not. Sabidius walked beside him without speaking and then, at some point in the afternoon, began to speak again, about food, about every meal he had eaten since he was a child, in order, and Vettius let him because it was something to listen to.

The gates of Placentia were open when they arrived, and there were men on the walls with torches who shouted down to ask who they were. The centurion told them. Then someone on the wall asked where the rest of the army was, and the centurion did not answer that.

Inside the gate there was bread. Vettius ate it standing up, in the snow, with his back against the wall of a house. He thought about the farm under the Alban hills and his father telling him that the army fed you, that it was the one thing you could count on, and he laughed with his mouth full, and Sabidius asked him what was funny, and he could not explain.
