# Gratitude

POV: Fulvia · Rome, the Carinae · spring 41

---

Manius ate standing up, which was the first thing she held against him.

He had come in from the Appian Way at noon with his boots grey to the knee and gone straight to the tablinum as if it were his, and when the steward brought him a tray he took the bread off it and ate it walking up and down between Marcus’s book-chests, talking. He was a freedman of the house, or of the house’s friends, she had never been sure which, and he had managed Marcus’s affairs in Italy since before Mutina. He was good at it. He was a thin dark man who looked at everything as though he were pricing it, and he had been to Capua and to Beneventum and to Venusia in a month, and he wanted her to know it.

“They’re giving it all to his men,” he said. “The young Caesar’s. His surveyors, his freedmen with the lists, his name on the tablets. A man who stood in the line at Philippi under Marcus gets his land from a clerk with Octavian’s seal on his ring. Who does he thank?”

“He thanks the land,” Lucius said.

Lucius was on the couch with his feet up, the consul of Rome, picking at a bunch of raisins. He had been at the Senate all morning and come home cross.

“He thanks whoever gave it him,” said Manius. “Soldiers aren’t philosophers. Ask any of them in a year who gave him his farm, and he’ll say Caesar. Not Antonius. Caesar. Marcus is in Ephesus being called a god by Greeks, and his own veterans are being taught to say the other name.”

Fulvia sat in the chair by the window with her keys in her lap.

She had heard most of this before. Manius had written it, in shorter words, every week since the winter, and she had sent his letters on to Marcus with her own on top, and Marcus had answered two of them. One had been about the price of silver at Ephesus. One had been about a fleet. Neither had been about land. She had read them both several times, looking for a sentence that was about Italy, and had not found one, and had put them in the chest in her bedroom with the others. There were not many others. Under them, at the bottom, was the oldest paper in the chest: the quaestors’ demand for the price of this house, which Marcus had bought at Caesar’s auction of Pompey’s goods and had been indignant, then and ever since, to be asked to pay for. She had kept it because nobody else would.

“Marcus knows,” she said.

Manius stopped walking. “Has he written?”

“He knows,” she said again, and Manius looked at her for a moment longer and then went back to his bread.

---

“What do you want?” she said to him, when Lucius had been called out to a man from the treasury and the room was quiet.

“Time.” Manius put down the crust. “The colonies are being planted now, this spring. If they’re planted by Octavian’s men, they’re Octavian’s colonies, and his men are his for the rest of their lives. If they wait until the autumn, until Marcus has sent people of his own, or come himself, then Marcus’s veterans get their land from Marcus’s hand.” He spread his own hands. “That’s all. A few months. Let the young Caesar find it slower than he likes.”

“And the farmers?”

“The farmers lose their land either way. Sooner or later. They’ll lose it to someone.” He looked at her steadily. “Better they lose it later and curse Octavian for it, than lose it now and have nobody to curse at all.”

She thought about it. She thought about it the way she thought about the household accounts, with her fingers on the keys in her lap, moving from one to the next. The storeroom. The cellar. The silver. The chest with Marcus’s letters in it.

“The soldiers here in Rome,” she said. “His. Marcus’s. Who are they waiting for?”

“For their land. For the lists.”

“No. Who are they waiting for?”

Manius understood her then, she saw it, and she saw him decide not to like it, and then decide that it would do.

---

They went out to the camp by the Esquiline gate in the late afternoon, in a litter, with the children.

She had not brought the children out of the house since Mutina. She brought them now because Manius was right and because she had nobody else to send. Marcus’s sons: Antyllus, six, in a little bordered toga that was too warm for the day, sitting very upright in the litter with his hands on his knees as he had been told, and Iullus on the nurse’s lap, asleep, with his mouth open.

They called it the camp. It was a field outside the walls where the men of three legions who had been discharged after Philippi and come to Rome for their lists had put up what they could, tents and lean-tos and a few huts of turf, and were waiting. They had been waiting since the winter. The field smelled of it.

She had the bearers stop at the edge and got down. She did not wait to be handed. She took Antyllus by the hand and walked into the field with the nurse behind her carrying Iullus, and Manius behind the nurse, and the men looked up from their fires and saw her.

She watched them see her.

It went through the field the way it always went, from the men nearest outward. A man at a fire nudged the man next to him. Someone stood up. Someone said her name, and then Marcus’s, and then a centurion she half knew, a big grey man with a broken nose who had been at Mutina, came pushing through between the tents and stopped in front of her and saluted, and then looked down at the boy, and his face changed.

“That’s his,” the centurion said. “That’s the triumvir’s boy.”

“Marcus Antonius,” Fulvia said. “His father’s name.”

The centurion went down on one knee in the mud, so that his face was level with Antyllus’s, and Antyllus looked at him with great seriousness and then, because nobody had told him not to, put out his hand and touched the man’s broken nose with one finger.

The men round them laughed. It was a good laugh, a loud one, and it spread, and by the time it had reached the far side of the field men were coming in from every side to see what was funny, and when they saw, they stayed.

She did not make a speech. She stood in the middle of them with her son’s hand in hers and let them look. She let them look at the boy, and at the baby asleep on the nurse’s shoulder, and at her. She let them think about whose children these were and where their father was, and whose name was on the tablets they were waiting for.

“When the triumvir sends to you,” she said at last, to the centurion, but loud enough, “you’ll know his hand.”

“We’d know it in the dark, lady,” said the centurion, and the men round him said so too.

She watched which of them said it. She marked the faces in the front, and the faces at the back that only smiled, and the faces at the edges that had not come in at all but stood by their fires and watched the litter. There were more of those than she liked. She noted it the way she noted a shortfall in the cellar, and did not let it into her face.

Iullus woke on the nurse’s shoulder and began to cry, and the men laughed again, kindly, and she let that be the end of it.

---

In the litter going home Antyllus sat with his hands on his knees and said nothing for a long time.

“Mother,” he said, as they came in under the Esquiline gate. “Why did the man kneel down?”

“Because of your father.”

He thought about that. “Is Father coming home?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

Manius was walking beside the litter. She saw him turn his head.

“When he’s finished,” Fulvia said. “Sit up straight. We’re nearly there.”
