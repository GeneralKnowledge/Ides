# The Marsh

**I-07** · POV: Silenus · the flooded country below the Apennines, spring 217 BC
**Sources:** the march through the marshes, four days and three nights in water, the last elephant, and Hannibal losing the sight of one eye (Pol. 3.78–79; Liv. 22.2): to check. Details of the march are invention inside that record.

---

Afterwards, when men asked him about the marsh, Silenus found that he could not remember the order of the days. He remembered the water. He remembered a great many separate things, very clearly, the way a man remembers a fever. But the days had run together into one long grey day with nights in it, and he could not have sworn how many.

The guides said four days and three nights. He wrote that down later because it was what everyone said.

They had come over the mountains from the Gauls’ country by a pass the Romans were not watching, and come down on the far side into a valley where the river had burst its banks with the spring rains and the snow melt, and spread itself over the whole country from hill to hill. There was no road because the road was under the water. There was no dry ground except, here and there, a hummock with a tree on it, or a heap of drowned baggage, or a dead mule, and the dead mules got more numerous as the days went on, until they were the only dry ground there was.

Hannibal had put the Spaniards and the Libyans in front, the old soldiers, with the baggage, because they would keep going. The Gauls came next, because they would not, and the cavalry came last behind the Gauls, to make sure of them. Silenus walked with the clerks among the baggage, in water to the knee and then to the thigh, and from time to time much deeper, when the man ahead stepped off whatever invisible ridge they were following and vanished up to his chest with a shout.

Nobody slept. That was the thing he remembered most. There was nowhere to lie down, so men did not lie down; they slept standing, leaning on each other, or they sat on the dead mules, or on the piled packs, with their feet still in the water. On the second night he saw a man of the Libyans fall asleep standing up and fall over into the water without waking, and come up still half asleep, spitting, and look round him with an expression of such complete bewilderment that Silenus laughed out loud, for the first time in days, and could not stop, and the Libyan looked at him and began to laugh too, and they stood in the flood together laughing like madmen until a clerk told them in Punic to be quiet.

The Gauls suffered most. They were not used to it, the old soldiers said; they had been born on dry farms and had never been made to go on when going on was impossible. They stopped. They sat down in the water. They tried to turn back, and found the cavalry behind them, and the cavalry did not let them turn back. On the third day Silenus heard screaming from behind, a long way behind, and asked a clerk what it was, and the clerk did not answer.

On the third day too the last of the elephants came up past the baggage.

There had been more than thirty when they left Spain. The cold had killed most of them that winter, and the fighting, and now there was one, a big old bull, with a driver on its neck and, behind the driver, sitting very straight with a cloak round him and a bandage across his face, Hannibal. The elephant waded through the flood as if it were nothing at all, in no particular hurry, the water foaming round its legs, and the men in the water stood aside for it and watched it go by. Some of them cheered. It was a strange feeble sound in all that grey.

Silenus looked up as it passed and saw the bandage, and saw the skin round the covered eye swollen and dark. He had heard there was something wrong with the general’s eyes. Everyone’s eyes were wrong, from the wet and the cold and no sleep. He had not known it was that.

Hannibal did not look down. He sat on the elephant’s back looking forward, over the water, with his one good eye, and the elephant took him on up the column and out of sight.

“He won’t stop to have it seen to,” said the clerk beside Silenus, very low, in Greek, which he spoke badly. “The doctor says he must lie in the dark and he says there is no dark here to lie in.”

“Will he lose it?”

The clerk shrugged.

On the fourth day there was dry land.

It came up out of the water in front of them, a long low rise with trees on it, and men who had walked for four days without a word began to run, splashing, falling, getting up, and threw themselves down on the first grass they reached and lay on it. Silenus lay on it with them, on his back, with his arms out, and looked up at the sky, which was clearing, with blue showing in the rents of the cloud. He felt the ground under him not moving. He thought he had never felt anything so good in his life.

He lay there for a long time. When at last he sat up and looked back, the flood stretched away behind them to the mountains, grey and flat and shining, with the dead mules standing up out of it here and there like rocks. And close in under the trees, a little way along the edge of the dry ground, the elephant was standing with its trunk hanging down to the water, quite still, and its driver beside it, and nobody on its back.

Hannibal was sitting under a tree with the doctor kneeling beside him. The bandage was off. Silenus did not go close. He did not need to. He saw the doctor shake his head, and saw Hannibal say something short, and saw the doctor wind the bandage on again over the eye and tie it, and get up, and go away.

Then Hannibal stood up and called for his horse.

That night Silenus wrote a sentence about it. He wrote that the general had lost an eye in the marshes for want of a single day’s rest, and had not taken the day, because there was no day to take. He read it over by the fire and thought it was a good sentence, perhaps the best he had written, and then he thought that it was good because it was true, and then he thought that he did not know if it was true at all, only that it was what had happened. He did not know what Hannibal had decided, sitting under the tree. He knew only what he had seen.

He left the sentence in. In the morning he showed it to Sosylus, who read it twice and said it was too long.
