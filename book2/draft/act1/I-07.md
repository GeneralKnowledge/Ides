# The Well

POV: Laberius · the plain north of Capua · early summer 41

---

The dog had stopped barking at him by the Ides of June. Laberius noticed it one morning coming round the corner of the house for water, and stood still, waiting for it to start, and it lifted its head off its paws and looked at him and put it down again.

He had never been any good with animals. Mules bit him. The horses of the officers he had served under rolled their eyes when he came near, and Gaius used to say it was because Laberius looked at a horse the way a quartermaster looks at a horse, which was true. At home in Picenum, when he was a boy, his father had given him the goat to mind one summer and taken it back in a week. He stood by the well looking at the yellow dog, and the dog went to sleep.

Aulius was at the trough with the bucket. He had seen.

He did not say anything about it. He drew the water and set the bucket on the stone and stood back from it, the way he always did. But when Laberius had drunk and straightened up, Aulius said, looking at the dog, “My father’s still alive. You can’t hold him to it.”

“Hold him to what?”

“What he said. About the dog.” Aulius took the bucket back. “He says a lot of things.”

---

The trouble came up the plain from the north in the middle of the morning, and Laberius heard it before he understood it. He was in the ditch at the bottom of the field, where the dead branch had been, clearing the last of the silt out with a mattock he had borrowed from Aulius and not given back, and the sound came down to him over the beans: men’s voices, a lot of them, and a woman screaming, and under it, steady, a sound he knew better than any other, which was a crowd of soldiers walking together on purpose.

He came up out of the ditch with the mattock still in his hand.

Vibius was already on the headland, shading his eyes. “Up by the vine,” he said. “Up past it. The Marcii’s place.”

The Marcii had the farm beyond the vine plot, two fields up, a big one with a stone house and an orchard and a well with a proper wellhead, the only one on that side of the road. Their land had not been on the surveyor’s list. Laberius knew that because he had walked up the track in May to look, the way he walked every boundary within a mile of the plot, because he could not help it. The surveyor’s stakes stopped at their hedge.

There were twenty or thirty men in the Marcii’s orchard now. They had come down from the colony lots further up the plain, where the land was poorer and the water was further down, and they had come in a body, and they had brought stakes.

Laberius knew some of them. He had served with some of them. He saw a tall man from the old third tent with a bundle of new-cut stakes on his shoulder, and a man he had shared a fire with at Forum Gallorum pulling at the Marcii’s wellhead rope, and the man with the scar through his lip standing at the edge of the orchard with his arms folded, not doing anything, watching.

At the stone house a woman was standing in the door with her arms across it, screaming at them. An old man was trying to pull her inside. In the middle of the orchard, between the soldiers and the house, there was a boy.

He was fifteen or so, thin, burnt dark, with the long arms and big hands of a boy who has not finished growing into them. Laberius had seen him before, though not often. He was Aulius’s eldest. He had been up in the hills with the sheep the day of the twine and most days since, and came down once a week for bread, and looked at Laberius on those days with an expression Laberius knew very well from new recruits, a kind of steady, appalled interest, as though Laberius were an animal that might do something.

He was holding a pitchfork. He was holding it the wrong way, too close to the tines, and he was holding it at the tall man with the stakes.

Laberius started up the track.

He did not decide to. His legs did it, the way they had done it at Munda and in the marsh and on the causeway at Philippi, when the line moved and you moved with it. Vibius said something behind him and then Vibius was beside him, keeping step, swearing under his breath without stopping, as he always did going in.

They came into the orchard by the gap in the Marcii’s hedge, and nobody looked at them. Everyone was looking at the boy.

“Put it down, son,” the tall man was saying. He said it reasonably. He was not angry. He had the bundle of stakes on one shoulder and his other hand out, open, the way you hold your hand out to a horse. “Nobody’s after you. Put it down and go home to your father.”

“It’s not yours,” said the boy. His voice cracked on *yours*. “None of it. You’re not on the list. I heard the surveyor. This is the Marcii’s and they’re not on the list.”

“Then they’ll be on the next one.”

“It’s not *yours*.”

The tall man took a step forward and the boy jabbed at him with the fork, wildly, nowhere near, and the men round the orchard laughed, and the boy’s face went dark red, and he set his feet.

Laberius knew that look. He had seen it on every recruit he had ever had, the first time, in the moment before the line met the other line: the face of a boy who has decided to do the thing and has not yet found out what it is. It was the face that got a boy killed, and usually somebody else with him.

The tall man saw it too. He stopped smiling. He put the bundle of stakes down, slowly, and his hand went to his belt, where every man on that plain still carried a knife.

Laberius stepped between them.

He did not remember doing it afterwards. He remembered being on one side of the orchard and then being in the middle of it, with the boy’s breathing behind him, ragged and very close, and the tall man in front of him, two arms’ lengths off, with his hand on his knife and his face changing as he saw who it was.

“Laberius,” the tall man said.

“Leave it.”

“Leave it? It’s *his* boy.” The tall man jerked his chin past Laberius’s shoulder. “Your farmer’s. The one you’ve left in your house.” The men round the orchard had gone quiet. “What’s it to you? You’ve got yours. The rest of us drew the stony end of the plain and no water, and here’s a well going begging that nobody’s put a number on. Leave it yourself.”

“It’s not on the list.”

“It’ll be on the next list. Everything’s going on the next list. You know that.” The tall man took his hand off the knife and held both hands out, open, as if to show Laberius how reasonable he was being. “Come on, Centurion. We stood on the causeway together. Whose side are you on?”

Behind Laberius the boy moved.

Laberius did not see it. He heard it, a step and a breath, and he knew without looking that the boy had decided, and he turned and caught the shaft of the pitchfork in his left hand a hand’s breadth below the tines, the way he had been taught to take a spear off a man at drill, and twisted it out of the boy’s grip, and the boy went over sideways into the long grass under the trees.

And the tall man, who had seen the fork come forward past Laberius’s shoulder and had not seen anything after that, came in fast with the knife.

Laberius hit him with the pitchfork.

He hit him with the shaft, across the side of the head, the way he would have hit a recruit who was out of line, without thinking, as hard as he could. The tall man went down on his knees in the grass. The knife went somewhere. For a moment nobody in the orchard did anything at all.

Then the tall man put his hand to the side of his head and looked at the blood on it, and looked up at Laberius, and said, quite quietly, “You’ve picked, then.”

Laberius stood with the pitchfork in his hand and did not answer.

---

They did not take the Marcii’s well that day. That was all that could be said for it. The men with the stakes picked up their stakes and went back up the plain in a body, the way they had come, with the tall man walking in the middle of them with a rag held to his head, and none of them looked back except the man with the scar through his lip, who looked back once, at Laberius, for a long moment, with no expression at all.

The boy had got up out of the grass. He stood a little way off, with his arms hanging and grass seeds stuck all down one side of his tunic, staring at Laberius.

Laberius held out the pitchfork. The boy did not take it.

“It’s your father’s,” Laberius said. “Take it home.”

The boy took it. He looked at it as if he had never seen it before. Then he turned and went off down through the orchard toward the track, and he did not run, which Laberius thought well of.

Vibius stood beside him. He was breathing hard and he had a cut on his knuckles that he had not got from anything Laberius had seen.

“Well,” Vibius said. “That’s the colony.”

Laberius said nothing.

“That’s the whole colony,” said Vibius. “You know that. Every man up there will have heard by dark that Laberius put a Second Legion man on his knees for a farmer’s boy. Every one.” He wiped his knuckles on his tunic. “You’ve picked, all right.”

“I picked the boy.”

“That’s what I said.”

They walked back down the track together.
