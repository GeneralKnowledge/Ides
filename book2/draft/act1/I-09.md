The news came into the city in the late afternoon, and by evening there was nowhere in Rome a man could go to get away from it.

Fabius was at home when it came. He was in the small room off the garden where he kept his books and the augurs’ records, working through a dispute about the sighting of a woodpecker that had come up from the college; he had been an augur for forty years and still found such things restful. His freedman came in without knocking, which he never did, and stood in the doorway and said that there were people running in the street.

Fabius put the tablet down and went out to the front of the house, and stood in the doorway with his hand on the post, and listened.

He had lived in Rome for sixty-three years and had learned its sounds the way a farmer learns his land. He knew the sound of a festival, and of a fire, and of a crowd going down to the Forum to hear a speech it wanted to hear. This was none of those. It was the sound of a great many people moving fast in the same direction without knowing why, calling out questions to each other and getting no answers. Under it, coming from the direction of the Forum, there was something else, a long low noise like wind, that he did not at first recognise. Then he did. It was the sound of a crowd weeping.

He went down to the Forum. He went slowly, with his freedman, because he did not walk quickly and saw no reason to start, and by the time he came out at the bottom of the Sacred Way the space in front of the Senate house was full from wall to wall. People were standing on the steps of the temples and on the bases of the statues. Women were there, a great many women, which he had never seen at such a time, standing together in their dark cloaks with their faces turned up towards the Rostra. On the Rostra the praetor was standing with his hands at his sides.

Fabius was too late to hear what he had said. He heard it repeated, all round him, by people who had heard it and could not stop saying it. *We have been beaten in a great battle.* Nothing else. No numbers, no names, no word of the consul. The praetor had said it and stood there, and the crowd was waiting for him to say something more, and he had nothing more to say.

The names came the next day, and the numbers the day after. Flaminius dead, and most of his army, on the shore of the lake, in a fog. Thousands taken. And then, before the city had stopped weeping for that, the news of the four thousand horse the other consul had sent ahead to help, caught on the road by Hannibal’s cavalry and destroyed or taken to the last man. That was the one that frightened the Senate. A defeat was a defeat. Two in a week meant that nobody knew what was happening anywhere, and that the next news might be Hannibal’s horsemen at the gates.

For three days the gates were full of women. They went down every morning and stood by the roads and waited for the men who were coming back, the stragglers and the wounded, and asked each one who passed whether he had seen this man or that. Fabius rode out through the Fontinalis gate on the second day on Senate business and saw them there, sitting on the ground in rows with their backs to the wall, and saw a woman get up and go forward to a soldier coming in, and saw her stop and look at him and put out her hands. He saw her fall. Men gathered round and he rode on. In the evening he heard that she had died there in her son’s arms, of nothing, of seeing him; and that there was another, in another street, who had been told her son was dead and had been sitting in her house in mourning when he walked in at the door, and she had died too.

Fabius thought about those two women a good deal in the months that followed. He did not think of them as a sign. He was an augur and knew what signs were and were not. He thought of them as a measure: that the city was so tightly wound that joy could kill as easily as grief, and that a man who wished to do anything with such a city would have to loosen it first.

They made him dictator on the fourth day.

It was irregular. A dictator was named by a consul, and of the two consuls one was dead and the other was cut off from Rome with Hannibal’s army between them, so the people voted instead, which had never been done. They voted for Fabius because he had been consul twice and censor and was old and slow and nobody could imagine him doing anything rash. He knew this. He accepted it as a fair description. As his master of horse the people gave him Marcus Minucius, who was none of those things and had been chosen, Fabius suspected, by men who wanted somebody near the dictator who would do something.

He went to the Senate the next morning and did not talk about the war. He talked about the gods. He said that Flaminius had gone out of the city without the proper rites, and that the books should be consulted to find out what the gods required, and that it should be done properly, and in public, so that the people could see it done. The Senate, who had expected a plan, looked at him with their mouths open. He let them look.

Afterwards Minucius caught him on the steps. He was a big man of forty or so, with a soldier’s face and a voice that was used to carrying across a parade ground.

“The gods,” he said. “With respect, dictator. Hannibal is in Umbria.”

“I know where he is.”

“Then when do we march?”

“When the city has stopped weeping,” said Fabius. “And when there is an army to march with. The one we had is at the bottom of a lake.”

Minucius looked at him. He seemed to want to say something more, and then not to, and he went off down the steps, and Fabius watched him go.

He went home in the evening the long way, along the river, because he liked to walk by water when he had a decision to make. He had made it already, as it happened. He had made it in the doorway of his house with his hand on the post, listening to the sound of the city. Hannibal had beaten three Roman armies in eight months, every one of them by getting it to fight on his ground, at his time, in his way. Very well. There would be no more fights on his ground. There would be no more fights at all, if Fabius could help it. Hannibal would be followed, and watched, and starved, and kept from the cities and from the harvest, and allowed to wear himself out on Italy like a man trying to eat a field.

It would take years. It would make Fabius the most hated man in Rome. He knew that too, and went home, and ate a little bread and some beans, and went back to the woodpecker.
