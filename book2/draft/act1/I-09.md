# As He Received Her

POV: Fulvia · Rome, the Carinae · summer 41

---

She did not read the tablet until the girl was asleep.

She took Claudia to her own room first, not the girl’s old one, and sat her on the bed, and sent the women out, all of them, even the Greek. She unpinned the girl’s hair herself. It had been done high in the way Fulvia’s own hair had been done the winter of the wedding, two years ago, and the pins were Fulvia’s pins, the ones with the little gold acorns she had sent with her, and Claudia sat quite still while her mother took them out one by one and put them in a row on the chest.

“Did he say anything to you?” Fulvia said. “Himself. Before you came.”

“No.”

“Nothing?”

“He wasn’t there.” Claudia looked at the row of pins. “He went out at the sixth hour. Glycon told me after.”

Fulvia brushed the girl’s hair. It was thick and dark and she had brushed it every night for fifteen years, until two winters ago, and her hand knew the length of it.

“Did he ever say anything to you?”

Claudia thought about it. She was a truthful child. She had always been a truthful child, which Fulvia had found useful and sometimes frightening, and had tried to make her less of, without success.

“He said good morning,” Claudia said. “And when I had a fever in the winter he sent a doctor. His own one. And once he asked me whether my father had had a good voice, because he had heard he had, and I said I didn’t remember.” She paused. “He didn’t eat with me. He didn’t eat with anybody. He was ill a lot.” She paused again. “I left the doll.”

“What doll?”

“Mine. The bone one. I forgot to give it to the Lares when I was married, and I forgot again, and then today there wasn’t time.” She was looking at the pins. “It was in the chest. The woman who packed asked me where it went, and I said the temple. But I didn’t take it.”

Fulvia put the brush down.

She thought about the doll. She remembered it. She had bought it in the Sigillaria the winter Claudia was four, the winter before Clodius was killed, and Clodius had laughed at the price and then sat up half the night with a bodkin and a scrap of red wool sewing it a tunic, badly, because Claudia had asked him to. She had forgotten that until this moment. She had not thought of it in thirteen years.

“It doesn’t matter,” she said. “Lie down.”

Claudia lay down. Fulvia drew the coverlet up over her, and sat on the edge of the bed with her hand on the girl’s shoulder until the breathing changed, which did not take long. The child had always slept easily. It was one of the things that had made Fulvia think she would do well in a hard house.

---

The tablet was on the table in the small room over the court, where the steward had left it. It was sealed with the young Caesar’s ring. The device was a sphinx, which she had heard about and not seen. She looked at it for a while before she broke it.

It was very short. It was written in a clerk’s hand, a good one, and it said that Gaius Julius Caesar, son of the god, sent his greetings to the lady Fulvia, and returned to her house her daughter Claudia, since the marriage contracted between them at Bononia for the sake of concord between their houses had not, in present circumstances, served that purpose; and that the dowry would be repaid in full by the Kalends of October. At the bottom, in a different hand, small and upright, a boy’s hand trying to be a man’s, he had written his name.

That was all. It said nothing that could not have been read aloud in the Senate.

She read it twice. Then she called for the Greek woman.

The Greek woman came and stood by the door with her thick wrists folded in front of her.

“There was something said,” Fulvia said. “With this. Not written.”

“Yes, lady.”

“Say it.”

The Greek woman said it. She said it in the steward’s voice, the young Caesar’s Greek steward, Glycon, she imitated him exactly, the way the women of the household always imitated the men of other households, without kindness. *He asks me to say, as well, in his own words, that he returns her daughter to her as he received her.*

“Those words,” said the Greek woman. “He said it twice.”

Fulvia sat with the tablet in her lap.

She understood it at once, and she knew that every house in Rome would understand it by the morning, because the young Caesar’s steward had said it in an atrium full of slaves and the Greek woman had heard it and Fulvia’s porter had seen the bundle come in at the front gate. *As he received her.* Untouched. Two years married and never a husband. He had taken Fulvia’s daughter as a hostage for his good behaviour, and kept her in his house like a hostage, and sent her home now like a hostage that was no longer required, and he wanted all Rome to know it: that he had never, for one night, been joined to Antony’s house in anything but name.

It was an insult to her. It was an insult to Marcus. It was an insult, she supposed, to the girl asleep in the next room, though she did not think the young Caesar had considered the girl at all.

She had thought of him all winter as a sick boy. She had heard him called it in her own dining room so often that she had stopped hearing it: the sick boy, the boy, the ill boy at Brundisium who might be dead by spring. Marcus called him *the boy* in his letters, the two letters there had been. She had seen him herself perhaps four times, at a distance, at dinners, a thin pale youth with a hand on his chest who did not drink, and had thought, *that one will not last*.

This was not a thing that one did if one did not expect to last.

“Lady?” said the Greek woman.

Fulvia looked up. She had forgotten the woman was there.

“Send for the consul,” she said. “And Manius, if he’s in the house. And tell the cook,” she said, “that the girl will eat with me tonight, in here, and he’s to make the fennel bread.”
