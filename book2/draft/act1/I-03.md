# Explaining

POV: Octavian · Rome, the Forum · spring 41

---

He had been sick in the basin at first light and then again, a little, in the latrine, and by the second hour he had stopped shaking and could hold a cup. Agrippa found him in the small room off the atrium with the cup in both hands and the shutters closed.

“They’re in the Forum,” Agrippa said.

“How many?”

“More than yesterday.” Agrippa had been out to look. He had mud to the ankle and the smell of the street on him. “They’ve got into the temple of Castor. The priests want them out and won’t put them out themselves. There are women on the steps of the Basilica with children. Somebody’s brought a cart of bread and is giving it away, and nobody knows whose bread it is.”

Octavian set the cup down. “Find out.”

“I’ve a man on it.” Agrippa looked at him. “You’re not going down.”

“I’m going down.”

“You look like a corpse that’s been asked to dinner.”

“Then I’ll look like one in the Forum.” He stood up, and the room behaved, more or less. “They’ve come to see whether I’m alive. They had all winter of the rumour that I was dead. If I stay in here, it’s true.”

Agrippa did not argue. He never argued twice. He went to see to the lictors, and Octavian stood in the closed room and pressed the palms of his hands flat on the table until they were still, and then took them off and looked at them, and went out.

---

He heard it before he saw it. Coming down the steps from the house, past the ring-makers’ stalls with their shutters still up, there was a noise from the Forum like a market that had lost its temper. It was not shouting. It was a great many people talking at once to each other, not to anybody, the sound of grievance passed hand to hand.

Then the lictors went down the last step into the open and the noise changed.

He had been looked at by crowds before. He had been looked at by the whole Forum on the day he came to claim the name, a boy of eighteen in thin shoes with three friends, and he had learned then that you could feel a crowd’s attention on your skin like weather. This was different. This crowd had been waiting for him in particular, and it had brought things to show him.

They had papers. Everywhere he looked there were hands holding up tablets and rolls and bits of bronze, the way men hold up a torch. There were women with children on their hips, a great many women, more than he had ever seen in the Forum at once, and some of them had their hair loose as if somebody had died. The temple of Castor had people on every step of its stair, sitting, with their bundles. A man on the base of a statue near the Rostra had a child on his shoulders, and the child was eating an apple and staring at Octavian with complete attention.

“Caesar,” somebody said, near the front. Then a lot of people said it, but not as it was said by soldiers. They said it as you say the name of a man who owes you money.

He went up onto the Rostra because there was nowhere else to go.

From up there it was worse. He could see all of it. He could see the edges, where the crowd thinned into the porticoes and where men stood in twos and threes watching the rest, and he looked for the paid men as he always did, the ones who had been sent by somebody to start a cry at the right moment, and he found a few. Not many. Antony’s people, probably, or Lucius’s, or nobody’s, just men who knew there was a day’s work in a crowd like this. They were not the problem. The problem was everyone else. Everyone else was a farmer.

He knew farmers by now. He had been reading their names all winter, on the lists, in the room at Brundisium with the fever coming and going, Agrippa reading them out and Octavian marking them, town after town, eighteen towns, Capua and Venusia and Beneventum and the rest, and then the towns that were not on the list but would have to be, because eighteen towns were not enough for the number of men who had been promised land at Philippi. He had marked the names with a stylus and not thought of them as people. He had not been able to afford to. Now they were in front of him, and they had faces, and they had brought their children.

He began to explain.

He told them that the army that had avenged Caesar had been promised land, and that the promise had been made in Caesar’s name, and that it had to be kept. He told them that the men who had fought at Philippi had fought for the Republic and for all of them, and that if the soldiers were not paid there would be no Republic, there would be the soldiers. He told them that compensation would be paid from the treasury when the treasury could pay it. He told them he had asked the cities to bear the burden fairly. He told them he was sorry.

He had prepared it in the night, between the bouts of sickness. It was a reasonable speech. He had read it back to himself and found nothing in it that was untrue.

It went out over the crowd and he watched it fail.

He could see it failing. He could see it the way you can see rain cross a field. Faces that had been turned up to him, waiting, began to turn to each other. A woman near the front laughed, once, a short sound with no breath behind it. When he said *compensation* a man at the back said *when?*, not loudly, and it was taken up all across the Forum, *when, when*, until it was not a question any more, it was just a noise the crowd was making. When he said *sorry* there was a silence, and in the silence a man on the steps of the temple of Castor stood up and held a tablet above his head, high, with both hands, and did not say anything at all.

Octavian stopped.

He had another paragraph. It was about the cities sharing the burden, and the Senate, and the gods who had seen Caesar avenged. He looked at the man on the temple steps with the tablet held up and found that he could not say it. Not because it was untrue. Because the man with the tablet already knew it, all of it, every word, and had come anyway, and was standing there with his deed held up to show the young Caesar that knowing it made no difference.

He did not say anything else. He stood on the Rostra and looked at them, and they looked at him, and the child on the man’s shoulders by the statue finished its apple and dropped the core.

After a while he came down.

---

Agrippa walked beside him up the steps to the house, close, closer than a lictor would, so that if Octavian stumbled nobody but Agrippa would see it. He did not stumble.

“The bread,” Agrippa said, at the top. “It’s Lucius’s. Antony’s brother. A baker in the Velabrum was paid to bring it. Two carts yesterday, one today.”

“Of course it is.”

“Do you want it stopped?”

Octavian thought about the man on the temple steps with the tablet over his head. He thought about him going home tonight, wherever home was now, and telling his wife that the young Caesar had come out and made a speech and said he was sorry, and that the consul’s brother had given them bread.

“No,” he said. “Send bread too. More of it. Don’t say whose.”

Agrippa looked at him.

“Don’t say whose,” Octavian said again, and heard how short it came out, and did not take it back.

In the small room off the atrium the cup was where he had left it. He drank what was in it, which was cold now, and sat down, and put his palms flat on the table. There was a list beside the cup, a fresh one that had come up from Campania that morning while he was in the Forum. Teanum. Cales. Venafrum. Some more names.

He took the stylus and began to mark them.
