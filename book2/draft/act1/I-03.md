# The Bait

**I-03** · POV: Maharbal · the Trebia, Carthaginian side, midwinter 218 BC
**Sources:** Pol. 3.72 (snowy, bitterly cold day; the Romans cross cold and unfed): text. Pol. 3.71 (Mago and two thousand hidden in a stream bed; the Numidians sent to draw the Romans over the river) and the Carthaginians fed and oiled by their fires: to check. Maharbal leading the Numidians here is invention inside the record (Liv. 21.45 has him leading Numidians before the Ticinus: to check).

---

Hannibal had chosen the stream bed himself, two days before, riding along the flat ground between the camps with a handful of officers, Maharbal among them.

It was nothing to look at. That was the point of it. A little watercourse that came down off the higher ground and wandered across the plain to the river, dry now except for ice in the bottom, with banks as high as a man and thorn and brambles grown up thick along the tops. You could ride past it at fifty paces and see a hedge. Hannibal had stopped his horse beside it and sat looking down into it for a long time, and Maharbal had looked too, and seen it at the same moment: two thousand men lying in the bottom of that would be invisible until they stood up.

“Romans like open ground,” Hannibal said. “They think anything they can see over is nothing.”

Mago took the two thousand in the night, half of them foot and half horse, the best they had, picked by Mago himself. Maharbal watched them go out of the camp in the dark without torches, leading the horses, and thought about how cold it was going to be in that ditch by morning and was glad he was not going.

He had his own work.

He took the Numidians out before light. A thousand of them, on their small rough horses, riding as they always rode, without saddles or bridles, with a rope round the horse’s neck and their knees and their voices. Maharbal did not ride like that and never would; he had been born in the city, and his father had put him on a horse with a proper bridle at six and sent him to a Greek to learn to read. But he had commanded Numidians for four years now, and he had learned what they could do, and what they would not do, and how to ask.

What they could do was annoy. There was nobody on earth better at it.

They crossed the river at the ford below the Roman camp in the last of the dark, the water to the horses’ bellies, and the cold of it went into Maharbal’s legs like teeth. On the far side they spread out along the bank, and then they rode at the camp. They did not attack it. They rode up to the ditch and along it and threw their javelins over the rampart at anything that moved, and shouted, and laughed. They called out what they would do to the Romans’ mothers, in Numidian, which the Romans did not understand but could not have mistaken. Then they wheeled off and came round again. A Roman sentry put his head up over the stakes to see what was happening and took a javelin in the neck and fell back inside, and the Numidians cheered as if they had taken a city.

Maharbal sat his horse a little back from it, where he could see the gate, and waited.

He had told Hannibal it would take an hour. It took less. The Roman consul, the one who was not wounded, had been wanting a fight for days. Everybody knew it, from the deserters and the Gauls who came in from the villages and the fact that he had marched his army out twice already and looked at them and marched it back. Now the gate opened and his cavalry came out, a great many of them, in good order, and behind the cavalry came the light troops, running.

The Numidians did what they had been told to do. They gave way. They let the Roman horse come at them and broke in front of it, scattered, came together again further back, threw, scattered again, always retreating, always just out of reach, always back towards the river, like a dog playing with a child.

Maharbal rode with them. He could see the Roman cavalry commander’s face once, quite close, a young man with his helmet pushed back and his mouth open, shouting, delighted with himself. He thought: you think you are winning. Then he turned his horse and went down the bank into the river again, with the Numidians all round him and the Romans coming on behind.

On the far side he stopped on the rise of ground above the ford and looked back.

The Roman cavalry had crossed after them, and the light troops after the cavalry, and now behind them, all along the far bank, the legions were coming down to the water. Rank after rank of them. He watched the front rank go in and saw it check, as if it had hit something, and then go on with the ones behind pushing. The water came up to their waists, to their chests. He saw them lift their shields over their heads. The snow was coming sideways now across the river, and he could see it settling on the helmets and the shoulders of the men in the water.

He had not been able to believe it, when Hannibal said it. That they would come out like this, before eating, into a river in midwinter, because a few hundred half-naked horsemen had shouted at their gate. He had been sure there would be an officer with sense enough to say no. There was not.

Behind him, on the flat ground, Hannibal’s army had been up since before the Numidians left. Maharbal had walked through the lines on his way out and seen the fires every few paces, and the men round them eating, slowly, the bread and the hot gruel brought round by the cooks, and rubbing oil into their arms and legs from the jars that went down the lines, laughing at each other because they smelled like Greek wrestlers. Now they were drawn up in their line, and they were warm and fed, and they stood and watched the Romans coming up out of the river towards them, shaking, with their hands too stiff to hold their weapons.

The Numidians came round behind him to re-form. He rode along them, counting heads by habit, and found he had lost eleven, which was nothing. A young rider called Hiempsal had a javelin in his thigh and was laughing at it.

“Now what?” Hiempsal asked.

Maharbal pointed with his chin to the right flank, where the elephants stood, and the heavy horse beside them, Spaniards and Gauls, waiting, and the open ground beyond them where the Roman cavalry would have to come.

“Now we stop running,” he said.

Later, when the Roman centre was through and gone up the road to Placentia and the rest of their army was dying on the riverbank between the elephants and Mago’s men, he rode down to the stream bed to see it. The thorns along the top were trampled flat where two thousand men had come up out of it all at once. In the bottom there was ice, and the marks of men lying close together, and here and there a cloak or a water skin left behind. Mago was sitting on the bank with his helmet off, eating a piece of bread, and his lips were still blue.

“You were right about the cold,” Mago said.

“It worked,” said Maharbal.

Mago looked out at the river, where the bodies were turning slowly in the current, and the snow falling on them, and the Numidians riding along the bank finishing what was left.

“It worked,” he agreed, and went on eating.
