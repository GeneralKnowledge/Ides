# Praeneste

POV: Lucius Antonius · the road to Praeneste, and the town · summer 41

---

He counted them again at the tenth milestone, because the road went straight there and he could see the whole column at once from the back of his horse.

Twelve lictors in front, which he did not count, because they were always twelve. Then the guard. They marched four abreast on the paved road, and he took the rows in tens, as he had taken the rows of vines in Campania three years before with the chain-man calling the numbers back to him, and he made it fifty-three rows and two men over. Two hundred and fourteen. Yesterday in the yard of the house on the Carinae he had made it two hundred and nine, and he did not know whether five men had come in the night or whether he had counted badly in the yard, where they would not stand still.

He decided they had come in the night.

They were Marcus’s men, most of them, from the field by the Esquiline gate: men whose lists had not come, or had come with the wrong name on them, or had come with a plot so far up a hillside that a goat would have asked for compensation. Some were his own, from his tribunate, who had had their land from him in the year Caesar died and had walked in from Campania when they heard the consul wanted a guard. One of those was in the third row from the back, a short bald man with a farmer’s neck, and every time Lucius rode past him the man grinned and touched his forehead, and Lucius could not remember his name, and it was beginning to be too late to ask.

It was the height of summer. The dust came up off the road and lay on the men’s shoulders and in the creases of their faces, and on the fields either side they were threshing, with oxen going round on the floors and the chaff hanging in the air above them like smoke. The people at the floors stopped and watched the column go by. A consul did not often leave Rome in August with two hundred armed men and no war to go to. He saw them wondering what it meant and was glad to be the thing they were wondering about.

Servilius had not come. Lucius had not expected him to. Of the senators who had dined with him on the Carinae through the spring, saying what a disgrace the lists were, three had come, and they were riding behind him now in a group with their own slaves, talking about their villas. One of them had brought a cook.

“They’ll come,” Lucius said, to the officer riding beside him. “When they see that it holds, they’ll come.”

“Yes, consul.”

“It’s a hard thing to ask a man to leave his house in the city on a guess. Give them a month.”

“Yes, consul.”

He was a young man, the officer, Lucius’s own appointment, the son of a friend. He had been saying “Yes, consul” since the Esquiline gate.

---

It had been agreed at dinner, four nights ago. He went over it on the road, because he liked going over it.

They had been at dinner with Manius, and Fulvia had had the news from the theatre, about the soldier in the knights’ seats and the centurion of the young Caesar’s who had been found in the river afterwards. She had told it well. She told everything well, quickly, with the names in the right places, and she had ended it by putting down her cup and saying, “That is a city where a man’s own soldiers drown his centurions for him. What do you suppose they do to a consul?”

And Lucius had said, he remembered it exactly, that a consul of the Roman people had his lictors and the protection of the law, and that he was not going to be frightened out of Rome by a sick boy and a mob in a theatre.

“Of course not,” Fulvia had said. “Nobody would frighten you.”

Then she had talked to Manius about the price of wheat. Then, later, while the slaves were taking the tables away, she had said, to nobody in particular, that the air at Praeneste was very good in summer, and that her mother had had friends there, and that the town had been loyal to Marius, and had stood a siege for his son, and that the people there had long memories and did not like the men who gave land to soldiers.

And it had come to him, lying there on the couch with the lamps being trimmed, that a consul who withdrew to a strong town in the hills with a guard of his own was not running from anything. He was choosing his ground. He had said so. He had sat up on the couch and said so, and Manius had looked at Fulvia and then nodded slowly, as though Lucius had said something clever, and Fulvia had said, “If you think so.”

He thought so. He was still thinking so on the road.

---

Praeneste stood on its mountain like a thing that had been piled up rather than built, the town at the top and the great temple of Fortune coming down the hill below it in terraces, one on top of another, ramps and stairs and colonnades, so that from the plain the whole slope looked dressed in stone. They saw it from fifteen miles off. He watched it get bigger all afternoon.

The magistrates met him at the bottom of the temple stairs, two men in borders with the town council behind them, and made a speech about the honour. The older of the two had a voice like a creaking door and said “most illustrious” four times. They had found him a house below the citadel, they said, belonging to a man who was at the coast for the summer. They said nothing about the two hundred and fourteen men behind him, and their eyes kept going to them.

He went up the stairs of the temple before he went to the house. He wanted to see what he had chosen.

From the top terrace he could see everything. The plain going away west in the heat, yellow and brown, with the threshing floors on it like coins. The line of the road he had come by. Far off, where the plain went blue, a smudge that the creaking magistrate told him, pointing, was the city. He stood with his hands on the warm stone of the balustrade and looked at it, and the magistrate stood beside him not saying anything, and then said, as though it had just come to him, that the lots of Fortune could be drawn for him in the morning, if the consul wished, by the boy who drew them, from the chest of olive-wood.

“What do they say?”

“It depends what you ask, consul.”

“I’m not going to ask anything,” Lucius said. “I know what I’m doing.”

He paced the terrace on the way down, from one end to the other, because he could not see a long level surface without pacing it. It was two hundred and forty paces, near enough. He would have liked to tell somebody, and there was nobody to tell but the magistrate, who would not have known what to do with it.

---

Fulvia came two days later.

He heard her before he saw her, because the guard at the gate sent a man up running, and then a second man, and he went down into the street to see. She came up the steep road into the town in a litter with the boys, and behind her another litter with her women, and behind that a line of carts and mules with the household on them, chests and bedding and the boys’ tutor sitting on a roll of carpets, and the cook, and the steward with the keys, and behind all that, on horseback, eleven senators and a crowd of knights with their slaves, raising more dust than the guard had.

Eleven. He counted them twice. Four of them he had asked in the spring, and they had not come for him.

She got down in the street in front of the house without waiting for anyone to hand her down. The magistrates had arrived, out of breath, and she gave them her hand one after the other and thanked them for receiving her, and the creaking one began on “most illustrious” and she let him reach the second.

“I am grateful to the people of Praeneste,” she said. Her voice was not loud. It carried anyway. “I could not stay in Rome. I am afraid for my children there.” She put her hand on Antyllus’s head, and the boy stood very still under it. “The young Caesar has soldiers in the streets who murder their own officers. I am the wife of Marcus Antonius, and these are his sons. I came to the consul, and to you.”

The magistrates bowed. Somebody behind them said something, and the townspeople who had come out into the street looked at the boys and then at each other, and Lucius saw it go round, the way the cheering had gone out into the Forum from the steps of Saturn. Antonius’s wife. Antonius’s sons. Afraid in Rome.

It was true. He believed it was true. He had seen her with the boys. And it was also the best thing that could have been said in that street, to those people, and she had said it before she had shaken the dust off.

---

She took the upper rooms of the house, which had the view, and the steward had the household in by dark. Lucius ate with the senators in the lower dining room, all eleven, and the cook from Rome sent up the fennel bread, and the men who had not come in the spring were very warm to him and very interested in the guard.

He went up to her afterwards. She was at the window with the lamp behind her, looking out at nothing, because there was nothing out there but the dark of the plain and one or two fires.

“Two hundred and fourteen,” he said. “At the gate this morning. And more on the way from Campania, they tell me. Men I settled.”

“Good.”

“And eleven of the Senate. You saw them. Eleven, Fulvia. They didn’t come for a guess.”

“No.”

“It’s holding,” he said. “I told you. Choose your ground and let them come.”

She turned round. She looked at him for a moment as though there were something she might say, and then she seemed to decide that there was not.

“Who pays the two hundred and fourteen?” she said.

“I do.”

“From what?”

He had an answer to that. He had had one on the road. He began to give it to her, the consul’s right to the public revenues of Italy and the money owed his brother by the cities of the coast, and she listened to the first part and then said, “Tomorrow, then. Manius will be here tomorrow. You can tell him,” and went back to the window.

He went down. In the lower room the senators were still at the wine, and one of them called him over to settle a dispute about the height of the temple, and he said he would pace it in the morning and tell them, and they laughed and made room for him on the couch.
