# The Dream

**I-14** · POV: Silenus · Hannibal's winter camp at Gerunium, Apulia, early 216 BC
**Sources:** Cic. *Div.* 1.49 (Silenus's story of Hannibal's dream: called by Jupiter to the council of the gods, given a guide, told not to look back; behind him a vast serpent laying everything waste; the voice says it is the devastation of Italy): text. Nepos, *Hann.* 13.3 (Silenus and Sosylus in Hannibal's camp): text. Wintering at Gerunium (Pol. 3.100–101): to check.

That winter the army ate its way through Apulia and Silenus wrote down the dream.

He had heard it first in Spain, years before, in the form the soldiers told it, and had not believed it. He heard it again at Gerunium in a form he did believe, which was not the same thing as believing it was true. He heard it from Hannibal.

It was at dinner. Hannibal did not keep a table in the way the Greek kings did, with courses and musicians and philosophers to argue for their supper. He ate what the soldiers ate, sitting on a stool in the big tent with whichever of his officers were in camp, and he ate it quickly. But in the winter the evenings were long, and when the eating was over he would sometimes let the talk go on, and sometimes he would talk himself. It was the only time Silenus ever heard him talk about anything but the war, and even then it usually came back to the war.

That night it had been raining for six days. The tent leaked at one corner and a soldier had put a helmet under the drip, and the drip went into the helmet all through the meal like a slow water clock. They were eating mutton. It was the last of a flock they had taken in the autumn, and everyone at the table knew it was the last, and nobody said so. The foragers were going out further every day now, ten miles, fifteen, and coming back with less. The Romans followed them on the high ground. The old dictator’s six months had run out and the consuls had their army back, but the Roman army still followed the foragers and cut them off when it could, and did not fight.

Somebody, Mago perhaps, asked Sosylus to tell them something about Sparta. Sosylus told a story about a king who had been sent a dream before a battle, and had read it wrongly, and lost. It was a good story and he told it badly, as he told all stories, giving away the end in the middle, and when he had finished there was a pause. Then Hannibal said, “I was sent a dream once.”

The table went quiet. The drip went into the helmet.

He told it simply, the way he gave orders, without decoration. It had been after Saguntum, the winter before they marched. He had dreamed that he was called up into the council of the gods, and that Jupiter himself had told him to carry the war into Italy, and had given him a guide from among the gods to lead him. They had set out, Hannibal and the guide, and the guide had told him one thing: not to look back. He had gone on for a long way without looking. Then he had heard a noise behind him, a great noise, of trees falling and earth breaking, and in the dream he could not help it; he had looked round.

“And there was a serpent,” Hannibal said. “Enormous. Bigger than anything. It was coming along behind me, and everything it passed over was destroyed, the trees and the houses and the fields, all crushed flat. And behind it there was a storm, with thunder. I asked the guide what it was. He said that it was the laying waste of Italy, and that I was to go on and not ask any more, and let the rest be hidden.”

He took a piece of bread and began to wipe his plate with it.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then one of the younger officers laughed, a little uncertainly, and said that the gods had sent it the right way round, at least; they had put the serpent behind him and not in front. Hannibal smiled at him with the side of his face that still had an eye and said yes, that was the right way round. The talk moved on. Mago asked the Numidian commander about the horses.

Silenus sat with his cup and did not drink from it.

He had heard the soldiers’ version a dozen times. In the soldiers’ version the serpent was Hannibal’s army, and it would eat Rome, and it was a promise. He saw now that it was not quite that, as Hannibal told it. In Hannibal’s version the serpent was the war itself, and the guide did not say who would win. He said only that it would be terrible and that Hannibal was not to look at it. He wondered whether Hannibal had changed it, over the years, or the soldiers had, or whether he had heard it wrong in Spain. He wondered whether Hannibal had told it tonight because he believed it, or because he knew that a Greek historian was at the table and would write it down, or because it was raining and the mutton was the last.

He would never know. That was the rule he had made for himself, after the marsh: he would write what he saw and what he heard, and he would not write what Hannibal thought, because he did not know it, and a historian who pretended to know the thoughts of kings was only writing a play.

He wrote it down that night in his own tent, by a lamp that guttered in the damp. He wrote it as Hannibal had told it, as nearly as he could remember the words, the council and the guide and the order not to look back and the serpent and the storm. At the end he wrote what the guide had said, that the rest should be hidden. Then he sat for a long time looking at the tablet.

Sosylus came in, shaking the rain off his cloak, and read it over his shoulder.

“He told it better than that,” Sosylus said.

“He told it shorter.”

“That’s what I said.” Sosylus sat down on the other stool and began to unlace his boots. “You know he told it for you.”

“Perhaps.”

“He knows you will put it in the book. And the book will be read in Syracuse and Athens and Alexandria, and they will say that the gods sent Hannibal to Italy, and some of them will believe it.” He got one boot off and held it up to the lamp and looked at the sole, which had come away from the upper at the toe. “And some of the people who believe it will be the people deciding whether to send him ships.”

Silenus thought about that.

“It doesn’t make it untrue,” he said.

“No,” Sosylus agreed. “It just makes it useful.” He put the boot down. “There are going to be a great many things like that, I think, before this is over. You had better decide now what you will do about them.”

Silenus did not answer. He looked at the tablet, at the serpent coming on behind the man who was not to look back, and he left it as it was. Outside the rain went on, and somewhere at the edge of the camp the foragers were coming in late with their mules, and he could hear from the sound of the hooves on the mud that the loads were light.
