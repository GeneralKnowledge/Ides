# Thanks

**I-14b** · POV: Hanno · Carthage, winter 217–216 BC
**Sources:** none for this meeting; invention inside the record. Trasimene and the loss of the Roman cavalry under Centenius (Pol. 3.84; Liv. 22.8): text. Hannibal freeing the Italian prisoners (Pol. 3.85): to check.

---

The second letter from Italy came in the winter, overland through Spain again, and it was longer than the first, and it said Trasimene.

Hanno read it at home before the senate met. He had friends among the clerks, as a man in his position always had, and one of them brought him a copy wrapped in a fish basket a few hours after the original came up from the harbour. He read it at his table with his breakfast going cold. A consul killed. An army killed. Six thousand taken on a hill. A second Roman force of horse cut to pieces a few days later. The Romans had made a dictator. The Gauls were firm, the Italians were wavering, Hannibal had freed every Italian prisoner without ransom and sent them home to tell their towns. And then, near the end, the same sentence as last time, asking the senate to remember his men; except that this time there were two sentences, and the second one said what remembering should mean. It asked for money and soldiers, and a fleet to keep the coast open.

He read it twice. Then he put it down and finished his bread.

He found he was not surprised. He had not expected Trasimene, exactly; nobody could have expected a whole consular army drowned in a lake. But he had expected a victory, sooner or later, and a big one, because he had known Hamilcar, and knew what Hamilcar’s sons had been trained to do. And he had expected the second sentence, because he had known Hamilcar, and knew that Hamilcar had always won his battles and always asked for money afterwards.

The senate met at noon and voted thanks.

They did it at greater length this time, with speeches. Himilco spoke for an hour, and two of the Barcid men from Spain, and an old admiral who had served under Hamilcar in Sicily in the last war and wept openly about the debt that was being paid. They voted a sacrifice to Baal Hammon and another to Tanit, and a golden shield to be hung in the temple with Hannibal’s name on it. They voted a public dinner. They voted that the letter should be read aloud in the square outside the senate house so that the people could hear it, and a clerk went out and read it, and Hanno heard the roar come in through the doors.

Then the motion was put for the second sentence, and it went more slowly.

It did not fail. That would have been impossible, after the roar. It went to a committee. A committee of seven was appointed to consider what Hannibal needed and how it might be sent and what it would cost and where the money might be found, and to report to the senate in the spring, when the sailing season opened. Hanno was not on the committee. He had not asked to be. He sat in his place and watched the seven names being chosen, and knew five of the seven, and knew that four of the five would rather spend the city’s money in Spain than in Italy, where their own estates were nowhere near.

He said nothing. He had nothing to say. It had all happened exactly as he would have arranged it if he had been arranging it, and he had not lifted a finger.

At the door afterwards Himilco caught his arm.

“No questions today?” Himilco said. He was flushed and pleased with himself and smelled of the incense from the sacrifice.

“None,” said Hanno. “It was a great victory.”

Himilco looked at him with suspicion.

“You voted for the thanks.”

“Of course I voted for the thanks. I would vote him a golden statue if he asked for one; it would cost less than what he is asking for.” He took his arm out of Himilco’s hand, gently. “I notice you voted for the committee.”

Himilco opened his mouth, and shut it again, and went off down the steps.

Hanno stood at the top of the steps for a while, leaning on his stick, and looked out across the square. They had set up tables for the public dinner already and slaves were carrying out jars of wine. The crowd was thick and noisy and happy. He could see from where he stood, over the roofs, the masts in the inner harbour, the round harbour where the war fleet lay. There were a great many of them, sixty or seventy ships, sitting at their moorings with their masts down and their crews ashore for the winter. In the spring they would go out to Sardinia, probably, or to Spain, or to sit off Sicily and watch the Romans. They would not go to Italy. Nobody would say so. The committee would see to it, in the most patriotic way, with the greatest regret, for the best of reasons.

He thought about the boy he had argued against all those years ago, standing up to his knees in the snow and the marshes and the blood with his one eye, writing home for money. He found, to his considerable surprise, that he was sorry for him.

He went home and did not go to the dinner.
