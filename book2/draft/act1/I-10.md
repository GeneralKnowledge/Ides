# The Fourteen Rows

POV: Octavian · Rome, the theatre and the river bank · summer 41

---

The man in the fourteen rows was a soldier, and Octavian saw him before anybody else did, because he was looking for something to look at that was not the stage.

The play was a Greek comedy put into Latin by somebody who had not understood the jokes, and the actors were shouting it to make up. Octavian had come because the aedile had asked him and because he had been told, by Maecenas, in that soft voice Maecenas used for things he had decided, that the people should see him sitting down for two hours in a public place without being sick. So he sat. He sat in the place kept for him at the front, in the cool, with a cushion, and Agrippa on one side and Maecenas on the other, and the whole of the theatre behind him, and the back of his neck could feel every one of them.

The fourteen rows behind the orchestra were for the knights. The law said so; the law had said so for a hundred years. Men in narrow-striped tunics and gold rings sat in them with their sons, and fanned themselves, and talked through the play about the price of land.

The soldier sat among them in a plain tunic and boots, with his arms folded, watching the stage.

He was not drunk. He was not doing anything. He was sitting very straight in a knight’s seat, the eleventh row, near the end, and the knights on either side of him had moved along a little, so that there was a gap round him on the bench like a gap round a dropped dish. He did not look at them. He had the face of a man who has been waiting a long time for something and has decided to sit down while he waits.

Octavian watched him for a while. He watched the knights watch him. He watched the knights look, one after another, toward the front of the theatre, toward the place where Octavian sat, and look away.

“Have him moved,” he said to the attendant behind his chair.

He said it quietly. He thought afterwards that it was the quietest order he had given all year.

---

The attendant went up the side stair with two of the theatre’s slaves. The soldier saw them coming. He did not argue. He looked at them, and at the knights on either side, and at the front of the theatre where Octavian was sitting, and then he stood up and went with them, down the side stair and out under the arch, and the knights moved back along the bench to fill the gap.

That was all. The actors had not stopped. It took perhaps forty breaths.

But up in the high seats, the cheap seats, where the soldiers sat, there was a stir.

Octavian felt it before he heard it, the way you feel a change in the wind on the back of your neck. A man stood up in the top rows, and then another, and somebody shouted something that was lost in the actors’ shouting, and then somebody else shouted it louder, and this time it was not lost.

*They’ve taken him out to kill him.*

He turned in his seat. Agrippa had already turned. Up in the top rows of the theatre, all along the curve, men were standing, two hundred, three hundred, more, and they were all looking down at the front, at him.

“He’s been moved to another seat,” Octavian said to Agrippa. “Tell them.”

“Tell three hundred of them?”

“Tell them.”

But it was too late for that. The men in the top rows were coming down. They were not running. They came down the stairs between the benches in a body, steadily, the way soldiers come down a hill toward a line, and the people in the middle seats got up out of their way, and the actors on the stage stopped at last and stood with their masks in their hands, and the theatre went quiet except for the sound of hobnails on the stone steps.

They stopped at the barrier above the orchestra and stood there, looking down at him.

He had never been so close to so many of them. At Philippi he had been in a litter, and then in a marsh, and then in a tent. He had seen the legions from a horse, in ranks, with their officers in front. He had never stood in front of three hundred of them with nothing between them and him but a wooden rail and twelve lictors with bundles of sticks.

He stood up. He made himself stand up.

“Where is he?” a voice said. A big man, near the front, with a grey beard and no front teeth. “The man you had taken out. Where is he?”

“He was moved to another seat,” Octavian said. His voice came out thin. He hated it. “Those rows belong to the knights. By law.”

“By law,” said the big man. He said it the way you repeat a word in a foreign language. “Whose law? He was at Philippi. He was at Mutina. He’s been in more fights than any man in those rows has been to dinners, and he sat down in a seat. And you had him taken out to have his throat cut.”

“He is not hurt.”

“Then show us.”

They found him. It took a long time, longer than Octavian would have believed, standing in front of the rail with three hundred soldiers looking at him and the whole theatre looking at the soldiers. Agrippa went out under the arch himself, and came back after an age with the soldier from the eleventh row, who was perfectly well, and very puzzled, and had a cup of wine in his hand that somebody had given him in the street outside.

The soldiers looked at him. Somebody laughed. The big man with no teeth did not laugh.

“He says they didn’t hurt him,” the big man said. “He would. He’s frightened.”

“I’m not,” said the soldier from the eleventh row, but nobody listened to him.

---

They went back up to the top rows in the end. Not because they were satisfied. Octavian could see they were not satisfied. They went back because they had come down to the rail and stood there and nothing had happened, and there was nothing more to do, and the actors had put their masks back on.

The play went on. Octavian sat through the rest of it. He did not see any of it.

On the way out, at the arch, a centurion of his own guard was waiting with his face very white. He was one of the old ones, a hard grey man who had served under the god in Gaul, and he had gone up into the top seats while the soldiers were at the rail and told some of them, loudly, what he thought of men who came down on their general in a theatre like a mob of fullers. Octavian had not seen it. He was told it now, at the arch, by the centurion himself, who seemed to think he should know.

“Go home,” Octavian said. “Go home and stay in tonight.”

The centurion did not go home. Or he went, and came out again. In the morning he was in the Tiber, below the Aemilian bridge, with his throat cut and his tunic gone, and the men who pulled him out said that soldiers had done it, and nobody knew which soldiers, and nobody was going to.

---

Octavian heard it at the second hour, in the small room off the atrium, from Agrippa.

He sat for some time without saying anything. He had his palms flat on the table. They were not shaking, which surprised him. He found that he was very calm, in the way he had been calm in the marsh at Philippi, lying in the reeds with the water to his chest: a calm that had nothing to do with courage.

“They’ll want a hanging,” Agrippa said. “The knights will. The Senate will.”

“Who would we hang?”

Agrippa did not answer.

“Find out what they’re owed,” Octavian said. “All of them. The ones in Rome. Every man who’s still waiting for his land. What was promised, what’s been paid, what’s still owing. I want it by tonight.”

“And then?”

“And then we pay it.” He took his hands off the table. “Before they ask. Before anyone asks. Before Lucius tells them he’ll pay it.”

Agrippa looked at him for a long moment, and Octavian looked back, and neither of them said what they both knew, which was that it would be paid with money that should have gone to the farmers on the treasury steps, because there was no other money.

“I’ll see to it,” said Agrippa, and went.

Octavian sat in the small room. Outside, in the street below the house, the people with their deeds were already sitting along the wall in the morning sun. He could hear one of them, a woman, telling a child to stop kicking the step.
