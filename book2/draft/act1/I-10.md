# The Senate House at Capua

**I-10** · POV: Pacuvius Calavius · Capua, spring 216 BC
**Sources:** Liv. 23.2 (Pacuvius Calavius, noble and popular; his children by a daughter of Ap. Claudius; his daughter married to M. Livius at Rome): text. Liv. 23.2–4 (he shuts the senators in their house, puts each name to the people for a replacement, and when they cannot agree saves the whole senate, which is then his): text. **When** this happened: Livy tells it as Capua's state when Hannibal came after Cannae; whether the shutting-in was before Cannae, as here, is to check.

---

The people of Capua had been talking about killing the senate for a month, and Pacuvius Calavius was the only man in the city who had decided to do something about it before they did.

He had heard it first in the baths. That was where Capua talked. The Romans did their talking in the Forum, standing up, in togas, as if they were at a funeral; the Campanians did it lying down, in hot water, with oil on them, and said what they thought. What they thought that spring was that the senate was a nest of Roman lovers. The senators had married their daughters to Romans and sent their sons to be educated in Rome and taken Roman money for Roman contracts, while the rest of Capua paid Roman taxes and sent its young men to die in Roman armies on Roman wars. The Romans had lost three of those wars in a row now, and Hannibal had walked across the Falernian land the autumn before, under the walls, burning the farms of men whose grandfathers had been Samnites. When he came back, as he would, there should be no senate in Capua to stop the city doing what it pleased.

Pacuvius had lain in the hot room with his eyes shut and listened to all of it. He agreed with most of it. He was one of the Roman lovers it was about.

That was the joke of his life, and he enjoyed it more than anyone. He was a noble of Capua with the oldest name in the city. He had a wife who was a Claudia, a daughter of Appius Claudius, of the family that had built the Appian road, and she had given him sons. He had given his own daughter in marriage to Marcus Livius at Rome, a man of consular family. He went to Rome every other year and dined with consuls. And he was the most popular man in Capua, with the people in the baths and the markets and the street of the perfume sellers, because he had always given them games and grain and his time, and laughed at their jokes, and remembered their names. He was the only man in the city who could walk into both houses, the senate and the street, and be welcome in each.

He understood what was coming better than either of them. That was why he moved first.

He called the senate together on a morning in early spring and told them, in a quiet voice, with the doors shut, that they were going to be murdered. He did not dress it up. He told them what was being said in the baths, and by whom, and how many, and what weapons there were in the city, and how long it would take a crowd to come up the street from the market. He watched them understand him. Some of them went white. Some of them were angry and began to talk about the garrison and calling on Rome. He let them talk until they had finished.

Then he said he could save them, if they would do exactly what he told them.

They did. He went out of the senate house and had the doors locked behind him, with the whole senate of Capua inside it, and put men of his own on the doors. Then he called the people to the square in front of it.

They came, a great crowd of them. He had sent word round the baths and the markets that something was going to happen, and in Capua that was always enough. He stood on the steps of the senate house with the locked doors behind him and told them that the senate was in there. He had shut them in himself. They were at the mercy of the people of Capua, and the people of Capua could do as they liked with them.

The crowd roared. He waited.

“But think,” he said, when they were quiet enough to hear him, “before you do it. A city needs a senate. Kill this one, and you’ll need another by tomorrow, or the Romans will give you one. So let us do it properly. I will call out the senators’ names, one at a time, and you will tell me whether he is to die. And if he is, you will tell me who is to have his place. A better man, an honest man, one of yourselves. Then we will put them in one by one, and Capua will have the senate it deserves.”

They loved it. He had known they would. He called out the first name, which was the name of a man he knew they all hated, a moneylender with a squint and a reputation, and they howled for his death. Then he asked them who should have his seat.

There was a pause. Then someone called out a name. Others laughed, and shouted it down; that man was a drunk. Someone called out another. That man was a cheat, people shouted, and worse than the moneylender. A third name: too poor, too young, nobody had ever heard of him. Men began to argue with each other. The howl broke up into fifty quarrels.

Pacuvius stood on the steps and waited, with an expression of great patience.

He called out the second name. The same thing happened. And the third. By the fifth name the crowd had stopped howling for anyone’s death and had begun to laugh at itself. At the end of the afternoon a fishmonger at the front shouted that since there wasn’t an honest man in Capua to be found, they had better keep the thieves they had, who at least knew the work. Everyone laughed, and Pacuvius laughed with them, and said he thought the fishmonger was very wise.

He let the senators out at sunset.

They came out into the empty square blinking, in their good cloaks, old men most of them. One by one they came to him and took his hand, and some of them could not speak, and one of them kissed it. He was gracious to all of them. He told each of them, quietly, that they owed him nothing. And he saw each of them understand that from that day on they owed him everything, and that he could open the doors again whenever he liked.

He walked home through the city in the dusk feeling very well. At the corner of his street there was a shrine to the Lares with a lamp lit in it, and he stopped, as he always did, and touched the stone, and thought, for no reason he could name, of his son. The boy was seventeen and hated Rome with the clean, uncomplicated hatred of the young. He had asked his father at dinner a few nights ago why Capua did not simply open its gates to Hannibal and be done with it.

Pacuvius had told him that it was more complicated than that. He thought now, with the senate in his hand and the whole of Campania waiting for summer, that it was not. It was only a question of when, and of who would be holding the doors.
