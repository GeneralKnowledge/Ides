# The Treasury Steps

POV: Lucius Antonius · Rome, the temple of Saturn · spring 41

---

The quaestor had a cold and kept wiping his nose on the back of his wrist, and Lucius found it hard to look at anything else.

“There is nothing in the chest for it, consul,” the quaestor said. “I don’t say there won’t be. I say there isn’t. There is the grain money, which is spoken for. There is the money for the fleet, which is spoken for twice. There is a sum set aside for the games of Apollo, which the praetor will murder me for if I touch.” He wiped his nose. “And there is a decree of the triumvirs, which I have read, which says that compensation for the lands of the eighteen cities shall be paid from the public treasury. It does not say when, or how much, or out of what.”

“Then who decides?”

The quaestor looked at him with the patience of a man who has been asked the same question by a great many people that week and has learned to admire it as a question.

“You are the consul,” he said.

They were standing in the doorway of the treasury, under the columns of Saturn’s temple, with the cold dark of the strongrooms behind them and the Forum in front. Lucius’s co-consul had been with him when they came out of the Senate and had gone home. Servilius always went home. He was a sensible man with a sensible house and he had been consul before, under Caesar, and he had told Lucius at the door of the Senate, quite kindly, that the young Caesar had the lists and the soldiers had the swords and a consul in this year had a chair and twelve men with sticks, and that a sensible consul would sit in the chair.

Lucius had not gone home. He had come to the treasury because the farmers were at the treasury. They had been on the steps of Saturn every morning for a month, because somebody had told them that this was where compensation came from, and they were there now, below him, sixty or seventy of them, men in country cloaks with their deeds in their hands, looking up at the consul in the doorway.

“Thank you,” Lucius said to the quaestor. “Get to bed. You sound like a dying goose.”

He went down the steps.

---

He had done this before. That was the thing nobody in the Senate seemed to remember, or wanted to. Three years ago, the year Caesar died, he had been tribune of the plebs, and he had carried a law to give land to Caesar’s veterans and the poor of the city, and he had sat on the board of seven that gave it out. Cicero had made jokes about it in the Senate. Cicero had called him a surveyor with a gladiator’s training. But Lucius had walked the fields in Campania that spring with the chain and the groma, and stood beside men while they were shown their plots, and he had seen what it did to a man’s face to be told *this is yours*.

He had not, then, had to see the other face. The land had been public land. Nobody had been on it but the state’s tenants and some sheep.

The farmers made room for him on the steps. They did it without being asked. They knew who he was; somebody always knew. A man in front, grey-bearded, with a stick, said “Consul” and nothing else, and waited.

“Where are you from?” Lucius said.

“Cremona.” The man said it as if it were the name of a person who had died. “In the north. On the Padus. Twenty days on foot.”

“You walked here?”

“I walked here.” The man held out a tablet. “Forty years on it. My father before. Now it’s a soldier’s, and there’s a centurion in my house with his woman, and my wife is at her sister’s in Patavium, and I’m on the steps of Saturn waiting for the treasury to buy me a new life. They say it’s in here.” He nodded at the dark doorway behind Lucius. “Is it?”

Lucius thought about the quaestor wiping his nose.

There were things he could have said. He could have said that the treasury was empty and would be empty until the eastern taxes came in, and that the eastern taxes were being collected by his brother, who was spending them in the East. He could have said that the decree had been signed by three men in a tent, and that one of those men was his brother, and that it had been his brother who had promised the legions their land at Philippi, in a speech Lucius had heard about from a dozen officers since, a good speech, one of Marcus’s best. He could have said that he did not know what Marcus wanted done.

He looked at the old man from Cremona, and at the sixty or seventy faces behind him on the steps, all turned up to him, waiting. He felt the thing he always felt in front of a crowd, and had since he was a boy, the thing that made Marcus laugh at him: as though a door had opened in his chest and the wind had come in.

“It isn’t in there,” he said. “Not yet.”

He heard them take it. A sound went along the steps like a breath going out.

“But I am the consul of the Roman people,” Lucius said, and he raised his voice now, so that it went out past the steps into the Forum, where people had begun to stop and look. “And the land of Italy is not the property of three men. It belongs to the people of Italy, and to the Senate and people of Rome. I say that no man should be put off his land until he has been paid for it. I say it here, on the steps of the treasury, where you can hold me to it.”

They were quiet for a moment. Then the old man from Cremona said “Consul,” again, differently, and somebody at the back began to cheer, and the rest took it up.

It went out into the Forum. He watched it go. Men at the foot of the steps turned and told other men, and those men stopped and looked up, and the cheering got louder, and somebody in the crowd shouted *Antonius*, and somebody else shouted *Lucius*, and for a moment he did not know which of them he was hearing, and did not care.

---

He wrote to Marcus that night.

He wrote it himself, without his secretary, sitting up on the couch in his own room with the lamp on the stand beside him and his knees for a desk. He wrote that the young Caesar was giving out Marcus’s veterans’ land under his own seal and taking Marcus’s credit for it. He wrote that the farmers of Italy were in the Forum every day and that the young Caesar had nothing to give them but speeches and bread. He wrote that he had told them, as consul, that no man should lose his land unpaid, and that the Forum had cheered him, and that he believed the whole of Italy would follow if Marcus would only say the word.

Then he stopped.

He read it back. It was a good letter. It was full of things Marcus would want to know, and of things Marcus would be glad to hear, and he could see Marcus reading it, in Ephesus or wherever he was now, with his feet up and a cup in his hand and that look on his face that Lucius had been trying to put there since they were boys.

What it did not say, anywhere, was *Is this what you want?*

He held the stylus over the wax for a long time. He thought about adding it at the end, plainly, as a question, the way he would have asked it across a dinner table. *Brother, is this what you want me to do?* He thought about Marcus reading that, and laughing, and handing it to whoever was beside him on the couch.

He did not add it. He wrote *Your brother, who holds Italy for you*, and sealed it, and gave it to the slave at the door for the courier at first light.

Then he lay back on the couch with his hands behind his head and listened to the city, which was quiet, and found that he was still smiling from the steps of Saturn, and could not stop.
