# Security

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Janus · spring 41

---

The first allotment tablet came across the board on a wet morning in May, and Hordeonius did not touch it.

It was bronze, a thin plate the size of a man’s palm, with the triumvirs’ seal stamped in the corner and the lettering cut by a man who had cut so many of them that he no longer looked at what he cut. A name. A legion. A town, Venusia. A number of iugera. No date.

The soldier who had put it down was young, under thirty, with a burn scar up one forearm and very clean nails. He had come to the stall at the opening of the day, when the arcade under the Janus was still half dark and the moneychangers along the row were taking the covers off their scales, and he had waited politely for the man ahead of him to finish, and then he had laid the bronze plate on the board between them and said, “What’ll you give me on that?”

Hordeonius looked at it. He did not pick it up. He had a rule about that, which he had made thirty years ago and broken twice, both times to his cost. A thing you have picked up is a thing you have begun to buy.

“What is it?” he said, although he knew.

“My land. At Venusia. Forty iugera, good ground, the surveyor said.” The soldier’s voice was easy. He had been told what to say. “I’ll be going down there in the autumn. I need something to live on till then.”

“And you want me to lend you something to live on, against this.”

“That’s what the others do.”

“Which others?”

The soldier glanced along the arcade, down toward the end of the row where the light came in from the Forum. Hordeonius did not need to look. He had heard the stool scrape on the stones that morning before he had his own shutter down.

“Then why have you come to me?” Hordeonius said.

The soldier was quiet for a moment. Then he said, honestly, “He only gives a third.”

Sosia, who was on the stool behind the board with the day-book open on his knees, made a small noise through his nose.

---

Hordeonius had been lending money under the Janus since before the soldier was born. He had lent to senators against their villas and to traders against their cargoes, to a consul once against his province and to a Vestal’s brother against a vineyard the Vestal did not know he had. He had lent against horses, and against a slave-girl’s freedom, and on one memorable occasion against a racing chariot that had won at the Plebeian Games and then been burned in the Clodian riots with the debt still on it. He had never lent against a thing that did not yet exist.

He looked at the bronze plate. He looked at the place where there should have been a date.

“When were you to have this land?”

“The autumn. When the surveyors are done.”

“Is that written here?”

The soldier looked at the plate as though he had not read it before, which perhaps he had not. “It says it’s mine.”

“It says it is to be yours,” said Hordeonius. “That is a different instrument. It is a promise. I have lent against promises before. They are the dearest paper in the world.” He took his cloth out of his sleeve and wiped his fingers, though he had not touched anything. “And there is a man on your forty iugera now, I suppose, with his own tablet, older than yours.”

“There’s a man on everyone’s,” the soldier said.

“Yes.” Hordeonius put the cloth away. “So when you lay this on my board, young man, you are asking me to lend you money on the chance that the young Caesar can move that man off, and keep him off, and that nobody in the next year or two decides the land was never the young Caesar’s to give. That is not a loan. That is a wager on the young Caesar.”

“Everybody’s wagering on him,” said the soldier, a little sullenly.

“Bucco is wagering on him,” said Hordeonius. “Bucco gives a third. That is what Bucco thinks the young Caesar is worth.”

---

The soldier went away without his money, and without his tablet, because Hordeonius had told him to leave it on the board while he thought, and he had. Hordeonius sat looking at it.

“You’re never,” said Sosia.

“I am thinking.”

“You said yourself. A wager. You don’t wager. You’ve told me a hundred times, you don’t wager, you lend.” Sosia put the day-book down. “Bucco’s had half the Seventh through this arcade since the Kalends. He’s got a box of these things under his stool the size of a coffin. If the young Caesar falls, Bucco’s lost a coffin full of bronze. If he stands, Bucco owns half of Campania.” He spread his hands. “Let Bucco have the wager.”

Hordeonius did not answer. He was looking past Sosia, down the row.

There was a man standing at the end of the arcade, by the pillar where the light came in, who had been there for some time. Hordeonius had noticed him first while the soldier was talking. He was not a soldier. He was a countryman, sixty or so, in a tunic of undyed wool and a good thick cloak gone thin at the shoulders, and he had something under his arm wrapped in a cloth, and he had been watching the soldier at Hordeonius’s board the way a man watches a dog he does not trust near his chickens.

Now that the soldier had gone he came down the row. He did not hurry. He stopped in front of the board and unwrapped the cloth, and laid on the board, next to the soldier’s bronze plate, a wooden tablet with the wax gone almost black with age.

“I’m told you lend,” he said.

“I do.”

“Against land?”

“Against a great many things.”

The countryman put one finger on the black tablet. It was a deed. Hordeonius could read it without picking it up: a sale of land in the territory of Venusia, sixty years old, the seller’s name and the buyer’s name and the witnesses, seven of them, and the bounds. Forty-two iugera. From the oak to the spring to the old road.

“That’s mine,” the countryman said. “My father bought it from the colony. It’s good ground. And in the autumn they’ll put a soldier on it.” He glanced at the bronze plate lying beside his deed, and Hordeonius saw him read the word *Venusia* on it, and saw his mouth go tight. “Maybe that one.”

“Maybe.”

“So I’ll lose it,” the countryman said. “That’s settled. The young Caesar’s said so and the Senate’s said so. But I’m told there’ll be compensation. From the treasury. When the treasury can pay.” He looked at Hordeonius directly. “I’d like to borrow against that.”

Sosia laughed out loud.

Hordeonius looked at him, and Sosia stopped laughing and picked up the day-book again.

---

He sat for a long time with the two tablets on the board in front of him, side by side: the bronze plate with the soldier’s name and no date, and the black deed with the countryman’s father’s name and every date there had ever been. Both men wanted money now, against an autumn neither of them could see.

“What will you give me?” the countryman said.

Hordeonius took the stylus.

“I will lend you,” he said slowly, “a quarter of what your land would fetch at a fair price, at one per cent a month, against your claim to compensation, which I will hold. If the treasury pays, you will pay me. If the treasury does not pay, I will have lent a quarter of the value of forty-two iugera to a man who no longer owns them, and I will have a black tablet to show for it.” He wrote. “And I will have deserved it.”

The countryman watched him write. He did not look grateful. He looked like a man watching a surgeon.

“That’s not much,” he said.

“It is more than anyone else in this arcade will give you for a promise from the treasury,” Hordeonius said. “Including Bucco. Especially Bucco.” He turned the wax so the man could read it. “Your name there. And the witness.”

Sosia witnessed, with a face like a closed shutter.

When the countryman had gone, with his money in a purse inside his tunic and his hand on it, Hordeonius sat for a while longer. Then he took the soldier’s bronze plate, finally, in his fingers, and turned it over. The back was blank. He put it in the strongbox, under the leases, where the Visellius copies had lain for three years, and locked it.

“You’re going to lend him something too,” Sosia said. “The soldier. When he comes back.”

“A third,” said Hordeonius. “The same as Bucco. I would not like to be thought to know better than Bucco.”

He wrote both loans into the day-book in his own hand, one under the other, and drew a line under them, and found that his hand was not quite steady, and wiped it on the cloth.
