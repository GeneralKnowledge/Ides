# The Door

POV: Nysa · Rome, the young Caesar’s house · spring 41

---

In the young Caesar’s house the door was the busiest room.

Nysa learned that on her second morning. On the first she had been put in the wool room with the old freedwoman whose eyes were going, and had sewn, and had been shown where the lamps were kept and how much oil went in each, which was less than she would have thought possible for a house with that name over the door. On the second morning the steward came to the wool room before light and stood in the doorway and looked at her the way the steward in the Carinae had looked at her, as if she were a jar and he was trying to remember what was in it.

“You count,” he said.

“Yes.”

“The porter can’t. Or he can, but he counts what he’s paid to.” He was a short man, Greek, with a careful beard and ink in the creases of his knuckles. His name was Glycon. “Sit at the door. Count who comes. Write down where they’re from, if they’ll tell you, and what they’ve brought. Don’t promise anybody anything.”

“What would I promise?”

He looked at her for a moment longer. “They’ll ask,” he said, and went.

The house stood above a flight of steps off the Forum, the stairs where the ring-makers had their stalls, and it was not a great house. It had belonged to an orator, somebody told her, a small man with a big voice, dead now. The atrium was narrow and the masks along its wall were not the young Caesar’s family but the orator’s, because nobody had taken them down. The floors were plain. The couches in the dining room had cushions on them that Nysa would have sent back to the fuller’s in the Domus. The young Caesar ate there, when he ate, which the kitchen said was not often, and he ate bread and small fish and green figs and drank very little, and the cook had given up trying.

But the door was full.

They were there when she came out with her stool and her tablet in the grey before the first hour, sitting along the bottom of the wall and on the steps below it, wrapped in their cloaks against the cold. She counted them in rows, as she always did, and in tens, and in Greek, because it was quicker. Forty-one. Then a family came up the steps from the Forum, a man and a woman and three children and an old woman on the man’s arm, and it was forty-seven.

She had not expected the children.

At the Domus Publica the door had been full of men who wanted something: senators with a favour, freedmen with a contract, the Gaulish chiefs in their trousers who had come to see the man who had beaten their fathers. They had stood. They had waited the way men wait who expect to be let in. These people sat. They had come a long way, she could see it in their feet, and they had the look of people who would sit there until somebody made them go.

Nearly all of them had something in their hands. A tablet in a leather case. A roll of papyrus tied with string. A bronze plate with a hole in the corner for a nail. One man held a piece of a boundary stone in his lap, the top of it, with letters cut on the face and the rough break below where somebody had knocked it off its base, and he held it the way a woman holds a baby that is asleep.

“Where are you from?” she said to the first of them.

“Cremona.”

She wrote it down. “And what have you brought?”

He held out his tablet. It was a deed of sale. She could read enough to see that it was old, and that it had a lot of names on it, and that the last name was the same as the first.

“My grandfather’s,” he said. “Bought from the colony. We’ve been on it sixty years. There’s a man there now with a stake through my wife’s onions says it’s his.” He held the tablet out further. “You can give it to him. To Caesar. He’ll see it’s in order.”

“I’m only counting,” Nysa said.

He looked at her, and at the tablet she had on her knee, and at the stylus. “Then count this,” he said, and put the deed of sale into her lap on top of her own tablet, and went back to his place on the wall.

---

By the third hour there were ninety-two. She had a pile of deeds on the stool beside her that she had not asked for and could not give back, because every time she tried to give one back its owner looked at her as if she had slapped him. She had stopped trying. She had begun instead to tie each one with a strip of wool from the basket and scratch on the wool a letter she could read again, *C* for Cremona and *M* for Mantua and a little crooked *B* for Bononia, and the porter watched her do it and spat between his feet.

“You’ll have them in here all summer,” he said. “Every one you take, he’ll tell ten.”

“I didn’t take them.”

“No,” the porter said. “You count.”

The young Caesar did not come out. Twice in the morning a man in a good cloak came up the steps and the porter opened the door for him without asking his name, and he went in past the rows of sitting people as if they were a hedge, and came out again after a while, and went down the steps the same way. Nysa wrote them down as she had been told. *A man in a blue cloak, in at the second hour, out before the third, brought nothing.* She did not know who they were. She found that she wanted to.

A little after the third hour the door opened from inside and a girl came out.

She was perhaps fifteen. She had a woman with her, older, who carried a basket on her arm and walked a step behind. The girl wore a pale blue stola that was too good for the house, and her hair was done high in a style Nysa had last seen on a woman in the Carinae, and she stopped on the top step and looked down at the people sitting along the wall, and the people looked at her.

Nobody said anything. The girl’s face did not change. Then she went down the steps between them, keeping her skirt in her hand so it did not touch anybody, and the woman with the basket followed her, and they went away across the Forum toward the Sacred Way.

“Who’s that?” Nysa said.

The porter looked at her as if she were simple. “The mistress.”

“His wife?”

“His wife.” The porter said it the way you say the name of a wine you do not like. “Goes to the Vesta every morning with an offering. Her mother sends the offering. Her mother sends the woman.” He spat again. “Her mother sends everything.”

Nysa had known that the young Caesar had a wife. Everybody knew that. It had been part of the bargain two winters ago, when the three of them met on the island in the river and divided the world, and the kitchen of the Carinae had talked about it for a week: the young Caesar to take Fulvia’s girl, Clodius’s girl, a child, so that Antony’s house and the young Caesar’s house should be one house. She had not thought about it since. She had not thought, until she saw the girl on the step with her hair done that way, that she was now in the same house as Fulvia’s daughter, and that Fulvia’s daughter’s woman would know her face.

She looked down at her tablet until they were gone.

---

The man from the oil came up the steps in the fifth hour.

She knew him at once, though she had never known his name. He had come to the Carinae every month of the two years she had been there, up the lane from the Esquiline gate with a mule and four jars, and handed the jars in at the kitchen door and taken his money from the steward and gone away without ever coming further in than the step. A Sabine, from somewhere up the Via Salaria. The best oil in the house had been his. The cook had said so, and had said it was because his trees were old.

He did not have a mule now. He had a tablet, and his face had changed in some way she could not have described, except that it looked smaller.

He did not know her. He would not have looked at the people inside the kitchen door, any more than they had looked at him. He sat down at the end of the row on the steps, by the man with the piece of boundary stone, and the two of them began to talk in low voices about which magistrate was taking papers and which was not.

“Where are you from?” she said, when it was his turn.

“Reate,” he said. “Near.”

“And what have you brought?”

He showed her the tablet. It was a lease, not a deed. She could read a name near the top of it that was not his, and a date, and a sum.

“I don’t own it,” he said. “I never said I did. I rent it. I’ve rented it twenty years, my father before me, from a man who owned it from his father. Now he’s lost it, it’s been given to a soldier, and the soldier’s sold it to a man in Rome, and the man in Rome wants me off by the summer so he can put his own man in.” He turned the tablet over and back. “So I went to the house of the man I sell my oil to. I went to Antony’s house. I thought, he’s a great man, his wife will see me, she’s seen my oil. And the steward there said the triumvir is in the East and the mistress is not receiving, and if I had a complaint about the young Caesar’s surveyors I should take it to the young Caesar.” He looked up the steps at the closed door. “So here I am. Taking it.”

Nysa wrote down *Reate. A lease.* Then she sat with the stylus on the wax and did not write anything else.

She could have told him that the steward in the Carinae was a careful man going grey who would not have let a supplier past the kitchen step if Jupiter had come with him. She could have told him the mistress had been in the house that day, because the mistress was always in the house, and would have seen him if she had wanted his oil more than she wanted him to go to the young Caesar’s door. She could have told him that his oil had been the best in the house. None of it would have been any use to him, and all of it would have told him where she had been.

“Do you want me to take it?” she said instead. “The lease. I’m only counting. But I can tie it.”

He considered her. Then he gave her the tablet, and she tied it with a strip of wool, and scratched an *R* on the wool, and put it on the pile.

“Ninety-eight,” she said, to nobody, and wrote it down.

At the sixth hour Glycon came out and took the pile of deeds off her stool without a word and carried it inside in both arms like firewood, and she heard him put it down somewhere in the passage behind the door, on top of something else.
