# The Descent

**I-01** · POV: Silenus · the Alpine descent and the Taurini, late autumn 218 BC
**Sources:** Pol. 3.54.1 (snow near the setting of the Pleiades), 3.56.3–4 (fifteen days in the Alps; the numbers that came down), 3.60 (the Taurini's town taken in three days): text. Hannibal showing the army Italy from the pass (Pol. 3.54.2) and the broken path on the descent (Pol. 3.55): to check.

On the morning they began to go down, Silenus wrote nothing, because the ink had frozen in its horn and he could not make it thaw against his body however long he held it there.

He had kept his notes every day since New Carthage. He had written the rivers and the tribes and the names of the passes as the guides said them, and the number of days, and the weather, and once, on the Rhône, a sentence about the elephants on the rafts that he had read aloud to Sosylus that night and that Sosylus had admitted, in his grudging Spartan way, was not bad. He had meant to write the summit. He had stood on it two days before, in a wind that took the skin off his lips, while Hannibal rode along the column pointing down into the haze and telling the men that what lay below them was the plain of the Po, and the Gauls who were waiting there to be their friends, and beyond that Rome. Silenus had looked where the hand pointed and seen cloud. He had meant to write that too, honestly, that he had seen only cloud. Now the ink was ice and the sentence was going cold in his head with the rest of him.

The path down was worse than the path up. On the way up they had been fighting the tribes in the passes, and a man could at least be angry at something with a face. On the way down there was nobody to fight but the mountain. The snow of this year had fallen on top of the snow of last year, and the new snow was soft and the old snow beneath it was ice, so that a man put his foot down, felt it hold, put his weight on it, and went, sliding, with his arms out, down the slope and off the edge and gone. It happened without any noise. Silenus saw it happen four times in the first morning, and after the fourth he stopped looking at the edge and looked only at the heels of the man in front of him, a Libyan spearman with rags wound round his feet, and put his own feet where the Libyan’s had been.

The mules were worse off than the men. Their hooves went through the crust of the new snow into the old and stuck there, and they stood with their legs trapped and screamed until someone cut their loads off and left them. He passed one that had been there since the day before, frozen standing up, its pack still on its back. A Gaul was trying to get the pack off. He looked up as Silenus passed and grinned and said something in his own language and went back to work.

Around midday the column stopped altogether. Word came back along it, mouth to mouth, in four languages, that the path ahead had gone. A whole stretch of the mountainside had fallen away, old landslip or new nobody knew, and there was nothing beyond the edge of it but a long white slope to the bottom of the valley. The guides had tried to take the army round, up over the shoulder of the mountain, and the leading men had gone into snow to their waists and could not get out. So the army sat down on the path, or stood, since there was nowhere to sit, and waited, and the light began to go.

Silenus spent the night in a hollow in the rocks with Sosylus and two Carthaginian clerks who kept the pay lists, all four of them pressed together under every blanket they had among them. The clerks talked in Punic in low voices, and he did not have enough of it to follow, only the numbers, which he could always follow in any language. Sosylus slept, or pretended to. Silenus lay awake and listened to the sound of men working along the path in the dark, the knock of picks and the scrape of shovels, and every so often a shout, and once a long falling cry that stopped.

In the morning they were building a road.

He went forward to see it, because it was what he was there for, and because his feet hurt less when he moved. They had cleared the new snow off the broken stretch and were cutting into the rock of the mountainside itself, a ledge wide enough for a mule, then wide enough for a horse, Numidians and Libyans and Spaniards all working at it together in shifts, the officers working beside them. He saw Hannibal there. He was down on the ledge with a mattock like any of them, his cloak thrown off, and when a rock came loose and went bouncing down the slope he stood and watched it go all the way to the bottom and said something that made the men around him laugh. Silenus was too far off to hear what it was. He would have given a good deal to know. He made up three versions of it as he walked back along the column and threw them all away.

It took them a day to make the road for the men and the horses. It took three days more to make it wide enough for the elephants, and by then the elephants were so starved that they stood with their heads down and let the drivers lead them across the ledge without a struggle, and two of the drivers wept.

Then they came down out of the snow.

It happened faster than he had imagined. One evening they were still walking on ice; the next afternoon there were trees, real trees, pines and then beeches with their leaves still on, and the path turned to mud, and a stream ran beside it that was not frozen, and men threw themselves down beside it on their bellies and drank like dogs. There was grass. The horses put their heads down and would not lift them however they were beaten. That night they slept in a valley where it was only cold, and Silenus held his ink horn over a fire until the ice in it turned black and liquid, and sat with his tablet on his knee and found that he did not know how to begin.

He tried: *On the fifteenth day after they began the ascent, the army descended into Italy.* It was true and it was nothing. He scratched it out. He tried the summit, the hand pointing at the cloud. He scratched that out too, because it made Hannibal look a liar, and Hannibal was not a liar, the plain had been down there, he had only not been able to see it. He sat with the stylus in his hand until Sosylus leaned over and read what was on the tablet, which was nothing, and said, “Good. Short.”

He lay down afterwards beside Sosylus under the same two cloaks and listened to the camp. It was louder than it had been for a month. Men were singing somewhere down the valley in a language he did not know, and somewhere else a man was weeping steadily, without any shame, the way men weep when they are too tired to stop, and nobody told him to be quiet.

“What will you call it?” Sosylus said in the dark. “The book.”

Silenus had thought about this a good deal, in Spain, in the long evenings, with the wine. He had considered *The Deeds of Hannibal*, which was what Hannibal’s clerks would expect, and *The War against Rome*, which was what the Greeks in Sicily would buy. He had considered, in his more ambitious moments, something that would put him next to Thucydides on a bookseller’s shelf.

“I don’t know yet,” he said. “It depends how it ends.”

Sosylus grunted. “They all say that.”

“Then they are all right.”

There was a pause, and then Sosylus laughed, which he did perhaps twice a year, and turned over and went to sleep.

The next morning the clerks counted the army.

They did it in a meadow below the last of the trees, the men filing past by companies, the clerks at a table made of two shields on a baggage box, the Punic numbers going down on the lists. Silenus stood behind the clerks and watched the lists grow. He knew what had left New Carthage in the spring. Everybody knew; it had been a thing to say, at the start, the size of it. He watched the companies go past, the Libyans in their mended mail, the Spaniards in white with the purple borders gone grey, the slingers from the islands, and the Numidians last, leading horses so thin the ribs showed under the hair like the frames of boats. When it was over the senior clerk added the columns twice and showed the totals to an officer, and the officer looked at them for some time and then took the tablet away to Hannibal’s tent.

Silenus did not need to see the tablet. He had been adding as they passed. It came to something over twenty thousand on foot and about six thousand horse, and that was the army that had come to take Italy.

He did write that down. He wrote the number, and under it the number they had started with, and under that nothing.

Three days later they took the town of the Taurini, who would not be friends, and Hannibal had every man in it of an age to fight put to death so that the other tribes of the plain would hear of it before they made up their minds. Silenus watched from the slope above the town, with the smoke coming up past him. Afterwards Gauls came into the camp from all over the plain, in twos and threes and then in whole villages, with gifts and with their chiefs in front, wanting to be friends after all. He wrote that down as well. It was the first thing since the summit that he could write without having to decide what it meant.
