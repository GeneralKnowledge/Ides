# II-03: The Watchword

POV: Fulvia · Praeneste
Date: September 41 BC
Sources: Dio 48.10.4 (the sword, the watchword, the speeches to the soldiers); App. *BC* 5.21 (Fulvia at Praeneste, for fear for her children)

---

The tribune came for the watchword before it was light, and Lucius was not there to give it.

Fulvia heard him in the court below, a young man’s voice trying to be quiet and not managing it. Lucius had ridden out the evening before to the colonies on the Labici road with forty horse and a speech, and had sent back word at midnight that he would sleep at Gabii and be home by the third hour. The tribune knew that. Everybody in the house knew it. He had come anyway, to the house where the consul was not, and was standing in the court explaining to the porter that the guard on the lower gate changed at first light and could not change without a word.

She lay and listened to him explain it twice. Then she got up and put on her day tunic and went down without her woman, with her hair still in its night plait, and the tribune saw her on the stair and stopped explaining.

He was one of Lucius’s, a Volscian from somewhere south with a good family and no beard to speak of. He had been at Mutina with Antony as a boy of eighteen, he had told her so twice at dinner, and he had stood in the marsh at Forum Gallorum. Most of the men who had stood in that marsh were now on a plot of somebody else’s land in Campania, waiting for the young Caesar’s surveyor, and the ones who were not were here, and she had been counting them for a month.

“The watchword,” she said.

He looked past her up the stair, where Lucius was not.

“The consul will be back by the third hour,” he said. “The guard changes at first light.”

“Then it changes now.” She came down the last three steps. “Unless you want to stand there until the third hour and tell the men on the lower gate that the consul’s wife wouldn’t give it you.”

He did not want that. She watched him not want it.

Behind her on the landing her younger son had come out of the nursery in his shift and was holding the rail with both hands, the way he held everything, as if it might be taken off him. Iullus was not yet three. He had his father’s chin already and the nurse’s way of standing. The nurse came out after him and took him up, and he did not cry, but he did not take his eyes off the tribune either.

“*Philippi*,” Fulvia said.

The tribune said it after her, carefully, to have it right. Then he looked at her as if he would like to ask something, and went.

She stood in the court in her night plait and thought about what she had given him. A word for the lower gate, to be passed from mouth to mouth along the wall all day by men who had been at Philippi with Marcus Antonius and had watched the young Caesar carried out of his camp into a marsh. Every one of them would say it to the next man and think of who had won that battle, and who had been sick in a reed bed while it was won. By evening it would be at the colonies. By the end of the week it would be in Rome, and somebody would carry it up the steps of the house above the Ring-makers’ Stairs and say it in front of the young Caesar, and she would very much like to have been there to see his face.

Her woman found her there and took her up to dress.

---

The men were drawn up on the flat ground below the temple by the second hour, because she had told the tribune to have them there, and Lucius was still on the Gabii road.

She had not decided to wear the sword until she saw it on the chest. It was one of Marcus’s, an old one he had left in the Carinae when he went east because it had been his at Mutina and he did not want it any more: a plain cavalry sword, too long for her, in a scabbard with the leather gone dark where his hand had been. Her woman had packed it with the plate when they left Rome. Fulvia had not asked her why. She had known Marcus’s women long enough to know that they packed what might be useful and did not explain.

It sat badly. The belt went twice round her and the scabbard knocked her knee on the stair. She walked down to the flat ground with it knocking her knee and her two sons behind her with the nurse, and Claudia behind them, who had not been asked and had come anyway, in a grey cloak, with her face very still.

There were perhaps three thousand men on the ground. She could see who was at the front and who had put himself at the back. She could see the colony men by their new boots, bought on credit against land they had not ploughed, and the men from Lucius’s levies by their bad ones, and here and there an old Antonian with no boots worth looking at and a face like a fist. Those were the ones she watched. The others would go where they were paid. The old ones went where they had been before.

The temple was behind her on its terraces, the great stair going up the hill to Fortune and her lots, and she was aware of it the way you are aware of a crowd at your back.

She did not make a speech. Lucius made speeches. She had listened to them for a month and they were good speeches, about the law and the farmers and the dignity of the consulship, and the men cheered them and went back to their tents and asked each other whether the money had come.

“Who paid you at Philippi?” she said.

She had a voice that carried without being raised. She had used it over Clodius’s body, in her own atrium, with the street outside full to the walls, and men who had heard it then still crossed the street.

Somebody near the front said *Antonius*.

“And who paid you at Brundisium, when you came home? Who paid you on the road, and at Capua, and for your boots?” She waited. “Nobody. The young Caesar has your money and he is giving it out as land, and the land belongs to men like your fathers, and when you have it he will have you. That is what he has paid for. Look at your boots.”

Some of them did. It ran along the front rank like wind along barley.

“My husband will be in Italy when the sailing season opens,” she said. “He has told me to hold until then, and I am holding.”

He had told her nothing. There had been one letter from Ephesus in the spring about the provinces and the price of silver, and one from Tarsus about a fleet, and nothing since the summer, and the summer had been long. She said it to three thousand men as if she had the letter in her belt.

From the back, from the men who had put themselves at the back, somebody shouted something about what she had in her belt, and about the size of it, and the men round him laughed.

She found him. It took no time at all. He was a big man, a colony man with new boots, standing in a gap he had made by being big. She looked at him until the laughing stopped, and then she looked at the men either side of him, one after the other, and they found something to look at on the ground.

“Tribune,” she said, not loudly. “His name, and his tent, and who lent him the money for those boots.”

The tribune went to get them.

It was then, with the whole flat ground quiet and the tribune walking down the line, that the old Antonians began to beat their shields. It was not all of them, and not in time: a slow knocking that went along the front rank and back into the second, and then the rest took it up because the rest always did, and it became a cheer, and the cheer went up the terraces of the temple behind her and came back off the stone.

She stood in it. She did not let it change her face. Her elder son had come forward to the edge of the nurse’s reach and was looking at the sword on her hip as if it were the most wonderful thing he had ever seen. Claudia was not looking at anything.

When the cheer had gone on long enough she turned and went back up the hill, and they went on cheering behind her, and she counted them as she went the way she counted a household: by who had been loudest, and who had looked to see who else was cheering first.

---

Lucius came in at the fourth hour with mud to his knees and found her in the small room over the court with the sword on the table.

“They’re saying you gave the word,” he said. He was smiling. He always smiled first and asked afterwards. “They’re saying you made them a speech. Did you? What did you say?”

“I asked them who paid them.”

“Well, and who did?” He sat down on the end of the couch and pulled at the strap of his boot. “I’ve got four hundred more at Gabii who’ll come in if we can feed them. They want to hear from Marcus, mostly. I said we’d heard. Have we heard?”

“No.”

He stopped pulling at the boot. Then he started again.

Her woman came in with a tablet and stood by the door, the one who counted the silver, a narrow woman with clever hands who had been with Fulvia since Clodius. She waited until Lucius had got the boot off and gone out shouting for water.

“Shall I write to Alexandria again?” she said. “The ship from Puteoli goes on the Ides.”

“Write to both,” Fulvia said. “Alexandria and Ephesus. Say the same thing to each, and put the date on it.”
