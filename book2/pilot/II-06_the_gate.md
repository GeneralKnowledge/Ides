# II-06: The Gate

POV: Lucius Antonius · Rome
Date: Late 41 BC (month to check)
Sources: App. *BC* 5.30 (three cohorts by night; Nonius admits Lucius at the gates; Lucius’s promise that Antony will lay down his powers, check); Dio 48.5 (*Pietas*, check); App. *BC* 4.3 (Beneventum among the eighteen, check)

---

The gate was shut, and then it was not.

Lucius had been told it would open and had not believed it. He sat his horse in the road under the wall with three cohorts behind him in the dark, men who had marched since noon with their shields wrapped against the wet and their mouths shut, and looked up at the tower, where a single lamp had been burning since they came over the last rise. Nobody had said anything from the tower. Nobody had challenged them. He could hear his own horse breathing and the drip off the arch.

Then a bar went back on the inside with a sound like a tree coming down, and the left leaf of the gate moved, and a man came out through the gap with a lantern held low, the way you hold one when you do not want to be seen from above.

“Consul,” the man said.

It was Nonius. Lucius knew him a little, from the Forum, from the kind of dinner where the man at the bottom of the couch is the one who knows where the keys are. He had charge of this gate and of the men on it. He had a wide face and a careful mouth, and he held the lantern so that it lit Lucius and not himself.

“Is it done?” Lucius said, and then wished he had said something else, something a consul says.

“It’s open,” said Nonius. “I’d be quick about it, consul.”

The cohorts went in past him in the dark, the hobnails loud on the stones under the arch and then quieter on the street, and Lucius rode in among them. The street was empty. A door opened a hand’s width on the left and shut again. A dog barked behind a wall and somebody hit it. Then they were in.

Somebody’s hearth had been put out with water, and the whole street smelled of wet ash.

---

By the second hour of the morning the Forum was full.

He had not been sure it would be. He had lain awake in the house on the Carinae with his boots still on, Fulvia’s house, Marcus’s house, listening to the city wake round him and trying to hear what kind of waking it was. He heard shutters go back, and a cart, and a woman calling a child, and it sounded like any morning. At first light his secretary had come in with a tablet and said that there were people in the street, and then that there were a great many people, and then that they had begun to go down towards the Forum on their own, without being sent, and Lucius had got up and washed his face and sent for his lictors.

Now he stood on the Rostra with the twelve of them below him and looked out at the crowd, and the crowd looked back.

He had stood here as tribune and as consul and he knew how to read it. He knew where to look for the men who had been paid to be there, at the edges, near the steps, in pairs, and he looked and found almost none. He knew how a crowd stood when it had come to watch a fight, loose and turned half away, ready to go, and this one was not standing like that. It was close. It was facing him. A great many of them had papers in their hands.

He began with the law, because he always began with the law. He told them that the land of Italy belonged to the people of Italy and that no decree of three men in a tent could take it from them without the vote of the Roman people in assembly. He told them that he was their consul, elected by them, and that he had come into Rome not as an enemy of anyone but because the city’s consul belonged in the city. He told them his brother would lay down his extraordinary powers as soon as the Republic was settled and that he, Lucius, had his brother’s word for it.

That was the part he had worried about in the night. He had his brother’s word in a letter that was eight months old and said nothing of the kind. But it was the kind of thing Marcus would say, if he were asked, and Marcus was not here to be asked, and the crowd cheered it so loudly that the pigeons went up off the Basilica all together and came down again on the other side.

He felt it then, go through him from the stones up.

He talked for longer than he meant to. He always did. He gave them the farmers of Cremona and of Capua, the old men on benches in the sun and the boundary stones their grandfathers had set, and he could see it landing, he could see faces in the front of the crowd go still the way faces did when a man said aloud the thing they had been saying to their wives. When he stopped they did not let him go. They called his name. Somebody called *Pietas*, the name he had taken for Marcus’s sake, and others took it up, and he raised his hand to them and did not trust himself to say anything.

Coming down the steps of the Rostra was like coming down out of a high sea.

They pressed round him at the bottom, and the lictors made a lane, and the papers came at him through the lane from both sides. He took them as they came and passed them back to his secretary. There was a tablet of title to a farm at Arretium, very old, with the wax gone brown, and a petition from the townsmen of Sutrium about their common pasture, and a letter from a widow at Bovillae that somebody else had written for her and she had signed with a cross. A man with no front teeth held up a deed of sale and said, *My father’s, my father’s, read it*, and Lucius put his hand over the man’s hand and said that he would.

He said it to all of them. *Bring it to my house. I’ll see it. I will.*

At the edge of the lane, where the lictors’ rods ended, there was a man who was not pushing.

He was standing very straight, with his weight on both feet, the way soldiers stand when they have decided not to be moved, and he had a bronze tablet in his hand instead of a wax one. He was perhaps forty. He had a scar through the hair at his temple and the brown arms of a man who had been working outside all summer. When Lucius came level with him he did not hold the tablet out. He waited to be asked.

“You’ve a paper,” Lucius said.

“Discharge.” The man held it so that Lucius could see the seal. “Your brother’s army. Philippi. My plot’s at Beneventum, by the river. Marked out in the spring.” He did not look at the crowd round them, the men with their wax deeds and their old men’s handwriting. “There’s a family on it still. They say the consul’s on their side. I’d like to know if the consul’s on mine.”

Lucius looked at the seal. It was a good seal, the triumvirs’ seal, clear and deep, the kind that had been cut by somebody who meant it to last.

“My brother’s soldiers,” he said, “will have everything my brother promised them.”

The soldier looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded once, the way a man nods when he has been told the hour, and put the bronze tablet back inside his tunic, and turned and went off through the crowd without hurrying.

Lucius stood in the lane between the lictors. Behind him his secretary had stopped, because he had stopped.

“Consul?”

“Yes.” He turned round. The secretary had a basket on his arm, a big flat market basket that somebody’s slave had found for him when his arms were full, and it was heaped to the rim with the papers of the morning. There was wax in it and bronze and papyrus, titles and petitions and deeds of sale, and somewhere in among them, Lucius knew, there were discharge tablets too, because soldiers had pressed in with the rest and he had taken everything, and said *bring it to my house* to everyone, and had not looked.

“Take them home,” he said. “Put them with the others.”
