# Rings

**Pilot scene II-12** · POV: Hanno · Carthage, the senate house, autumn 216 BC
**Sources:** Liv. 23.11–13 (Mago’s report; the rings poured out in the porch of the senate house; Himilco’s taunt; Hanno’s reply beginning “I would have kept silent today”: text). The size of the heap (Livy’s three and a half measures, or one) and the reinforcements voted: to check.

---

They had to open the doors of the senate house to bring the baskets in, and the wind came in with them off the harbour and lifted the corners of everybody’s robes.

Hanno had been told what was in the baskets the night before. Half the city had been told. Mago had landed at noon and gone straight up to the Byrsa to sacrifice, and by evening every slave who carried water in the upper town knew that Hannibal’s brother had brought rings from Italy, gold rings, taken off the hands of dead Romans, so many that they had been measured out like grain. Hanno had heard it from his steward, who had heard it from the fishmonger, and he had said, “How many measures?” and the steward had said that nobody agreed.

So he knew, and he had come anyway, and he had sat in his usual place on the third bench where he could see the door, and he watched the baskets come in.

Mago spoke first. He was a handsome young man, the youngest of Hamilcar’s sons, and he spoke well, as all of them did; their father had made sure of it. He told the senate what his brother had done in Italy. He named the battles, and the men his brother had fought, four consuls and a dictator and a master of horse, and he said how many Romans had died, and the number was so large that Hanno, who had spent his life with numbers, could not make it mean anything. Then Mago said that words were cheap and Romans were liars and Carthaginians were merchants and liked to see the goods, and he made a sign to the slaves in the porch.

They tipped the baskets out on the stone.

The noise was not like coins. It was lighter and thinner, a sort of pouring hiss, and it went on for longer than Hanno expected. The rings slid and settled and slid again. The senators on the lower benches stood up to look and the ones on the upper benches leaned forward, and for a moment the whole house was quiet except for the rings, still finding their places on the floor.

“Only their knights wear gold,” said Mago into the quiet. “Their knights, and the first men of their senate. These came off the field at Cannae.”

Hanno looked at the heap. He tried, out of habit, to weigh it. A man’s ring might be a quarter of an ounce, a rich man’s more; say a third. The heap was the height of a child at its peak. He began to count it in silver and then found that he did not want to, and stopped.

Mago went on. His brother had won everything that could be won in the field. Capua had come over to him, and other cities would follow. What he needed now was men to replace the men he had lost, and grain, and money to pay the Gauls and the Spaniards, because the Romans had stopped coming out to fight him and a war of sieges was a war of payrolls. He said that as if it were a small thing.

When he sat down, Himilco stood up. Himilco had been Hamilcar’s man and was now the sons’ man, and wanted the whole house to know it. He turned on the bench so that he faced Hanno directly, and he spread his arms.

“Well, Hanno?” he said. “What now? Do you still say the war was a mistake? Shall we hand Hannibal over to the Romans after all, as you advised? Shall we send them an apology with the rings?”

There was laughter, a good deal of it. Hanno waited for it to finish. He was seventy and had sat in this house for forty years and had learned that laughter ran out if you let it.

“I would have kept silent today,” he said, “so as not to spoil anyone’s pleasure. But since Himilco asks me, I will answer him.”

He did not stand. He found that his knees did not want to, and in any case a man who stood looked as if he wanted something.

“I am glad of the victory,” he said. “I would be a fool not to be. I am glad of anything that frightens the Romans. But I have been listening to Mago, and I want to ask him some questions, because I am an old man and slow, and the young understand these things better than I do.”

Mago inclined his head, very politely.

“Your brother has won,” Hanno said. “He has destroyed their armies. Then why does he need soldiers? He has taken their camps and their baggage and the rings off their fingers. Then why does he need grain and money from us? If I had won a war I would be sending money home, not asking for it.”

Somebody on the lower benches said something sharp and was hushed.

“Another question,” said Hanno. “Has any of the Latin cities gone over to him? Any one of them?”

Mago said, after a moment, that none had yet.

“Has a single man of their thirty-five tribes deserted to Hannibal?”

Mago said that he had not heard of one.

“Have the Romans sent anyone to him to talk about peace? Has anyone come out of Rome at all?”

Mago said no.

Hanno looked at the heap of rings in the porch, where the wind was still moving the dust round the edges of it.

“Then we are exactly where we were on the day he crossed into Italy,” he said. “The same war. The same enemy, who has not asked for terms. Only now we have a great many gold rings, and he wants the money back.”

Nobody laughed this time. He thought for a moment that he had them. He said what he had come to say: that now, while Rome was frightened and Carthage was winning, was the one time in the whole war when a peace could be made on good terms, and that if they let it pass they would not see another. He said it plainly, without any ornament, the way he would have told his steward to sell a cargo before the price fell.

Then the house voted, and it voted for Hannibal.

It was not even close. They voted him horse and elephants and silver, and they talked about Spain, and about sending men there to hire more men, and the talk went on into the afternoon. Hanno sat through all of it. He had lost votes in this house before. He had lost the vote that sent the boy out to Hasdrubal’s army in Spain, and the vote on Saguntum, and he had found that the thing to do was to sit through the rest of the business and go home and eat dinner, and remember exactly who had said what.

When it was over, the slaves came back into the porch with shovels and began to put the rings into the baskets again. Mago had gone out with Himilco’s arm round his shoulders. The senators went past the heap on their way to the door, and most of them did not look down, and a few of them did.

Hanno stopped. One ring had rolled away from the others and lay by itself against the foot of a column. It was a small one, plain, with nothing cut in it. He thought it would fit a boy’s finger, or a thin young man’s, and he wondered how old a man had to be in Rome before they let him ride with the knights.

He did not pick it up. A slave with a shovel was waiting for him to move, very respectfully, and after a while he moved.
