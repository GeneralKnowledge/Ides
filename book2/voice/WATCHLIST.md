# Drafting watchlist

From the outside editorial review of Book 1, Chapters 1–6 (11 Nov 2026). The reviewer found a strong opening with no rewrite needed, and named five habits of the house style to manage, not eliminate. Questions 6 to 9 were added on 24 Nov 2026 from the cold critical review of the whole of Book 1 and the audit that checked it against the text; they carry the lessons forward, not the proposed Book 1 corrections (Book 1 is frozen). All nine are questions for the three-tests self-audit at the end of each act, read alongside `writing/voice/MIND_SHEETS.md`. They are not bans or quotas, and none of them is a reason to make the prose less polished, less articulate or less literary.

## Questions

1. **Spacing of good lines.** Read a scene's dialogue aloud. If every speaker lands a quotable line, which one is this person's and which are the book's? Keep the best one. Let the others misunderstand, repeat themselves, trail off or say the ordinary thing, where that is what this person would do. Do not manufacture awkwardness. Watch where they gather, too: the Book 1 audit (24 Nov) found about 5.5 maxims per 10,000 words in Ch 1–20 (the dinners and the Ides morning) and about 1.3 in Ch 21–38. Dinners, councils and mornings of crisis are where the habit returns; read those scenes for it first.
2. **Reversals.** Look for the turn "not this, but that", the thing that turns out to mean its opposite, the statement privately recognised as false. Once in a scene is thought. Twice is a shape. Change how the second thought moves (a question, an interruption, an action, a plain statement) and keep the insight.
3. **Scene endings.** The ending tests in the mind sheets push endings toward a physical action. The review adds the other half: if every scene ends on a gesture that carries the theme, readers start waiting for it. Across an act, let some scenes stop when the business is done: someone leaves, the door shuts, the next thing on the list. Keep the gesture where it is earned. The Book 1 audit found the share of gesture endings steady (35%) and the trouble in runs: eight gesture or widening endings in a row from Ch 18 to Ch 21. At the act audit, list the endings in scene order; where more than three in a row land on a gesture or a widening line, look again at the weakest of them. A long run is a reason to look, not a rule. Watch for two neighbouring scenes ending on the same image or phrase (Book 1: two column endings in Ch 36–37, two “list of promised acres” in Ch 38).
4. **Minor characters.** A walk-on gets eloquence only when the person would have it: education, rank, drink, nerve, a reason to show off. The test case is the former praetor in Book 1 Ch 6 ("fear when it has learned Latin"), which stays because the line is the event: he is a senator, he is drinking, the room goes quiet, he takes it back. A muleteer, a clerk or a soldier in Book 2 talks like a muleteer, a clerk or a soldier.
5. **Visible design.** Echoes, parallels and rising consequences are the book's structure and stay. Each scene must also work as an encounter between people who want something from each other, with no knowledge of the scenes around it. If a scene is there only to complete a parallel, give it its own trouble or cut it.
6. **Motif ownership.** At each act audit, count the book's recurring motifs (counting, clean hands, flat palms, silence, objects at endings) by scene and by owner. In Book 1 the spread was defensible because the meaning changed with each owner (Nysa's counting is her one power, Octavian's is legitimacy, Decimus's is the engineer's habit, Laberius refuses it); the faults were saturation inside a single scene (twelve counting words in one Nysa scene, eleven in one Octavian scene) and a motif repeated until it announced itself (Decimus's washing). Ask: whose motif is this, does it mean something different here, and is it doing the work a thought should do? A tell used once is revealing; the same tell in four scenes is a habit of the writer. The same goes for devices: in Book 1 the steward sends Nysa out to count four times and every errand lands her at a sourced event. Book 2 ends on a fifth count, so every earlier Nysa errand in Book 2 needs its own reason, and the last count must read as hers (see her mind sheet).
7. **Convergence.** List the act's crossings, where an invented life touches a great man or a sourced event. Each needs a cause on the page: how he got there, how the great man came to know. In Book 1 the crossings a lender or a household would make (Hordeonius and Calenus's steward, Nysa at Julia's door) held; the two that strained were an invented life reaching a great man's ear with no route shown (Octavian has a centurion's line copied out of thousands at a pay table; Rufrius asks Antony for a post with no sign of how he knows his friend is there). One clause usually pays the cause. If it cannot be paid, ask whether the crossing is worth keeping.
8. **The act's question.** Before the act audit, write in one line the question that drives the act. Mark each scene: moves it, or counterpoints it. Counterpoint is allowed and often the best of a book (Book 1's Act IV withdrawals are the other half of its money story), but a reader should never go four scenes without the engine turning, and the engine should be visible as it climbs (a price said aloud, a number that changes). Book 1's Act IV had its engine (who pays the soldiers, and with what) and still read to a cold reader as touring, because the climb was interrupted and unmarked. Do not add action to fix this; make the existing question visible.
9. **Period diction.** Run a word sweep at each act audit for modern and managerial words: schedule, agenda, channel, seeded, calibrated, policy, renegotiate, mugging, chat, and their cousins. Book 1 had thirteen in 94,000 words, clustered in two scenes, plus one phrase repeated nearly word for word in two chapters. A word that is another character's view of a man (Decimus's schedule as Caesar and Antony see him) can stay; the same word in his own head goes. Search also for long phrases repeated across chapters.

## How to use it

At the act self-audit, read each scene once for questions 1 to 5, after the three tests. Questions 6 to 9 are counts and lists across the whole act, made once at the end of it. Note what you change in the changelog line for that act. If a question turns up nothing, move on.

The same points are counted against Book 1 in `reader_feedback/BOOK1_OPEN_CRITICISMS.md`, so that a criticism heard several times gets acted on.
