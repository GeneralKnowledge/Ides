# III-10: Larissa

POV: Brutus · from the marsh below Pompey’s camp to Caesar’s tent at Larissa
Date: August 48 BC by the Roman calendar (early summer by the sun), the days after Pharsalus
Sources: Plut. *Brut.* 5.1 (checked: Caesar orders Brutus spared); 6.1 to 5 (checked: a gate to marshy ground full of water and reeds, by night to Larissa, writes to Caesar, Caesar glad and keeps him among his friends, sounds him on where Pompey has gone, Pompey “put in at Egypt, as Brutus conjectured”, Brutus tries to soften him towards Cassius). The letters from Pompey’s camp burned unread: Sen. *De Ira* 2.23.4; Plin. *NH* 7.94; Dio 41.63 (check which says Pharsalus). Book 1 binds: a tent; mud to the knees; expecting chains; dinner; Caesar’s hand on his shoulder; grateful, and hating it; Caesar writes to Servilia that night (Ch 6, 8, 26, 29).

The reeds were taller than a man and the water between them was warm, which he had not expected, and full of things that moved against his legs.

He had gone out by the gate at the back of the camp because it was the gate nobody else was using. Behind him on the hill there had been shouting and then a different kind of shouting, and smoke from the tents, and a man in a good cloak running past him with a silver jug in his arms as if it were a child. Brutus had walked. He had decided that he would not run, and he had been able to keep to it as far as the reeds, and in the reeds it did not matter, because nobody could run there. The mud took him to the knee at every step and gave him back with a sound like a mouth.

He walked all night. The frogs stopped when he came near them and began again when he had gone by, so that he went forward through the dark in a small travelling silence of his own. Once he heard horses on the dry ground to his left and stood still in the water until they had passed, and found when he moved again that he had been holding his breath and that his hands were shaking, and he looked at his hands for some time in the starlight without being able to make them stop. He had not drawn his sword all day. He was not sure he still had it.

Towards morning the reeds thinned and there were fields, and a farm with a dog, and beyond them, under the first light, the walls of Larissa.

He wrote from the farm. The farmer’s wife gave him a wax tablet that had been used for counting cheeses and he smoothed it with his thumb and sat on the bench by her door with his feet in the dust and composed. It took him a long time. He wrote that he was alive, and where he was. He began a sentence about the obligations of a man who has borne arms in a cause he believed lawful towards the man who has defeated that cause, and found it would not fit on a cheese tablet, and smoothed it out again. In the end there were four lines. He gave it to the farmer’s son with a coin he found in his belt, the last one, and told him to take it to the nearest of Caesar’s officers, and then he sat and waited, because there was nothing else he could think of that would be fitting to do.

They came for him at the third hour: a young tribune and eight horsemen and a spare horse. The tribune got down and saluted, which Brutus had not expected either, and said that Caesar would see him now.

“Now,” Brutus said. He looked down at himself.

“Now, he said.”

So he went as he was. He rode into Caesar’s camp outside Larissa with the marsh dried grey to his thighs and a reed cut across the back of one hand that had bled and stopped, and the soldiers at the gate looked at him and then away, as men look away from a cart that is going to the burial ground. There were guards at the big tent. He saw them from a long way off, and he saw what one of them was holding, a coil of something that he took at that distance for a chain, and he found that he had settled the matter in his mind and was quite calm. It was a horse’s tether. The man was waiting to take his horse.

Inside it was cooler, and dim after the sun. There was a table with food on it, and a brazier, unlit, and a smell of lamp oil and leather, and Caesar was standing at the far side of the table with a secretary, reading something, and he looked up.

“Brutus,” he said. “Good.”

That was all at first. Then he came round the table, and Brutus, who had spent the night and the morning deciding how he would stand and what he would say and in what order, found that he could not remember any of it, because Caesar had taken him by the hand, the cut one, and was turning it to the light from the door.

“Reeds,” Caesar said. “Have it washed, or it will go bad in this heat. Sit down. Have you eaten?”

“No.”

“Then eat. Then we’ll talk, if you want to talk. Or you can sleep. There’s a tent for you.”

A slave came with a basin and knelt and took off his sandals and began to wash his feet, and the water in the basin went brown at once, and the slave asked him, in a low voice and in Greek, whether he would like the water changed. Brutus said yes, please, and thanked him, and then thanked him again for the second basin, and it seemed to him afterwards that he had spent a great deal of that first quarter of an hour thanking a slave for water because it was the one thing in the tent he knew how to do.

He ate. He had not known how hungry he was. There was bread and cheese and cold meat and figs, and wine well watered, and Caesar ate a little of the bread and nothing else and talked to the secretary about grain at Larissa as if Brutus were not there, which was a kindness, and Brutus saw that it was a kindness and could do nothing about it.

At the end of the table there was a wooden box with its lid open. It was full of letters, rolled and folded and sealed, dozens of them, and a corner of one hung over the side with a seal on it that Brutus knew, a family he had dined with in Rome the winter before the war. The secretary saw him looking.

“From Pompey’s tent,” Caesar said, without turning round. “His correspondence. My people brought it in this morning very pleased with themselves.” He nodded to the secretary. “Light the brazier.”

The secretary lit it. When it had caught, he took the letters out of the box in handfuls and put them on it, one handful after another, without opening any, and the seals ran and the wax smelled like a temple, and the tent filled with a thin smoke that went out at the roof. Caesar watched it for a while. Then he went back to the grain.

Brutus sat with a fig in his hand and watched the letters burn and did not eat the fig.

Later, when the secretary had gone and the light was going long and yellow across the floor, Caesar walked him out to see where he was to sleep. They went along the lane between the tents, slowly, and Caesar talked about Polybius. He had heard that Brutus had been making an epitome; somebody in Pompey’s camp had written it home, and it had gone round Rome. Was it true that he had worked at it in the camp, every day? Even the day before?

“Most days.”

“I envy you. I can’t read on campaign. I start a page and somebody brings me a horse.” Caesar put a hand on his shoulder, lightly, as they walked, the way a man steadies a friend on a bad road. “Where do you suppose he’s gone?”

It was asked in the same voice as the question about Polybius. Brutus had been thinking about the epitome, about the passage on the Roman constitution that he had copied out twice because he could not decide where to cut it, and he answered the way he would have answered at a dinner, by thinking aloud.

He said that Pompey would not go to Syria, which had sent its legions west with his father-in-law and had nothing left to give. He said that Pompey would not go west, to Africa, not yet, because it was too far and Juba was a king who liked winners. He said that the king of Egypt was a boy, and that the boy’s father had been put back on his throne by Pompey’s friends with Pompey’s name behind it, and had borrowed the money for it in Rome and never paid it, and that the boy had an army and a fleet and a great many reasons to be grateful. He said that if he were Pompey he would go where he was owed.

He heard himself say it. The hand on his shoulder did not move, and did not grow heavier, and Caesar said, “Yes. I think so too,” and went on walking.

At the tent they had given him Brutus stopped, because it was fitting to say something, and because there was one thing he had decided in the marsh that he would say whatever happened. He said that Gaius Cassius had commanded Pompey’s ships, and was somewhere at sea, and was a better soldier than any man Pompey had, and that it would be a waste.

Caesar looked at him for a moment with great friendliness.

“Cassius has ships,” he said. “Let him bring them.” He took the hand off Brutus’s shoulder. “I’m writing to your mother tonight. Is there anything you want me to put in?”

Brutus opened his mouth to say no, and found that he was weeping. It came up from somewhere below his ribs without any warning at all, and he stood in the door of the tent in the last of the light with his muddy legs and his washed feet and wept, not loudly, and could not stop it, and was not ashamed, and at the same time was ashamed past anything he had felt that day, in the reeds or on the hill or at the brazier, because he understood that he was grateful, and that Caesar could see that he was grateful, and that Caesar was glad.

“Tell her I ate,” he said.

Caesar nodded, as if that were a perfectly sensible message, and went back along the lane towards his lists.

There was a clean tunic laid out on the bed in the tent, and a basin, and a lamp. The tunic was his size. Brutus sat on the edge of the bed and did not light the lamp, and some time after dark, still sitting up, he slept.
