# II-04: Between the Camps

POV: Laberius · Ilerda, between Caesar’s lines and Afranius’s
Date: July 49 BC by the Roman calendar (late spring by the sun)
Sources: Caes. *BC* 1.74 to 76 (checked: men ask after acquaintances and fellow-townsmen; Petreius kills those he catches; Caesarians found in the camp killed at headquarters); 1.77 (check: Afranius’s men sent back unharmed; some tribunes and centurions stay with Caesar of their own will; Petreius arming his slaves and horse, check; Afranius’s Picene origin, check). Rufrius’s coming over is this book’s proposal; Book 1 says only “next door since Ilerda”.

---

The first of them came over the ridge a little after the second hour, two men with no shields, walking slowly with their hands where everyone could see them, and the picket let them come.

Laberius was at the ditch with a mattock. The century had been digging since first light, a spur of the new line running down to the water, and the ground here was stony and full of roots, and nobody had told him what the spur was for. He straightened up and leaned on the haft and watched the two men come down the slope. They were Afranius’s men. They had the same boots as he had and the same belts, and one of them had a sunburned neck, and they stopped at the edge of the ditch and looked down into it at the men digging as if they had never seen a ditch before.

“Anyone here from Asculum?” the sunburned one said.

Nobody answered for a moment. Then a man at the far end of the spur put his mattock down and climbed out, and the two of them looked at each other, and the man from Afranius’s army said a name, and the man from the ditch said, “That’s my uncle,” and they took hold of each other by the forearms and stood there.

After that they came in tens. They came down off the ridge all along the line, from where the Pompeian camp sat on its hill with its water cut off, and Caesar’s men climbed out of the ditches to meet them, and by the fourth hour there was no line, only a field of men standing about in groups in the sun, asking for towns.

“Firmum.”

“Cupra.”

“Anyone from Auximum? My sister married in Auximum.”

Laberius stood on the lip of the ditch and listened to the names go up and down the field. Half of them were Picene. He had not thought about that. He had known that Afranius was Picene, as Pompey’s men mostly were, and that Pompey had raised legions in Picenum the way other men raise a crop, but he had not thought about what it would sound like. It sounded like the market at Asculum on a feast day. A man went by him laughing high in the nose, the way they laughed in the hill towns, and he had not heard that laugh in fifteen years.

Gaius came up from the bottom of the spur with his helmet in his hand, wiping his face.

“They want to know if we’re going to kill them,” he said. “I said probably not today. One of them’s from Interamnia. He knows my cousin. He says my cousin owes him money.”

“Does he?”

“Of course he does. Everybody’s cousin owes everybody money.” Gaius looked out over the field. “Are we stopping digging?”

“Nobody’s said.”

“Then we’re stopping.” Gaius put his helmet down on the spoil heap and went off into the crowd to find out about his cousin.

Vibius stayed. He sat on the spoil heap with his staff across his knees and watched the men from the other army the way he watched a new recruit putting on mail, waiting for the strap he would get wrong. “Their officers are coming down,” he said. “Look. Tribunes. Two of them. They’re asking for Caesar.”

“Let them ask.”

“If their tribunes come over, the war’s done here.”

Laberius did not say anything to that. He did not know how wars were done. He knew how days were done, and this one was going a way he had not seen a day go, and he did not trust it, and he could not have said why. He stood on the lip of the ditch with the sun on the back of his neck and let the field go on around him.

A big slow man came down the slope towards the spur on his own, carrying something in front of him in both hands. He came the way a man comes into a house where he has been invited to dinner, as if he had all afternoon. He stopped at the ditch and looked down at the stones and the roots and then up at Laberius.

“You’re a Cordus,” he said.

Laberius looked at him.

“From up the valley from Asculum. Above the mill, with the walnut.” The big man nodded to himself. “Your father had a lame mule he wouldn’t sell. You’ve got his face. I’d have known it at a quarter-mile.”

“The mule died.”

“I should think it did.” He held out what he was carrying. It was a small brown jar with a cloth tied over the mouth. “Olives. My wife’s mother puts them up. They came on a ship to Tarraco in the spring and I’ve carried them ever since, and I’m not carrying them up that hill again.”

Laberius took the jar because it was held out to him. It was heavy. He untied the cloth with his thumb and the smell came up, oil and fennel, and he stood there on the edge of a ditch in Spain holding it and could not for a moment think of anything at all.

“Rufrius,” the big man said. “Third cohort, over there. Centurion.” He jerked his head back towards the hill. “For what that’s worth this week.”

They sat on the spoil heap and ate the olives with their fingers and put the stones in a row on a flat rock. Vibius had three and said they were too salty and had two more. Rufrius talked. He talked about the valley, about who had married whom and who had died and which of the mills had burned, and about the hill behind him, where the water had been cut off for two days and the horses were drinking from the pack animals’ ration, and he talked about these things in the same voice, slowly, as if they were all the same kind of news. He did not ask Laberius anything about Caesar’s army. Laberius noticed that and was grateful for it.

Gaius came back with a wineskin he had not gone away with and a story about his cousin. The sun went over. Somewhere up the line men were singing. It was a song about a girl at Cingulum, and both armies knew the words.

It was the trumpets from the hill that stopped it. Not Caesar’s. They came from the Pompeian camp, sharp and close together, the call to arms, and the whole field turned and looked up at the ridge, and along the top of it there were men in a line who had not been there before, armed, with shields. Some of them were not soldiers. They were slaves, with spears, and horsemen behind them, and a man on a horse in the middle of the line was shouting.

“Petreius,” Rufrius said. He stood up. He did not hurry. He brushed the olive oil off his fingers on the front of his tunic and looked at the ridge for a while, and then he looked at the field below it, where Caesar’s men had gone up the slope with the others, as visitors, into the camp, to see a cousin or drink with a man from home. There had been a great many of them. They were coming back down the slope now, running, and not all of them were coming.

Laberius put the jar down and went to count his century.

He had them in a line on the spur in the time it takes to say it, those who were there, and he went along it touching each man on the shoulder, and Vibius went along behind him saying the names. Two were missing. One came in at a run a little after with blood down his face from a cut above the eye and said that they had been sitting in a tent drinking when Petreius’s people came through the lines, and that he had gone out under the back of it, and that the other one had not. The other one was a young man from Fanum who had gone up the hill because a man in Afranius’s army said he had known his father.

Vibius said the name again, as if it might answer the second time.

From up on the ridge there was a noise that went on for some time and then stopped. Nobody on the spur said anything about it.

Rufrius was still standing by the spoil heap. The other men from Afranius’s army who had been down in the field had mostly gone back up the hill by now, slowly, looking at the ground, because their officers were up there and their kit was up there and there was nowhere else to go. Rufrius had not gone. He stood with his big hands hanging and looked at the ridge, and then at the row of olive stones on the flat rock, and then at Laberius.

“I’ll need somewhere to sleep,” he said.

Laberius looked at Vibius. Vibius shrugged, which with Vibius was a long speech.

“There’s room by us,” Laberius said. “You’ll have to see the tribune.”

“I’ll see the tribune.” Rufrius bent and picked up the jar, which still had a third in it, and tied the cloth back over the top with great care, and held it out again. “You keep it. I’ve carried it far enough.”

That night the tent next to Laberius’s had a new man in it who snored. In the morning word came down that Caesar had found Afranius’s men who were still in his own camp, the ones who had come over to visit and could not go back, and had sent them all home unharmed, to the same hill. A few had asked to stay. Rufrius was one. He came back from the tribune’s tent at midday with his name on a tablet and nothing else in his hands, for once, and sat down outside the next tent and started taking his boots off.
