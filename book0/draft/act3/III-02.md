# The South Wind

POV: Antony · Brundisium, the crossing, and the harbour at Nymphaeum · March 48

---

Caesar’s last letter from across the water had three lines in it, and the third line said that if Antony did not come soon, Caesar would come back for him himself, in a fishing boat, alone.

Antony read it on the mole at Brundisium in the rain and gave it to Calenus, and Calenus read it and gave it back, and neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say. It was the fourth letter in a month and they had all said the same thing, more or less, and the answer to all of them was out there on the grey water at the harbour mouth: Libo’s ships, fifty of them, lying off the island in the roads where they could see every sail that came out of the harbour and run it down before it had cleared the mole. Libo had been sitting there since the winter, the way a cat sits by a hole.

“He means it,” Calenus said at last. “About the fishing boat.”

“I know he means it.”

“Then we’d better go before he does.” Calenus was a heavy, careful, grey man who did sums on his fingers and was always right, and Antony liked him in the way a man likes a good mule. “Before he drowns and we have to explain it to the Senate.”

---

They got rid of Libo first. It took a week, and it was the best week Antony had had since Ariminum.

He did it with rowing boats. He had the idea at dinner and it was a stupid idea, and he knew it was stupid, which was the best thing about it. Libo’s ships came close in to the harbour mouth every morning to look, the big ones, the quadriremes, with their oars going like the legs of beetles, and every morning they went back out again to the island. So Antony took sixty of the ships’ boats from the harbour, the small open ones, and put soldiers in them, picked men, and covered them over with hurdles and sails so that from the sea they looked like nothing at all, like boats drawn up for the night. And he put them along the inside of the mole where the quadriremes would have to pass, and sent two triremes out of the harbour in the early light as if they were going to exercise, as bait. Libo’s ships saw the triremes and came in after them. The triremes ran back. The quadriremes came in through the harbour mouth after them, rowing hard, with their men shouting. And the sixty boats came out from under their sails.

Antony was in the first boat. He should not have been. Calenus told him so afterwards, at length, with numbers. But he had stood under that sail in the half dark for two hours with the soldiers breathing round him and the water slapping on the planks, smelling of tar and wet wool and men, and when the signal went he had not been able to stay under it a moment longer. He came out roaring. He did not know what he was roaring; he never did. The soldiers came out roaring with him. And they were alongside the nearest quadrireme before its oars had stopped, with grappling irons over its rail and men going up its side like monkeys, and the quadrireme was theirs, rowers and all, inside the time it took to shout it. The others turned and ran for the open sea.

He stood on the deck of the captured ship afterwards, soaked to the waist, with his sword still in his hand and his voice gone, and the whole harbour cheering him from the mole. He had never in his life been so happy.

Then he put cavalry along the shore so that Libo could not land anywhere for water, and Libo, who had sat off Brundisium all winter on water from the island and the beaches, found that he had none, and in a few days he took his ships away down the coast. And the harbour mouth was open.

---

Antony did the sum that night with Calenus, at the table in the harbour-master’s house, with a lamp and a tablet.

Four legions. Eight hundred horse. The ships they had, which was not enough, because the ships Caesar had crossed with in the winter had come back, but not all of them; Bibulus had caught thirty on the return and burned them with their crews. Calenus did the sum on his fingers and then on the tablet and arrived at a number, and Antony did it in his head and arrived at the same number, and was secretly pleased about that for the rest of the evening. It was enough ships, if the men stood up on the decks like sheep in a pen and the horses went in the holds, and nobody was sick, and the wind was right.

“The wind,” Calenus said.

“The wind will be right.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No,” Antony said. “But Caesar’s going to come back in a fishing boat if I don’t go, so it had better be.”

---

The wind was right. It came round to the south on the third day, a good steady wind off Africa, warm, and Antony put the army into the ships in a morning and took it out past the mole and the island, where Libo’s empty anchorage was, and pointed it at Greece.

It was a long day and a longer night. The sea got up as soon as they were out of the lee of the land, and the men were sick, all of them, standing on the decks packed so close that a man who was sick was sick on the man next to him, and that man was sick in turn, and so on down the deck. The horses screamed in the holds. Antony walked up and down his ship among them, talking. He talked to the steersman about the wind and to the men about anything, about girls, about the wine they would drink in Greece, about the time Calenus fell off a horse in Gaul; he laughed, loud, at nothing, so that they could hear him laughing above the noise of the sea and the horses. He did not know if it helped. He did it because if he stopped he would have to stand still, and look at the water, and think about how many men there were on the decks of all those ships and how deep it was under them.

At dawn they saw the coast of Greece. And at noon they saw the ships coming out of Dyrrachium after them.

They were Pompey’s Rhodians, sixteen of them, galleys with full crews, built for this, coming out from the harbour where Pompey had his army and his stores; and Antony’s ships were merchantmen and transports, heavy, crowded, slow, with sick men on their decks. He watched the Rhodians come. He could see their oars. He could see, after a while, their rams, catching the light at their bows when they rose on a wave. He did the sum again, without wanting to: how far, how fast, how long. He did not like the answer. He stood at the stern and laughed and told the steersman to put on everything she would carry, and the steersman looked at him as if he were mad, and did it.

And then the wind dropped.

It dropped all at once, in the middle of the afternoon, as if somebody had shut a door. The sails went slack. The ships lost way and wallowed. And the Rhodians, who did not need the wind, came on with their oars, closer, closer, so close that Antony could hear their drums. He drew his sword. He did not know what else to do with his hands. The men on the decks were drawing theirs too, the ones who could stand.

And then the wind came back. It came back from the south-west, from the open sea, stronger than before, and it filled the sails with a bang like a door slamming, and Antony’s ships heeled over and ran before it, straight up the coast, faster than any rowers could follow. He saw a harbour opening ahead, between two headlands, a narrow mouth with calm water behind it. He did not know its name. The steersman did. *Nymphaeum*, he shouted. *Nymphaeum, three miles past Lissus, sheltered from the south*. And they went in through the narrow mouth one after another, all of them, the whole fleet, with the sails cracking, into the calm.

The wind did not stop at the mouth. It went on blowing, harder, straight onto the rocks along the coast outside the harbour, where the Rhodians were. Antony stood on the mole at Nymphaeum and watched it happen. He watched the galleys try to turn, and fail, and go onto the rocks, one and then another, with their oars snapping like reeds; he watched the men in the water. Sixteen ships. He counted them. Most of them broke up. His soldiers ran along the shore with ropes and pulled out the men from the Rhodian crews who could be reached, and brought them up the beach, and Antony said they were to be fed and sent home, because Caesar would have said so, and because he was too happy to want anybody killed.

That night in the camp they cheered him again. The legions knew what had happened: that the wind had dropped when it should have killed them and come back when it could save them, and had turned on the enemy as they watched. They cheered him because he had brought them across. He knew that it was the wind. He knew that if the wind had stayed down another quarter of an hour, the Rhodian rams would have been in among his transports and half of his army would have been on the bottom of the sea, and that there was nothing he had done or could have done about it. He knew it was luck. He let them cheer. Luck you had been given was as good as skill, if you had the sense to take it. He drank a great deal. He did not sleep.

---

In the morning they told him about the two ships.

They had been at the back of the fleet when the wind came round, and they had not made the harbour. They had been carried past it in the dark and had anchored off Lissus. One had two hundred and twenty recruits in it, new men from Italy, who had never been in a fight. The other had two hundred veterans. Pompey’s people at Lissus had sent boats out to both in the night and called across the water that if they gave themselves up nobody would hurt them.

The veterans had told them to go to the crows and run their ship onto the beach at dawn and fought their way up it and along the coast in the night, and they had come into the camp at Nymphaeum an hour ago, most of them, wet and furious and very pleased with themselves.

The recruits had believed it. They had given themselves up. They had been taken ashore and brought to Otacilius, who commanded at Lissus. And Otacilius had had them killed, all two hundred and twenty, on the beach.

Antony heard this from a centurion of the veterans, standing in front of him with a bandage round his head. He heard it all. He asked how they knew. The centurion said that a fisherman had seen it, and had come along the coast to tell them, for money. Antony asked how much. The centurion told him. Antony paid it out of his own purse, which was the first thing he had paid out of his own purse in a year, and then he went out of the tent and walked down to the beach by himself, and stood there at the edge of the water looking down the coast towards Lissus, which he could not see, for a long time.

Then he went back up to the camp and gave orders to march north to join Caesar, and was loud about it all day.
