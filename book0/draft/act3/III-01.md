# The Jokes

POV: Cicero · Pompey’s camp near Dyrrachium · January 48

---

The joke about the eagles went round the camp in a day, and by the evening Cicero had heard it told back to him three times, twice wrong.

He had made it in the morning, at the council. It had not been a council, properly; it had been Pompey’s officers standing about in the big tent while Pompey read dispatches, and the news was bad, because Caesar had crossed. Nobody had thought he would cross in winter, with Bibulus’s ships all along the coast, and he had crossed, with seven legions, in a night, and landed at some beach in Epirus that nobody had heard of, and taken Oricum and Apollonia before the week was out. And the tent was full of men explaining why this was good news. One of them, a decent man with a loud voice, had turned to Cicero with a great deal of cheerfulness and said that they should all take heart, because there were still seven eagles in Pompey’s camp.

“Excellent advice,” Cicero had said, “if we were at war with jackdaws.”

There had been a laugh. Not a large one. Two or three men near him had laughed, quickly, the way men laugh who have been waiting for somebody to say something; and the decent man had gone red and laughed too, after a moment, because he was a decent man. And Pompey had looked up from his dispatches, across the tent, and had not laughed. He had looked at Cicero for a moment with an expression Cicero could not read, and then looked down again.

By the evening the joke had gone through the camp. Cicero heard it from his own slave, who had heard it at the water-carriers’ line, where it was being told as *if we were fighting crows*, which spoiled it. He heard it from a young tribune at dinner, who told it to him as a joke Cicero would enjoy, not knowing who had made it, and who got the bird right and the number wrong. And he heard it, last, from his son, who had heard it from the cavalry, where it was being told against Pompey.

“They’re saying it at every fire,” Marcus said. He was sixteen and had been given a squadron of horse, which he was very proud of and spent his whole day with, and he had come in smelling of horses, pleased. “They think it’s the best thing anybody’s said since we got here.”

“Who’s they?”

“Everybody. The young ones.” Marcus pulled off his boots. “Is it true you said it to Pompey’s face?”

“I said it to an officer’s face. Pompey was reading.”

“They’re saying you said it to Pompey.”

Cicero did not answer. He sat on the edge of his camp bed and looked at the lamp, and thought about who had laughed in the tent, and who had told it at the fires, and who had told it so that it was against Pompey; and he found, examining his feelings with care, that he was pleased. He was ashamed of being pleased, but he was pleased. It was the first time in two months in this camp that anybody had wanted to hear anything he said.

---

He had been in the camp since the summer. He had come across from Caieta with his brother and the boys, and been received, as he had known he would be, with courtesy; Pompey had embraced him and given him a tent and asked after Terentia and Tullia. And then nothing. Pompey had not asked him to the councils. Or he had, but as a man is asked who has a right to be there, and then not asked anything. Pompey had not given him a command, which was right, because he had not wanted one, and had refused the first that was offered with a speech about his health. And nobody had asked him what he thought.

He told them anyway. That was the trouble. He could not help it. He looked at the camp, at the army that was enormous and badly drilled and full of kings from the East with their own horses and their own cooks, at the officers who spent the mornings dividing up the property of men who had stayed in Italy and the afternoons quarrelling about who would be consul when they got back; and at Pompey, who was the greatest commander alive and who seemed, somehow, to be waiting for something; and he could not stop making sentences about it. The sentences came out as jokes because jokes were the only form in which a man could say such things in that camp and still be asked to dinner. And the jokes were good. He knew they were good. That was the other trouble.

When somebody had said, at dinner, that he had come late to the camp: “On the contrary, I came too early. Nothing’s ready.” When Pompey had made a Gaul who deserted from Caesar a Roman citizen, at a parade, with a speech: “What a man. He can give a Gaul a city that isn’t his, and he can’t give us back our own.” When somebody had asked him, in front of Pompey, where his son-in-law was, meaning Dolabella, who was with Caesar: “With your father-in-law.” He had said that one before he could stop himself. It had been the best of them. Caesar had been Pompey’s father-in-law, until Julia died. The tent had gone quiet, and Pompey had laughed, after a moment, a short laugh, and said *very good*, and had not spoken to him again for three days.

He knew what they said about him. The young ones repeated the jokes and the old ones did not forgive them. He thought he knew why. It was envy. These were men who had never in their lives made a sentence anyone wanted to repeat, and who had come to this camp on the coast of Illyria to win a war, and found that the man everybody was quoting was not a general at all but an old consular with a weak stomach who did not want to fight. Of course they did not like it.

---

Cato came to his tent the next morning.

It was early. Cicero was still at the basin, with his face wet, and his slave announced Marcus Cato and Cato came in at once behind the announcement without waiting to be asked, as he did everywhere, and stood in the middle of the tent in his plain dark cloak with his arms folded. He had not cut his hair or his beard since the war began; he said that while the Republic was in mourning, so was he. He looked, Cicero thought, like a man who had been shipwrecked on purpose.

“Marcus Tullius,” Cato said. “A word.”

“Of course.” Cicero dried his face. “Sit.”

Cato did not sit. “The eagles,” he said.

“Ah.”

“It’s all over the camp. The cavalry are telling it against Pompey.” Cato’s voice was flat and harsh and did not go up or down; it was the voice he used in the Senate, for everything, as if every subject were the treasury. “You’ll say you didn’t mean it so.”

“I didn’t mean it so.”

“I don’t care how you meant it. I care how it’s told.” Cato looked at him steadily. He had very pale eyes, red at the rims from the lamp; he read at night. “I’ll say what I came to say, and then I’ll go. You should not have come.”

Cicero put the towel down.

“You were more use in Italy,” Cato said. “In your house at Formiae, saying nothing, neither for us nor against us. Every man in Italy who couldn’t decide looked at you and thought, if Cicero can wait, so can I. That was worth a legion to us. It kept them out of Caesar’s camp. It kept them waiting for us to come back.” He paused. “Now you’re here, and they’ve nobody to look at, and you’ve no command, and you won’t take one, and you sit in Pompey’s councils making jokes that his own cavalry tell against him at the fires. You’re doing us no good here. You’d have done us a great deal of good there. I’m sorry. I thought you should hear it from somebody who isn’t laughing.”

He stood a moment longer, to see if there would be an answer. There was not, or not one Cicero was prepared to give. Cato nodded, once, as if a vote had been taken, and went out.

Cicero stood in the middle of the tent with the towel at his feet.

He thought about what he might have said. He thought of several things, all of them good. He could have said that a consular’s place was where the consuls were; that he had come because honour required it, and that Cato of all men should know what honour required; that it was easy for a man who had no daughter married to the other side to talk about waiting. He thought about saying that last one for a long time. It was true, and it would have hurt, and Cato would not have shown that it hurt, and it would have gone round the camp by evening.

He did not go after Cato to say any of them.

Instead he sat down on the edge of the bed and thought, quite clearly, about the terrace at Formiae, eleven paces each way, and the sea under it, and the gulls; and about Tullia’s lamp at the water’s edge, and whether the child was still alive, because her last letter had not said; and about his own bed, in his own house, with the shutters closed against the wind. He thought about them very precisely, one after the other, as if he were making an inventory. He had not let himself do that since Caieta.

His slave came in to take the basin. Cicero said that he was not feeling well, and would not go to the council that morning, and that if anybody asked, it was his stomach.

Brutus came at noon, with a jar of honey and a book, because he had heard that Cicero was ill. He sat by the bed and asked after his health with great courtesy, and talked about Polybius for an hour, and did not once mention the eagles. Cicero was more grateful to him for that than for anything anyone had done in months. He wondered afterwards whether Brutus had heard the joke at all. He decided that Brutus had, and had thought it unfitting, and had come anyway, and had brought honey; and that this was very like Brutus, and that he would never be able to make a joke about it.
