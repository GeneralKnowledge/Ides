# Two Chairs

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Forum, the praetors’ tribunals · April 48

---

Marcus Caelius had borrowed from him twice, and paid him twice, and on the morning he set his chair down beside Trebonius’s in the Forum and offered to make every debt in Rome worth nothing, Hordeonius found that he could not dislike him as much as he wished to.

He watched him do it from the board. It was not far. The praetors’ tribunals stood at the east end of the Forum, by the Regia, and from the shade of the basilica a man could see the platforms and the chairs and the lictors in their rows without seeming to look. Trebonius had been sitting since the second hour. He was the city praetor, and Caesar’s man, a square, fair, orderly person who wrote down everything and sat very still in his chair as if somebody had told him to keep it warm, and in the months since the new year he had sat there every morning, hearing the debt cases under Caesar’s law, the arbitrations, the valuations, the appeals, in a voice so level that people at the back could not hear it and so had to come closer. Hordeonius respected him. He had watched him settle a hundred cases and had never seen him lose his temper or his place in a tablet.

Then Caelius came across the Forum with his own lictors and his own clerks and his own chair, which the clerks were carrying, and had it put down on the platform next to Trebonius’s. Not behind it. Not below it. Next to it, so close that the legs of the two chairs nearly touched. And he sat down in it, and arranged his toga, and looked out at the Forum with that bright, interested, handsome face he had, like a man at the theatre waiting for the play to begin.

“He can’t do that,” said Sosia.

“He’s a praetor. He can sit where he likes.”

“Next to the other one?”

“Next to anybody.” Hordeonius did not take his eyes off the platform. “Watch.”

---

Caelius stood up after a while and spoke. He spoke well. He had always spoken well; he had been Cicero’s pupil, years ago, and had prosecuted men and defended them and been prosecuted himself, for poisoning, at the instance of a woman he had been in love with, and had been defended by Cicero, and acquitted, and had become, for a season, the most talked-of young man in Rome. That had been eight years ago. Since then he had been tribune and aedile and run up debts that were famous even by the standards of men who stood for office, and had paid them, somehow, mostly. Hordeonius had lent him money for his aedile’s games. The games had been very good. There had been panthers. Caelius had written to Cicero in Cilicia, everybody knew, to ask him to send more.

What he said now, from the platform, in that clear quick voice, was that he would sit here every day beside the city praetor; and that any man who thought that the arbitrators had valued his house or his farm too low, or that his creditor was pressing him harder than the law allowed, might come to this chair instead of that one and appeal; and that he, Caelius, would hear him.

A murmur went over the Forum. People came closer.

Hordeonius watched who came. That was what he had come to see. He watched the faces in the crowd in front of the platform, and he knew a great many of them, because he knew most faces in the Forum that had ever owed anybody anything. And what he saw, after a quarter of an hour, made him sit back on his stool and fold his hands.

“Well?” said Sosia.

“Look at them. Who are they?”

Sosia looked. “Debtors.”

“What kind?”

Sosia looked again, longer. Then he said, slowly: “Not ours.”

They were not. They were not anybody’s, in the sense Hordeonius meant. They were the men who lived in the rented rooms up the stairs of the Subura and the Esquiline, who owed their landlords and the cookshops and the corn-dealers, small sums, sums nobody had ever thought worth taking to a praetor; they were the porters and the carters and the men without trades, and the young men of good family who had nothing but their family. They stood in front of Caelius’s chair and cheered him. But they did not come forward to appeal. They had nothing for an arbitrator to value. And the men who did, the men who owned houses and farms and who were paying their creditors in property at Caesar’s valuation, a third less than they owed, the men with something to appeal about: they were not in the crowd at all. Or they were at the back, on the edges, looking, and then going away.

“They’re satisfied,” Hordeonius said. “The ones with property. They’ve lost a third and kept two. They don’t want a quarrel. They want it done.” He shook his head, almost with sympathy. “He’s chosen the wrong debtors. The ones who’ll cheer him have nothing to save. The ones who have something to save won’t stand next to him.”

“So it’s nothing.”

“It’s a young man with debts making a noise.” Hordeonius unfolded his hands. “It will be over by the Kalends.”

---

It was not over by the Kalends.

On the Ides Caelius proposed a law. He did not wait for anybody to appeal; he simply stood up on his platform beside Trebonius, who went on hearing his cases with his eyes on his tablets as if the man next to him were a draught, and proposed to the people that every debt in Rome should be paid in six equal parts over six years, without interest. Without interest. From the day the law was passed. Every debt, on every tablet, in every board along the colonnade, with nothing to be added for the use of the money, for six years.

The Forum heard it in silence, the first time. Then there was a roar from the men in front of the platform that went all the way up the Sacred Way and into the colonnade, and Hordeonius sat at his board under it like a man under a waterfall and did a sum.

He did not need to do it on wax. He had done it before, for other laws, other tribunes, in other years. Six years without interest was the same, near enough, as giving up a third of the principal, the way money moved; more, if prices went up, as they would. It was the same loss as Caesar’s law, again, on top of Caesar’s law. He would lose two thirds of everything on the board that was not already a house in the Velabrum. And this time not quietly. This time out loud, in front of everybody, with a roar.

“Is it law?” said Sosia, at his shoulder. His voice had gone high.

“It’s a proposal.”

“Will it pass?”

“Not if the consul has any sense.” Hordeonius looked across at the platform. Trebonius had stopped writing. He was looking at Caelius, for the first time, sideways, with his stylus in his hand; and something in his face, which had been orderly all morning, had become even more orderly, the way a man’s face does when he has decided to stop being patient. “Servilius is consul. Servilius has sense. He’ll take it to the Senate, and the Senate will take Caelius’s chair away from him.” He paused. “And then Caelius will do something stupid.”

“Like what?”

“I don’t know. Something young.” Hordeonius wiped his fingers on the cloth, slowly, finger by finger. “He’s a fool, Sosia. A charming one. He paid his debts twice, so he thinks debt is a thing that can be talked out of a room. It can’t. It’s a thing that stays on the tablet until somebody pays it. He’ll find that out.”

---

Visellius came on the Kalends of May, at the second hour, as he always did, and counted his interest out on the board in stacks of ten.

The Forum was very unquiet that morning. There had been fighting in the night. Caelius had tried to pass his law by force, with the men from the Subura behind him; and they had come down into the Forum at first light with sticks, and gone for Trebonius’s tribunal, and pulled him off it, and broken his chair; and the consul had called out the soldiers, and there had been blood on the paving outside the Regia, which slaves were still washing away with buckets when Visellius came. Caelius had been suspended. The Senate had met before dawn and stripped him of his office and the consul had broken his chair too, they said, in front of him, with his own hands. Nobody knew where Caelius was.

Visellius counted. He did not look at the slaves with the buckets. His thumb squared each stack, the way it always did, and his face was what it always was, neat and narrow and attending only to the money.

“You’ve heard about the law?” Hordeonius said, when the last stack was down.

“Six years, without interest.” Visellius closed his satchel. “Yes. My tenants told me. One of them told me he’d pay me in six years. I told him the law hadn’t passed. He said it would. I said that when it did, I’d let him know.” He looked up. “It won’t pass, will it?”

“No.”

“No. I didn’t think so.” Visellius stood. He hesitated a moment, which was unlike him. “I’d have paid anyway,” he said. “If it had. Six years without interest. I’d have paid you the interest anyway. I wanted you to know that.”

Hordeonius looked at him. He found, to his surprise, that he did not know what to say. Nobody had ever said such a thing to him across the board in thirty years; it was not the kind of thing that was said across a board. He found also that something in his chest which had been tight since the Ides, without his noticing, had gone loose.

“Thank you,” he said, which was all he could find.

Visellius nodded and went away across the wet paving, between the slaves with their buckets, with his short careful steps. Sosia counted the stacks again.

“He means it,” Sosia said, a little puzzled. “He’d have paid.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Hordeonius did not answer for a while. He watched Visellius go. Then he said, “Because he wants to be the kind of man who pays,” and drew the next tablet towards him, and opened it, and found his place.
