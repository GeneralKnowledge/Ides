# The Tables

POV: Laberius · Pompey’s camp above the plain of Pharsalus · 9 August 48

---

There was myrtle over the doors of the tents.

That was the first thing Laberius saw, going in over the rampart of Pompey’s camp in the middle of the afternoon with what was left of his century behind him: green branches of myrtle, the leaves still fresh, tied in bunches over the doorways of the tents along the first street, the way women at home tied them over the door for a wedding. He stopped and looked at them. The men behind him stopped too. There was nobody to fight. The rampart had been held for a little, by Thracians and some auxiliaries, and then not; and the men who had been holding it had gone out of the back of the camp and up into the hills behind, and the camp was empty, and quiet, and full of myrtle.

“What’s that for?” said somebody.

Nobody answered him. Laberius walked on, slowly, down the first street, with his sword out, because he had not been told to put it away.

The tents on the first street were officers’ tents. He knew that by the size of them. They were bigger than any tent he had ever slept in, made of good leather, some of them dyed; one of them was striped, red and white. The flaps were tied back. He looked into the first one as he passed, and then stopped, and looked again.

There was a table in it. A dining table, a real one, of polished wood, with three couches round it as in a house; and on the couches there were cushions, and on the table there was a cloth, and on the cloth there were cups and dishes and a big mixing bowl, all silver, laid out for a dinner. Laid out. Waiting. There was food on the dishes: bread, and olives, and something under a silver cover; and the wine was in the bowl, already mixed. The couches had been strewn with flowers. The flowers had gone limp in the heat.

He stood in the doorway of the tent and looked at it.

“Hercules,” said Gaius, behind him, very quietly.

Laberius went on to the next tent. It was the same. Table, couches, silver, flowers. There was a tent beyond it with a turf bank built along one wall and ivy trained over the bank, so that whoever had lain on the couch inside could look at a little green garden while he ate; and the ivy had been watered that morning, he could see it, the earth round the roots still dark. He went on down the street. Every tent. Silver on every table. Dinner laid in every tent, the cups set out, the wine mixed, the cushions plumped, for men who had gone out in the morning to win a battle and come back to dinner, and had not come back.

He found that he was angry. He did not know at whom. He stood in the middle of the street in Pompey’s camp with his sword in his hand and his men behind him and the myrtle over his head, and he was so angry that he could hardly see. He thought of the chara root. He thought of four months of it, pounded on a stone, and the loaves going over the valley in the evening, and the horses dying on the other side, and his belt on the last hole; and he thought of these men sitting in these tents all those four months, under this ivy, on these cushions, drinking out of silver, while their horses died outside and his men starved. And then this morning, getting up, and having their slaves lay the tables, and mix the wine, and strew the flowers on the couches, for the dinner after. Because they had been sure. They had been so sure that they had put out the cups.

It was an insult. It was the worst insult he had ever been offered, and the men who had offered it had not even known he was there.

---

A tribune came down the street on a horse, shouting. The general’s order: nobody was to touch anything. Nobody was to stop. Pompey’s men had gone up into the hills behind the camp, a whole army of them, and they would come down again, or make a stand up there, if they were given time; the general wanted them followed now, before dark, before they could think, and anybody who stopped to plunder would be dealt with. The tribune said it three times, riding down the street, and the men stood back from the tent doorways to let him past and watched him go.

Laberius turned to his century.

“You heard.”

They had heard. They were looking at the tents. He saw the young man from Firmum, the one from the wall at Corfinium, standing in front of the striped tent looking at the silver with his mouth a little open, as a child looks at a fire. He saw Vibius, behind them, with his staff, not looking at the tents at all. Looking at the men.

“Form up,” Laberius said. “We’re going up the hill.”

They formed up. He went along the line and counted them, touching each man on the shoulder, and they were all there; and while he was counting, while his back was to the tents and the men were looking at him and not at the street, he went into the striped tent.

He did not decide to. He went in, as he had gone out of the gate at Dyrrachium, without deciding. The cloth was cool and dim and smelled of crushed flowers and wine. He went to the table and picked up the nearest cup. It was small, a drinking cup, with two handles and a pattern round the side of men and horses, and it was heavier than it looked, much heavier, so heavy in his hand that it surprised him. He put it inside his tunic, against his chest, under the mail. It was cold.

Then he went out again and the century was standing in line, waiting, and Vibius was looking at him. Vibius looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked away, at the hill, and said, “Ready, centurion,” in his ordinary voice.

---

They went out of the back of the camp and up into the hills after Pompey’s army until it got dark.

They did not fight. The Pompeians had gone up onto a hill with no water on it, a great many of them, and Caesar’s men dug a line round the bottom of the hill in the dusk, as they had dug round Pompey’s army all spring, and the Pompeians on the top looked down at the ditch being dug and knew what it meant. In the morning they came down and gave themselves up. Laberius was not there; his cohort had been sent along the hills to the east, after a smaller party that had gone that way in the night, towards Larissa.

It was there, on a slope above a stream, in the early morning, that they found Domitius.

He had been killed in the night, running. The horsemen had caught him on the slope. There were five or six bodies there, Pompeian officers, by their kit, who had come out of the camp at the back when it fell and tried for the hills on foot, and had not been fast enough, because they were old, or heavy, or had not run anywhere in years. Domitius was the heaviest. He was lying on his face. Somebody had turned him over before Laberius got there, and taken his ring, and his cloak, and gone through his belt, and left him.

Laberius knew him by the face. He had seen it at Corfinium, in the snow, swollen, walking behind a cart of silver. It was swollen again now, differently.

He looked at the shoes. He did not mean to; it was what he looked at. They were the same kind of shoes. Senator’s shoes, red, with the straps going up the calf, and the little ivory crescent. They were dusty now instead of wet, and one of the straps had broken, and the sole of the left one was coming away from the upper at the toe, as if he had been running on rocks for a long time. Laberius had seen those shoes go back into Corfinium through the gate. He had stood in the cordon at Corfinium and watched them pick their way across the frozen ground on their way back to freedom, and their money, and Pompey, and this.

“Who’s that?” said one of the new men.

“Domitius,” said Vibius. “Lucius Domitius. The general let him go at Corfinium.”

“And he came here?”

“He came here.”

The new man looked at the body for a while. “Well,” he said at last, “he won’t do that again.”

Somebody laughed. Laberius did not. He stood on the slope above the stream in the early sun and felt the cup against his chest, under the mail, where it had been all night. It had gone warm. He thought about taking it out and throwing it in the stream. He did not. He did not know why not. He thought about the six chests at Corfinium going back through the gate on the cart; and about the tables in the tents, and the myrtle; and he could not make it come out into anything, any of it. It was too big. It was like the two hundred thousand at Scaeva’s shield.

They left Domitius on the slope for the burial parties and went on east, towards Larissa. In the evening, at the halt, Laberius took the cup out and looked at it by the fire, the men and the horses going round it, and then put it in the bottom of his pack, under his spare tunic, wrapped in a rag. He did not show it to Gaius. He did not drink from it.
