# Chara

POV: Laberius · Dyrrachium, Caesar’s lines · April to June 48

---

The root was called chara, and the men who had been in Gaul said it grew there too and the Gauls fed it to pigs, and Laberius made the best bread out of it in the legion.

He had not known he would. Nobody had known what to do with it at first. Men from another legion had found it, digging a ditch: a long pale root like a thick finger, all along the bottom of the trench where the spades went through, and one of them who had been a farmer had pulled one up and bitten it and said it tasted of nothing much, which was more than could be said of anything else they had. That was in the spring, by the sun, when the army had been digging round Pompey for a month and the corn in the fields was green and the barley was all eaten, and the beans, and the mules had started to go thin. After that every century in the lines was digging for it.

Laberius had tried it raw and boiled and roasted in the ashes, and it was bad all three ways. Then he had watched a woman in a village behind the lines, an old Greek woman with no teeth, pound it on a stone with a little water until it was a paste, and mix the paste with goat’s milk, and pat it out flat on her hands, and bake it on a hot stone by the fire; and he had stood there and watched her do it four times, and then he had gone back to the century and done it himself. It came out grey and heavy and it tasted of earth. But it held together. It was bread. A man could put it in his pouch in the morning and take it out at noon and it was still bread.

“You’ve got the touch,” said Gaius, with his mouth full. “It’s like eating a roof tile. But a good roof tile.”

“It’s the milk. You have to get the milk right.”

“Where’s the milk from?”

“Goats.”

“Whose goats?”

“Ours,” said Vibius, without looking up, and that was the end of that.

---

They were digging. They had been digging since the winter and they would be digging, Laberius thought, until they died, and then somebody would dig for them. Pompey’s army sat on its hills by the sea with its back to Dyrrachium and its ships in the harbour, and Caesar’s army was digging a line round it. Seventeen miles, men said. A ditch and a bank and forts on every hill, from the sea on one side round to the sea on the other, so that Pompey would be shut in with the sea at his back and nothing to eat but what his ships could bring. It was a stupid thing to do. Everybody said so, cheerfully. Caesar’s army was smaller than Pompey’s, and hungrier, and Caesar was trying to besiege it, which was like a dog trying to besiege a bull. But the line went on getting longer, a little every day, and every day the Pompeian camp inside it got a little smaller, and the hills outside it got a little barer, because Pompey’s cavalry had eaten them.

Laberius liked the digging. He would not have said so. But it was a thing you could see, at the end of a day, how far you had got; and the ground here was good digging, stony on top and then clay, and the clay held its shape. His century had dug three hundred paces of ditch and bank in a month, and a fort at the end of it on a little hill with a palisade and a tower, and he walked along it in the evenings with his hands behind his back and looked at it.

From the top of the tower he could see the Pompeian lines. They were close, here; a long javelin-throw, no more, across a little valley with a stream in it. He could see the men on the other side, digging too, because Pompey was digging his own line inside Caesar’s to keep Caesar from getting closer, and so there were two lines, a long one and a short one, with men on both, shouting. He could see their horses. That was the thing he looked at most. Pompey had a great deal of cavalry, thousands of horses, and they were shut in on a few miles of hill with the grass all gone and the streams dammed and turned off by Caesar’s engineers, and they were dying. He could see it. He could see them standing in their lines with their heads down and their ribs showing, and the men going along the lines with leaves, with branches stripped off trees, with anything; and in the mornings he could see the carcasses being dragged out on ropes, and the crows coming.

Laberius had been hungry in Gaul and in Spain. He knew about hunger. But he had never seen it on the other side before, and he found that he watched it the way he would have watched a race. Their horses were dying. His men had bread.

---

It was Gaius who thought of throwing it.

They were on the tower one evening, the two of them and Rufrius, who had come along from his own century’s stretch of the line to borrow salt, and the men on the Pompeian side were shouting across the valley the way they did every evening, insults mostly, and something about Caesar’s army eating grass. One of them shouted that Caesar’s men were so hungry they were eating their own boots.

“Hold this,” Gaius said, and gave Laberius his helmet. Then he took a loaf of the chara bread out of his pouch, a whole one, grey and flat, and stood up on the parapet of the tower, and threw it.

It went across the valley in a long slow curve, turning over and over, and landed in the Pompeian ditch at the feet of the man who had been shouting. The man looked at it. He picked it up. He looked at it some more. Then he looked across at the tower, and Gaius shouted, quite loud, in his best parade voice:

“That’s what we eat! When it’s gone we’ll eat Pompey!”

There was a pause on the other side. Then somebody laughed, and somebody else, and the man with the loaf held it up over his head so the others could see it, and turned it over, and broke a piece off, and smelt it, and did not eat it.

The next evening the whole line was throwing bread. Every century along the front had a man on the bank with a loaf, and the loaves went across the valley one after another like stones from a sling, and the men threw them shouting, and the Pompeian side caught them, or did not, and held them up. Laberius did not throw any. He stood on his tower and watched. He felt something he did not recognise for a while, and then did. He was proud of it. Of the bread. Of his bread, going over in the evening light in a long curve, turning, with the whole army behind it shouting. He did not say so to anybody.

Rufrius, beside him, said: “They’ll stop it.”

“Who will?”

“Their officers. If I was Pompey I’d stop it. I’d tell the men it was all a trick, there was no bread, Caesar’s men were eating grass.” Rufrius watched a loaf go over. “I’d say anyone found with one would be flogged.”

“Why?”

Rufrius looked at him, slowly, as if surprised to be asked. “Because if I was over there,” he said, “with my horse dying, and somebody threw me that, I’d think: these men will eat roots out of the ground to get at me. And then I’d want to go home.” He picked up the salt he had come for. “I was over there, Cordus. On a hill. Last summer.”

They did stop it. A week later the word came across the lines from a deserter that Pompey had forbidden the loaves to be shown, and had said in front of his officers that Caesar’s men were not soldiers but wild beasts. The men on Caesar’s side were delighted with that. They called themselves the wild beasts for the rest of the summer. Gaius had it painted on his shield, in red, until Vibius made him scrape it off.

---

Laberius did not eat much of his own bread.

He had found, after the first month, that there was never quite enough of the root for the whole century, however much they dug; and that if he gave out the loaves himself, in the morning, he could give out one fewer than there were men, and nobody would count, because nobody counted the centurion. So he did. He did it every morning. He went along the line with the basket and gave every man his loaf and put the basket down empty at the end and said he had eaten his already, by the fire, early. Nobody asked when. He tightened his belt a hole in the first month and another in the second, and he did it at night, in the tent, in the dark, so that Gaius would not see.

Gaius saw. Gaius said nothing about it for six weeks, and then one morning, walking back along the bank from the latrine, he said in a perfectly ordinary voice, looking at the sea:

“Your belt’s on the last hole.”

“It stretches.”

“It’s leather. It doesn’t stretch. It shrinks.” Gaius kept walking. “There’s a man in the next cohort who’ll make you a new hole for an as. He’s got an awl.”

“I’ve got an awl.”

“Then use it,” Gaius said, “on yourself, or on the loaves,” and walked off along the bank, and that evening there was an extra loaf in Laberius’s place by the fire, and he did not know where it had come from, and did not ask.

---

At midsummer, by the sun, there was the fighting at the forts. Six fights in one day, along the line, wherever Pompey could find a weak place; and at one of the forts in the middle, which Laberius could see from his tower, a single cohort held off a whole legion for most of a day with their bank and their stones and their javelins. When it was over, they said, there was not a man in the fort who was not wounded.

The general came along the line two days later and had the centurion of that fort brought out in front of the army. His name was Scaeva. He was a broad, quiet man with a bandaged head and one eye gone, and they brought his shield out with him, and held it up.

Laberius went with the rest to look at it afterwards, where it hung on a post outside the general’s tent. There was a queue. He waited his turn. When he got to the front he stood and looked at it for a long time, at the wood and the leather and the iron boss, and he counted the holes, the way he counted anything, because he could not help it. He got to a hundred and twenty and stopped, because there was a man behind him.

“Two hundred thousand,” said the man behind him, low. “That’s what the general gave him. Sesterces. And made him first centurion of the legion. On the spot. Two hundred thousand.” He breathed out. “Two hundred thousand for a shield.”

Laberius looked at the holes. He thought about what two hundred thousand sesterces was. He could not. It was not a sum, it was a place, like Rome; you could not see the whole of it from anywhere.

“It’s a good shield,” he said, and went back to his tower.
