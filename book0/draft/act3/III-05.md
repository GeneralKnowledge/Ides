# Rumour

POV: Nysa · Rome, the chief priest’s house on the Sacred Way · May 48

---

The fighting in the Forum started before light and was over by the second hour, and Nysa heard all of it from the roof, where she had gone to take in the washing.

She had not meant to listen. The washing had been put out the evening before on the lines strung across the flat part of the roof over the kitchen, the mistress’s linen and the table-cloths, and there had been rain in the night, and the cook had sent her up before it got light to see whether it was wet. It was wet. She was taking it down anyway, to bring it in to the fire, when the noise began. It came up from the bottom of the Sacred Way, from the Forum, which she could not see from the roof because the Regia was in the way, and the Vestals’ house: a lot of men shouting at once, and then a crash, like timber breaking, and then more shouting, and the sound of running feet on stone going in all directions at once, like a jar of beans spilt.

She stood with a wet tablecloth in her arms and listened. She could not make out any words. She heard a trumpet, once, and horses, and then the shouting changed to something lower and steadier, and then it began to die away.

When she came down with the washing Chreste was at the bottom of the ladder waiting for her, white.

“Did you see?”

“I couldn’t see. The Regia’s in the way.”

“Dama says they’ve killed the praetor.”

“Which praetor?”

Chreste did not know. Neither did Dama, when they found him, sitting on the flour bin with his knees up and his eyes very bright. He was nine now and thought he was a man. He said that a praetor had been killed, or nearly, and that his chair had been broken, and that soldiers had come with swords and there was blood all over the paving outside the Regia and slaves were washing it with buckets. He had seen the buckets. He had gone down to the corner and looked.

“You went out?” said Nysa.

“Only to the corner.”

“Phileros said nobody goes out.”

“Phileros was asleep.” Dama looked at her. “Are you going to tell him?”

Nysa thought about it. “No,” she said. “But don’t do it again.” And she took the washing to the kitchen fire and hung it on the rack, the mistress’s linen in the warmest place and the tablecloths further off, and counted it as she went, because she had to know that none of it had blown off the roof in the night. None had. Twenty-two pieces.

---

The story came into the house in pieces over the next week, the way stories did, by the side door, with the fish and the bread and the water-carrier, and by the evening of the seventh day the kitchen had most of it.

The praetor who had been pulled off his chair had not been killed. That was the first thing. He had been knocked down and his chair broken, but he was alive, and he was Caesar’s man, and he was sitting again the next morning on a new chair with soldiers round the platform. It was the other praetor who had done it, the young one with the handsome face, who had wanted to let everyone off their debts for six years. The Senate had taken his office away from him. And then he had left Rome in the night, and gone south, and nobody knew where.

Then, a few days later, that he had gone to meet somebody. A man called Milo, who had been in exile in Gaul for years, and had come back across the sea to Italy with a band of gladiators and herdsmen, to raise the south. The cook knew who Milo was. Everybody over thirty knew who Milo was, the cook said. He was the man who had killed Clodius on the Appian Way, the year the Senate house burned.

“Who’s Clodius?” said Nysa.

The cook looked at her as if she had asked who Jupiter was. Then she remembered that Nysa never listened to anything that happened outside the house, and she sighed and told her: a nobleman who had made himself the people’s man, and had led gangs in the streets, and had been very handsome; and his widow was a woman called Fulvia, who had married Curio afterwards; and Curio was dead in Africa, the summer before last, with his head cut off. The cook told it all in one breath, gutting a fish, as if it were one story, and perhaps it was.

At the end of the week Dama came in from the baker’s to say that Milo was dead. He had been besieging some town in the south, and had gone up close under the wall, and somebody on the wall had dropped a stone on him. And the young praetor, Caelius, was dead too, at Thurii, a long way south, killed by some of Caesar’s horsemen, Gauls and Spaniards, who were stationed there and whom he had tried to bribe.

The kitchen was quiet for a moment.

“Well,” said the cook. “That’s that, then.” And she went on with the fish.

---

The mistress did not come down to the kitchen; she never did. But Nysa saw her nails.

She saw them because she was on the trays again, and because the mistress had started eating with her hands in her lap, under the table, when she ate at all. And one evening at the end of the month, when Nysa came in to take the tray down, the mistress was sitting by the window with her hands open on her knees, palms up, looking at them. There were marks across the middle of each palm. Four small red curved marks, in a row, deep enough that one of them had bled. Nysa knew what they were. She had seen them on a girl in the wool room once, who had been afraid of something for a long time.

The mistress saw her see them. She closed her hands.

“The tray,” she said.

“Yes, lady.”

Nysa took the tray. There was half a loaf on it, and the eggs untouched.

That was the night the news came. Not by letter. By the street. It came up the Sacred Way after dark, from the Forum, where it had come from the Ostia road, where it had come off a ship; and by the time it reached the side door of the house it had been told so many times that it had worn smooth, like a step. The water-carrier brought it. He told it to the porter at the side door, and the porter told it to Phileros, and Phileros, who had been drinking, said it out loud in the passage before he could stop himself, and every slave in the back of the house heard.

Caesar was beaten. Pompey had shut him in on the coast of Greece, the way you shut a fox in a hole, and starved him, and his army was eating grass and roots, and his men were deserting to Pompey in hundreds; and Pompey had come out of his lines and fallen on him, and the army was broken, and Caesar was dead.

---

Nysa lay awake on her pallet under the stairs with Chreste breathing beside her and listened to the house not sleep.

Nobody slept. She could hear them. She heard Phileros going round the doors, and then going round them again, and again. She heard the steward’s voice, low, in his room, and another voice answering, and the scrape of a chest being moved. She heard, from the back of the house, from the mistress’s rooms, nothing at all, which was worse.

She thought about what would happen. She did not mean to; it came on its own. If the master was dead, the house was not his house any more. It was the chief priest’s house, and there would be a new chief priest, and he would come with his own household. The mistress would go back to her father’s. And the slaves of the house would be sold, or given, or sent to the farms, to Labici and the others, wherever was cheapest; and nobody would ask where she would rather be. She thought about Labici, where Chreste’s cousin had been sent for breaking a jug.

She thought about it very carefully, the way she counted jars, all of it, one thing and then the next, until she had got to the end. Then she lay there.

In the morning there was a letter.

It came at the second hour, by a courier on a horse, from Brundisium, and it had the master’s own seal on it, and the courier had ridden through the night. The steward took it to the mistress. Nysa was in the passage with the lamps and she heard nothing through the door, nothing at all, for a long time, and then she heard the mistress laugh. Once. It was a strange sound. It was not like any sound Nysa had ever heard her make. Then the steward came out with his face all broken open and said to the passage at large that it was not true, none of it, the master was alive and well and the letter was nine days old and said that Pompey was shut in, not him, and that it was a matter of time.

The house came apart with relief. The cook cried. The girls in the wool room cried. Phileros went and got the good wine up from the cellar and nobody stopped him.

Nysa went out to the bench by the side door with her basket and sat down and trimmed wicks.

She found that her hands were shaking and she could not cut straight. She put the scissors down and sat with her hands in her lap. She was glad the master was alive. Of course she was. Everyone was. But what she felt, sitting on the bench in the cold with the lamps, was not about the master. It was about being sold, and Labici, and the new chief priest’s household, none of which was going to happen now; and it was so large and so selfish that she was ashamed of it, and she sat with it for a while until it got smaller, and then she picked up the scissors again and went on with the wicks.

Chreste came out and sat beside her and said, “Weren’t you frightened?”

“No,” said Nysa. “Were you?”

“Terribly.” Chreste put her head on Nysa’s shoulder. “I thought we’d be sold.”

“So did I,” said Nysa, before she could stop herself, and then she said, “Hold this,” and gave Chreste the lamp.
