# The Left

POV: Antony · the plain below Pharsalus, Caesar’s left wing · 9 August 48

---

The horse had a white mouth. That was what Antony would remember, when he was asked about Pharsalus, and he was asked about it for the rest of his life: the mouth of the horse he was on, a big grey he had taken off a Gaul at Dyrrachium, foaming white round the bit from the moment he got up on it in the morning, so that by the time the trumpets went there were strings of foam hanging from the bridle and the grey was shaking its head to be rid of them and spattering his knees.

He did not remember much else in order. Nobody did, afterwards, who had been on the left.

---

He remembered the morning. The army had been going to march; the tents were struck and the baggage loaded, because the general had decided that Pompey would never come down off his hill and that they must go and find food somewhere else. And then the scouts had come in at a gallop and said that Pompey’s army was coming down onto the plain, all of it, in line, and the general had stood in the middle of the half-struck camp and said, quite loud, so that everybody heard: *We must put off the march. Now’s the time for battle, which we’ve always wanted.* And then the shouting, and the running for kit, and the baggage unloaded, and the whole army going out of the gate onto the plain in the bright early sun with its heart going like a drum.

He remembered being told the left. He had known he would have it; he had had it all summer. The general had come along the line on foot, with his staff, and stopped by Antony’s horse and looked up at him and said, *Marcus, the left; keep your flank on the river, and don’t go forward till I send*. And had gone on along the line to the right, where the Tenth was, and where Pompey’s horse was massed across the plain in a great dark block, thousands of them, so many that the ground looked as if it were moving.

He remembered the river. It was on his left, the Enipeus, a small river with steep banks, and the general was right: no horse could come round that flank. That was why the general had given it to him. It was the safe end. He had known that, and minded it, and had not said so.

He remembered the legions in front of him: two of them, the weakest in the army after Dyrrachium, so short of men that they had been put together and stood as one, the ranks thin. He rode along behind them in the morning and looked at the backs of their necks. Sunburned. Thin. Men who had eaten roots. He did a sum, riding, without wanting to: how many men, how many ranks, how many paces of front, how long they would hold if Pompey’s right came at them in earnest. It was not a good sum. He laughed, loud, at something a centurion said, and rode on.

---

Then the trumpets, and everything at once.

He did not see the beginning. He was too far left. He heard it: a roar from the centre and the right, and then the long rattle of javelins, like rain on a roof, and then a sound that went on and on and that he had heard before only at Alesia, a sound like a forest falling down. His own legions went forward. He had not sent them; nobody had; they went because the line beside them went, at a run, shouting, and then, halfway across the plain, they stopped.

He did not understand why they stopped. He rode up behind them shouting and then he saw. Pompey’s line had not come forward. It was standing. It had been told to stand, to let Caesar’s men run the whole width of the plain and arrive tired and broken up, and Caesar’s men had seen it, the centurions had seen it, and they had stopped in the middle of the plain of their own accord, in the open, under the enemy’s eyes, and got their breath, and closed their ranks. And then they went forward again. Walking, then running, then the javelins, then the swords. He would think about that later: that nobody had told them to. He would tell it at dinner for years as if it had been his idea.

After that there was only the left.

It was a fight against men he could see. Pompey’s right wing was in front of him, legions from Cilicia and Spain, heavy men in good kit who had not been eating roots, and they held, and his two thin legions went into them and held too, and for what seemed a very long time the line did not move either way. He rode up and down behind it. That was his work. He rode up and down behind the line on the grey with the white mouth, shouting, and wherever the line bent he was there, shouting at the men to close up, shouting at the centurions, shouting names he knew, and some he made up. He could not hear himself. He could hear nothing but the noise. He saw men fall in front of him, close enough to touch with his foot; he saw a centurion of the second line, an old man, take a javelin through the thigh and pull it out and stand on the leg; he saw a boy, a recruit, step back out of the line with no face, quite slowly, and sit down. He saw the grey’s mouth. He did not see the right.

He did not see Pompey’s cavalry come across the plain in their thousands at the general’s right wing, at the Tenth; did not see Labienus leading them; did not see them go round the end of the line, and meet the six cohorts the general had hidden behind it, and the six cohorts come out from behind the line with their javelins held short, not thrown, jabbing upwards at the faces of the riders, at the eyes, so that the young men of good family on Pompey’s horses put their hands up over their faces and turned and rode away, all of them, the whole great block of them, back across the plain and up into the hills. He was told all of that later. At the time he knew only that the noise from the right had changed. That something had gone over there, like a dam going. And then that the line in front of him was moving.

It was moving forward. His own men. A step, and another. The heavy legions in front of them were looking over their shoulders. Then they were not looking; they were going. He saw the moment it happened, the moment the Cilicians stopped being a line and became men, separate, each one turning, each one alone; and he felt it go up through him from the grey’s back like heat. He shouted. He did not know what. He put the grey forward through his own ranks, through the gap where the centurion with the thigh was still standing, and the men round him shouted and came with him, and they went across the ground where Pompey’s right had been and up the long slope beyond, towards the enemy’s camp, with the dead under the horse’s feet, and nobody in front of them at all.

---

At the top of the slope, by the ditch of Pompey’s camp, he reined in, because the grey had stopped answering.

He sat there. The grey’s head was down, blowing. Its mouth was white to the cheek-strap and there was blood in the foam, from the bit, where he had hauled on it. He looked down at it for a long time. He was breathing as hard as the horse. Below him, the whole plain was covered: with men running, and men lying, and horses without riders going round in circles, and the standards of his own legions coming up the slope behind him, a little ragged, and somewhere on the far side, by the hills where the right had been, more running, more dust.

He had won. He had held the left and then he had broken what was in front of him and come up the slope at the head of it, first, on the grey, and here he was at the ditch of Pompey’s camp with nobody in front of him. It was his, this end of it. He sat there with the grey under him and the ditch in front, and the noise of his own men coming up behind him, cheering his name.

---

The general came up the slope an hour later, on foot, with the Tenth, and stood at the edge of Pompey’s camp and looked down at the plain.

Antony was beside him. He had got off the grey and given it to a groom and was standing there in his armour, filthy, with his voice gone. The general did not look at him. He was looking at the plain. The light was beginning to change; it was the afternoon, and the dead lay on the flat ground in rows where the lines had been, and in heaps where they had broken, and the soldiers were already going among them.

There was a centurion of the Tenth among the dead, men said, an old soldier recalled from his farm for the war, named Crastinus, who had been first in the whole army to charge, at the head of a hundred and twenty volunteers, and had said to the general as he went that he would earn the general’s thanks that day, alive or dead. They had found him with a sword through his mouth. The general had asked to see him. He had already been carried up.

“They would have it so,” the general said.

He said it to nobody. Antony heard it because he was standing at his shoulder. The general was looking at the plain, at the rows, at the Roman dead, with an expression Antony had never seen on his face before and did not see again, a look of great tiredness and something under the tiredness that was almost anger.

“They would have it so,” he said again. “After everything I’d done, they’d have condemned me, if I hadn’t come to my army for help.” He turned away from the plain. He saw Antony, then, and his face changed, and became the face Antony knew, quick and courteous and amused. “Marcus. You held.”

“We went forward.”

“So I saw.” The general looked at him a moment longer, as he had at Ariminum, with that whole attention. “Go and see to your horse,” he said. “It’s bleeding.”

And he went on into Pompey’s camp, with the Tenth behind him, to see what had been left on the tables.
