# The Old Camp

POV: Laberius · Dyrrachium, the southern end of the lines · July 48

---

Afterwards Laberius could not have said when he started running. He could say when he stopped. It was at a stream, a little muddy stream between two banks of reeds, half a mile from where he had been, and he stopped because he fell into it, and when he got up out of the water on his hands and knees he was by himself.

He knelt there. He had lost his helmet. He had his shield still, on his left arm, the strap cutting in; he did not remember holding on to it. His sword was in its scabbard. He had not drawn it. The water ran off his face and his mail and he looked at the reeds on the other bank and listened, and what he heard was the noise behind him, a long way off now, over the low ground: shouting, and trumpets, and horses, and men running, hundreds of them, all over the plain between the old camp and the sea.

He did not know which way he had come. He knew which way he had been facing when it began, and it was not this way.

---

It had begun well. That was what he kept coming back to, later. It had been a good plan, as far as he understood plans, which was not far. There was an old camp on the low ground near the sea, at the south end of the lines, which Caesar’s men had built in the spring and left; and Pompey’s men had got into it after the fighting at the shore, and were using it, a legion of them, men said. And the general had seen that the legion in the old camp was a long way from the rest of Pompey’s army, and could be cut off; and he had taken thirty-three cohorts, all he could spare, and gone round by the woods in the early morning, and come at the camp from the side where nobody was looking.

Laberius’s century had been in the left-hand column. They had gone in over the rampart of the old camp at a run, and it had been easy, much easier than he had thought; the Pompeians inside had been eating, some of them, and had thrown down their bread and run, and the column had gone through the gate and across the camp and up to the second rampart, the inner one, and there had been fighting at the inner rampart, and they had been winning.

And then it had gone wrong, and he did not know how.

Something about the right-hand column. They had gone the wrong way, men said afterwards; they had followed a wall, an old wall that ran out from the camp to the river, thinking it was the camp’s rampart, and gone along it looking for a gate, and got further and further from everybody else. Laberius had not seen it. He had been at the inner rampart, with his shield up, and Gaius beside him, and Vibius behind with the staff shouting, and the Pompeians on the rampart throwing everything they had down at them, and it had been hard, but it had been going. Then there had been a noise from the right, a different noise. Then horses. Pompey’s cavalry, coming down from the hills behind, and then Pompey’s legions, five of them, men said, coming to help the legion in the camp. And the right-hand column, out by the river, with a wall between it and everybody else, had seen them come and had tried to get back over the wall and could not, because the wall was high, and men had begun to jump off it, and some of them had fallen into the ditch and been trodden on by the men behind.

And then everybody was running.

That was all he knew. That was all he would ever know, he thought. One moment the century was at the inner rampart in a line with its shields up, and the next moment the men on his right were not there, and then the men on his left, and he turned to see where they had gone, and they were going back across the camp towards the gate, fast, all of them, not looking back, as if somebody had called them. And he went with them. He did not decide to. He did not remember deciding anything. He remembered the gate, and men jammed in it, pushing; and the ditch outside, full of men; and climbing over somebody in the ditch, a man on his back with his mouth open; and then the open ground, and the noise, and his legs going, and then the stream.

He stood up in the stream. The water was to his knees. He waded out of it on the far side and stood among the reeds and tried to think what he was supposed to do.

---

He found the century in the evening, or what had got back of it, in the main lines, behind the big ditch, sitting on the ground in the place where their tents were. Vibius was there, with his staff, and a gash on his forearm tied up in a rag, counting. Rufrius was there, from the next century, sitting with his back to a tent pole and his eyes shut. Gaius was there. Laberius saw him from a long way off and stopped walking for a moment, and then started again.

“Fifty-one,” said Vibius, when he got there. He did not look up. “Fifty-two, with you.”

“How many went out?”

“Seventy-four.”

Laberius stood there. He wanted to ask who. He did not. Vibius would tell him in the morning, from the tablet, name by name, and he would have to listen then; he did not want to listen now. He sat down on the ground beside Gaius. Gaius had a cut over one eye that had bled down his face and dried and he had not washed it off.

“Where were you?” Gaius said.

“I don’t know.”

“I looked round and you weren’t there.”

“I was in the camp. Then I was in a stream.” Laberius looked at his hands. “I don’t know how I got from one to the other.”

Gaius did not say anything for a while. Then he said, not looking at him: “Nor do I. Nor does anybody.” He touched the cut over his eye. “There’s a man in the next legion says he saw the general. In the middle of it. Out on the plain, on foot, grabbing the standard-bearers as they went past, trying to turn them round. He says he got hold of one and the man hit him with the standard and kept running.” Gaius laughed, a small, bad laugh. “Hit him. The general. With the standard. And kept running.”

“Who was it?”

“Nobody knows. Nobody’s going to say, are they.”

---

In the morning the standards were counted. Thirty-two had been lost. Laberius heard the figure from the tribune, standing in front of the cohort in the grey light with a face like old wax, and the cohort heard it in silence.

His legion’s eagle had come back. He saw it; it was in its place in the shrine by the general’s tent, on its pole. But the cohort’s standard had not. He went and looked at the place where it should have been, in the ground in front of the tribune’s tent, where the socket was. It was an iron socket, sunk in a block of wood, worn smooth on the inside from the pole going in and out every morning and every night for years. It was empty. There was nothing in it but a little water from the night, and a dead leaf.

He stood and looked at it until the tribune came out of the tent and looked at him, and then he went away.

---

The general came to the army in the afternoon. He had them paraded, all the legions that had been at the old camp, in the open ground behind the lines; and he came out and stood in front of them, bareheaded, as he always did, and Laberius waited for it.

He knew what was coming. They all did. He had seen it at Placentia, a year ago, the long ranks and the general’s voice and the helmet with the tokens in it; and he had thought then, at Placentia, that it had been about money, about men who had asked for money out loud, and that it was a thing that happened to other legions. And now his own legion had run, the whole of it, and lost its standards, and left the general on foot on the plain to be hit by his own standard-bearers, and he stood in the ranks with his shield on his arm and his helmet borrowed from a dead man and waited for the general to say *one in ten*.

The general did not say it.

He spoke to them for a while, and his voice was the same as ever, clear and level, and Laberius waited for the anger to come into it and it did not. He said that they should not be cast down. He said that they had won a great many fights in this war, and lost one, and that a single loss was a thing that could happen to any army, by chance, by a mistake, by the gods; that it had happened to them because of a wall, and a wrong turning, and Pompey’s horse coming over the hill at the wrong moment, and not because they were cowards. He said that they had been brave all through the spring. He said that the enemy had not known how to finish it, and that if the enemy had had a general who knew how to win, the war would have been over yesterday, and it was not.

Laberius stood in the ranks and did not understand. He kept waiting for the turn, the place where the voice would change. It did not come. The general was not angry. He was calm. He was calm the way he had been calm on the mole at Brundisium and on the tribunal at Corfinium and at Placentia before the lot, and Laberius thought, standing there, that it was the worst kind of anger, the kind that does not show, and that it would come out somewhere, later, on somebody.

Then the men began to shout.

It started in the front ranks, in the legion next to his, and it went along. They were shouting that they wanted to be punished. They were shouting for the general to decimate them, to take one in ten, to do it now; that they had run, that they had shamed him, that they would not be able to look at him unless he punished them. Men were weeping. Laberius heard a man in his own rear rank shouting it, a man who had been at Placentia and had seen the lot, shouting *one in ten, general, one in ten*, in a cracked voice, over and over.

Laberius did not shout. He stood with his mouth shut.

The general let them go on for a little. Then he held up his hand, and they stopped. And he said, quite quietly, that he would not punish them. That they would punish themselves enough, he thought, by the next fight. That he had a few standard-bearers he would speak to, and a few men he would reduce in rank, and that was all. And he turned and went back to his tent.

---

That night the army left the lines. All of it, in the dark, without trumpets, the baggage first and the legions after, marching south and then east, away from the sea, away from Pompey and Dyrrachium and four months of ditches. The men said they were going to Apollonia, and then over the mountains into Thessaly, where the corn was ripe.

Laberius walked at the head of his fifty-two. He had not told anybody. Gaius had not asked again. Vibius had not asked at all, and would not, he knew; Vibius had been behind the century with the staff and had seen exactly who ran first and who ran last, and had written fifty-two on the tablet and nothing else.

In the dark, on the road, Rufrius came up beside him, slowly, on his big feet.

“Cordus,” he said.

“What.”

“Afranius’s lot built camps like that one at Ilerda. Same shape, inner rampart and all. I know how they go.” Rufrius walked a while. “Nobody stood in that gate. Nobody. I looked.”

Laberius did not answer. They walked. After a while Rufrius dropped back to his own century again, and Laberius heard him in the dark behind, telling one of his men to pick his feet up.
