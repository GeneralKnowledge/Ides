# The Priesthood

POV: Brutus · Pompey’s camp on the hill above the plain of Pharsalus · 8 August 48

---

They were quarrelling about the chief priesthood again, and Brutus, who had come to the general’s tent to return a book, stood just inside the door with the book in his hands and could not find a moment to give it back.

It was the third evening of it. He had heard it on the first evening from his own tent, which was near enough, and had thought it was a joke; on the second he had been at the council and had heard it begin and had left. Now it had begun again, after dinner, in the big tent with the sides rolled up and the lamps lit and the wine going round, and there were perhaps twenty men in it, and Pompey was not one of them. Pompey had gone out to look at the enemy’s camp from the edge of the hill, as he did every evening, alone, with two slaves and a lantern. In his absence the tent had become what it was.

“It is not a question of seniority,” Domitius was saying. He was sitting up on a couch with his cup held out in front of him like a magistrate’s rod, a big fair heavy man with a red face. “If it were a question of seniority I should have yielded long ago. It is a question of the man. The office was held by Caesar. It must be held, after him, by a man who has suffered from Caesar. I was besieged by him. I was handed over to him by my own soldiers, like a runaway slave. I was made to stand in front of his tribunal in the snow and be forgiven.”

“You were given your money back,” said a voice from another couch.

“I was given my own money back. As if it were a favour.” Domitius’s face got redder. “There is no man in this camp who has been more insulted by Caesar than I have, and the chief priesthood is mine.”

“Lentulus says the same,” somebody said. “He was at Corfinium too.”

“Lentulus was in the same cart,” said Domitius. “Not the same insult.”

Lentulus Spinther, who was at the far end of the tent, said with great dignity that his insult had been at least as great, and that he had held a priesthood longer than Domitius had held anything; and Scipio, who was Pompey’s father-in-law and had come from Syria with two legions and an enormous baggage train and a manner to match, said that the question was academic, since the office would naturally go to the most senior consular present, which was himself. And then three men spoke at once.

Brutus stood by the door. Nobody had seen him yet, or if they had, they had not thought him worth stopping for. He held the book. It was Pompey’s own copy of a treatise on fortification, which Pompey had lent him a month ago with great courtesy, and which Brutus had read, out of courtesy, and found very dull, and had come to return with a sentence of thanks he had worked out on the way. He looked at the faces in the lamplight. He knew them all. He had known most of them all his life.

The chief priesthood. The office Caesar held, and would hold until he died, because it was for life. They were dividing it between them on the eve of the battle, as if Caesar were already dead. And not only the priesthood. That was only tonight’s. Last night it had been the consulships for the next three years, who would have them and in what order; and before that the houses on the Palatine and the Carinae of men who had stayed in Rome, which were to be confiscated; and before that, the night before, Domitius’s proposal that when they got back to Rome every senator who had stayed in Italy should be tried by the senators who had come to Greece, with three tablets for each juror to vote on: one for acquittal, one for death, one for a fine. That one had been serious. Domitius had had the tablets drawn up.

“And Cicero?” somebody said, out of nowhere. “What do we do with Cicero? He came, I’ll give him that. But he came late, and he’s done nothing but sneer since he got here.”

“Cicero’s at Dyrrachium,” said somebody else. “With Cato. Ill.”

“He’s always ill.”

Brutus felt his face grow warm. He thought of Cicero in his tent at Dyrrachium in the winter, sitting up in bed, grey, grateful for a jar of honey and a conversation about Polybius. He opened his mouth.

“Marcus Brutus.” It was Scipio who had seen him. Scipio raised his cup. “Our philosopher. Come in, come in. You’re a man of judgement. Tell Domitius he’s not fit to be chief priest.”

The tent turned and looked at him. He stood with Pompey’s book in his hands.

He could have said a great many things. He had them, in a row, the way he always did, the first words of each one ready: that the Republic was not a dinner to be divided before it was cooked; that the men they were proposing to try had stayed in Italy for the most part because they were old, or poor, or afraid, and that none of those was a crime; that Caesar was alive, on the plain below, with an army that had marched three hundred miles on roots and was still there; that Domitius had been forgiven by Caesar and was proposing to forgive nobody; that it was unfitting, all of it, and that he was ashamed to be in the tent.

“I have come to return a book,” he said. “To the general.”

There was a pause. Then Scipio laughed, quite kindly, and said that the general was out looking at Caesar, and that Brutus might leave it on the table, and turned back to Domitius. And the tent turned back with him, and went on.

Brutus put the book down on the table among the maps. He stood there a moment longer. Then he went out.

---

Outside it was very warm still, and quiet, and the sky over the plain was the deep clear blue of early summer just before it goes dark. The camp was on a hill, and the plain was below it, flat and wide and yellow with standing corn, nearly ripe; and on the far side of the plain, two or three miles off, low on its own rise, was Caesar’s camp, with its fires beginning to show.

Pompey was there, at the edge of the hill, as Brutus had been told: a big dark shape against the sky with a slave on either side and the lantern unlit at his feet. He was looking at Caesar’s fires. He did not turn round. Brutus did not go to him. He stood a little way off, in the shadow of a tent, and watched him look.

He had not said anything in the tent. He was glad of it. He had stood there, among them, while they did what they did, and he had not joined it and he had not opposed it, and that seemed to him, standing in the warm dark, the right thing: to keep himself apart from it, clean of it, as a man keeps his hands clean of a thing by not touching it. He did not belong to their quarrel. He was here for the laws. Whatever they did with the chief priesthood, and the houses, and the three tablets, it would not be his doing.

Somebody came up the path from the lower camp, two men, talking in low voices, and passed close to him without seeing him in the shadow. He knew one of the voices. It was Labienus, who had been Caesar’s right hand in Gaul, and who had come over to Pompey in the first week of the war, and who knew Caesar’s army as no man in this camp knew it.

“... not the army that beat the Gauls,” Labienus was saying. He had a hard flat voice, like a door. “Don’t believe it. The men who were at Alesia are dead, most of them, or sick, or left in Italy. What he has down there are the recruits from the Cisalpine and the levies he took from us. They’ve been eating roots for four months. We’ll swear an oath tomorrow, at the council. All of us. Not to come back from the field except as victors.” He laughed, shortly. “I’ll take it first, so the others have to.”

The two men went on up the path, towards the general’s tent, and the lamplight and the voices.

Brutus stayed where he was. He thought about the men who had been at Alesia, and about roots, and about a plain covered in corn. He thought about Pompey, alone at the edge of the hill, looking. After a while Pompey bent and picked up the lantern, and one of the slaves lit it from a torch, and the three of them came back along the edge of the hill towards the tents. Pompey passed quite close. His face in the lantern-light was heavy and calm and tired, the face of a man who has been asked to do a thing he has done many times and is no longer sure he wishes to. He saw Brutus, and nodded to him, as one nods to a guest one is fond of, and went on into the tent, where the noise rose to meet him and then, slowly, went down.

Brutus went to his own tent. His slave had lit the lamp. The Polybius was on the table where he had left it, with the wax tablets and the stylus, at the place he had got to that afternoon, which was the long passage in the sixth book on how the Roman state is made. He sat down and read it over. He read it three times. Then he wrote it out on the wax in four lines, plainly, without the arguments: the consuls, the Senate, the people; each holds the others back; no one part can rule alone; and so the state lasts. He looked at the four lines for a while. Then he smoothed them out with the flat of the stylus and wrote them again, more neatly, and went to bed.
