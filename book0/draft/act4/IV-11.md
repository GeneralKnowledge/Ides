# The Price

POV: Antony · Rome, the Carinae, Pompey’s house · October 47

---

Oppius came on the fourth day after Caesar got back, at the second hour, with a tablet, and was shown into the atrium under the beaks, and stood on the dolphins with his hands folded in front of him, and asked, in the gentlest voice in Rome, when Antony proposed to pay for the house.

Antony was eating. He had come out to the atrium with a piece of bread in his hand to see who it was, because nobody came at the second hour, and he stood there with the bread and looked at Oppius.

He knew Oppius well. Everybody did. Gaius Oppius had kept Caesar’s affairs in Rome for ten years, with Balbus, a small, round, soft-spoken knight with weak eyes and a beautiful hand, who wrote Caesar’s letters in cipher and paid Caesar’s bills and knew to the sesterce what everything in Rome had cost Caesar and what it would cost him next. He was never in a hurry. He was never rude. He had never once, in Antony’s hearing, raised his voice.

“Pay for it,” Antony said.

“The price bid at the auction. It’s owed to the treasury.” Oppius opened the tablet and looked at it, though he plainly did not need to. “Caesar has asked me to ask.”

“Caesar has.”

“Yes.”

Antony put the bread down on the edge of the pool. He looked round the atrium, at the light coming down the well, and the beaks on the wall, and the couches he had brought in from his old house, which looked like children’s furniture in this room. He had been living here for three weeks. He had had the household moved in on the day after the sale, his people and his things and his horses, and Cytheris’s people too, and he had had the first dinner the same night, forty people, on Pompey’s couches, under Pompey’s beaks, and it had been the best night of the year.

“I held Italy for him,” he said.

“I know.”

“For eighteen months. While he was in Egypt doing whatever he was doing in Egypt. Nobody else would have. I held it. I kept the Forum. I kept the legions. I kept the Senate in its seats.”

Oppius did not say anything about the legions. He did not say anything about the Forum, or about the morning Antony had brought the soldiers down from the Capitol, or about the men on the rock, or about the legions in Campania, which Antony had gone down to calm in the summer and had not calmed, and which had marched on Rome after he left and were sitting on the Field of Mars now, at this moment, a mile away, waiting for Caesar to come out and talk to them. Oppius did not mention any of it. He stood on the dolphins with his tablet and waited, and the not mentioning filled the atrium.

“I commanded his left at Pharsalus,” Antony said. “His left. I brought his army across the sea under Libo’s nose and Pompey’s fleet. I was at Ariminum in a slave’s tunic. Who else was at Ariminum in a slave’s tunic? And what have I had? I’ll tell you what I’ve had. Nothing. Not a sesterce. Not a house. Not a province. Every man who did a quarter of what I did has been paid for it twice over, and when I buy one house, one, at a public auction, where any man in Rome could have bid against me and nobody did, he sends you to ask me for the money.”

He heard his own voice. It was loud. It went up the well of the atrium and came back down off the walls. Two slaves who had been crossing at the far end stopped and then went on very quickly.

Oppius waited until it had stopped echoing.

“I’ll tell him that,” he said. “In those words, if you like. Or in others.”

“Tell him I’m owed.”

“I’ll tell him.” Oppius closed the tablet. He looked at Antony with his weak, kind eyes, and for a moment Antony thought he would say something else; something about Dolabella, perhaps. Dolabella, who had proposed to cancel every debt in Rome and made the Forum a battlefield and whom Antony had put down with soldiers on Caesar’s behalf; Dolabella, who had been to see Caesar the day after he got back, and been pardoned, and been made much of, and was going to Africa on Caesar’s staff. Everybody knew that already. It had been round the Forum in a morning. Oppius did not say it. He bowed, very slightly, and went out under the beaks.

---

Antony did the sum after he had gone. He could not help it.

He sat on the edge of the pool with the bread he had not finished and did the sum in his head, the way he had done it in the treasury vault with the gold. The price of the house, which he knew exactly; he had known it since the auctioneer’s hand came down. What he had. What he owed. What he was owed, by men who were not going to pay him. And what Caesar would give him if he went to Africa: nothing, probably, because he was not going to Africa; he knew that now, too, from Oppius’s face. Caesar was going to Africa with Dolabella on his staff and Lepidus to hold Rome, and Antony was going to stay here, in this house, being asked for the money.

The sum did not come out. It did not come out by a great deal. He did it twice, and it was the same both times, and he sat there on the edge of Pompey’s pool and laughed, because it was that or throw the bread.

Then he got up and called for his steward and said that there would be a dinner tonight. A large one. Everybody. Send to Cytheris, send to the mimes, send to the Greeks from the Palatine, send to everyone who had come to the last one and everyone who had not. Get the good wine up. Hire flute-girls. Light every lamp in the house.

The steward looked at him. “Tonight, master?”

“Tonight.”

“There are the legions on the Field of Mars, master. People are saying the city’s not safe after dark.”

“Then they’ll stay the night,” Antony said. “There’s room.”

---

The dinner was very loud. He made sure of that.

He lay on Pompey’s couch at the head of Pompey’s dining room, under a ceiling painted with ships, and drank, and made the room laugh, and laughed with it, louder than anyone. There were sixty people. There were mimes, and the flute-girls, and a man who ate fire. There were men who owed him money and men he owed money to and men who had come because everybody else had come, to see the inside of Pompey’s house and to see the Master of the Horse in it, the night after Caesar’s freedman had come to ask him for the price. They all knew. He could see them knowing. He drank to them. He made a speech, standing on the couch, about Pompey, who had been a great man and a great general and had had excellent taste in ceilings, and the room roared.

At the height of it, somewhere in the fourth hour of the night, he saw Fulvia.

She was standing in the doorway of the dining room. Not lying on a couch; she had not come to dine. She was in a dark cloak with the hood back, as if she had come from somewhere else and was going on to somewhere else, and she was looking at the room: at the flute-girls, and the fire-eater, and the men on the couches, and Cytheris, and him, standing on Pompey’s couch with a cup in his hand in the middle of a sentence. She looked at all of it without any expression at all. Then she looked at him, and lifted one eyebrow very slightly, and turned and went out.

He finished his sentence. He did not know afterwards what it had been. He got down off the couch and went out after her, through the room, with people calling to him, and found her in the atrium, by the pool, looking up at the beaks.

“You came,” he said.

“I came to see the house. Everybody’s talking about it.” She did not turn round. “It’s a very fine house.”

“It is.”

“Have you paid for it?”

He stood there. She turned round then and looked at him, in the light of the lamps round the pool, with her keys at her belt; she had a ring of them, as always, hanging loose, and they made a small sound when she moved.

“No,” he said.

“Are you going to?”

“No.”

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded, as if he had told her the price of something in the market.

“Then don’t,” she said. “But don’t throw a dinner about it either. Not tonight. Not with the legions a mile off and Caesar sitting in his house counting who came.” She glanced at the dining-room door, where the noise was. “Half of them will tell him by noon. The other half will tell him by the evening. You’ve just shown the whole of Rome you’re frightened.”

“I’m not frightened.”

“No. I know.” Something moved at the corner of her mouth. “That’s why I came.”

She drew her hood up. He did not want her to go. He found he did not want anything, in that moment, so much as for her not to go out of the door under Pompey’s beaks into the dark street with the legions a mile off; he wanted her to stay and tell him what to do, in that voice, the way she had told the Syrian at Curio’s gate to come back on the Ides. He did not know how to say it. He was not a man who knew how to say things like that. He knew how to say them to a room.

“Come tomorrow,” he said. “In daylight. When the wine’s unpacked.”

She looked at him, and he saw her remember it, the step at Curio’s house, two years ago, with the creditors’ boys on the kerb.

“I might,” she said, and went out.

He stood in the atrium by the pool for a while after she had gone. Behind him the dinner went on without him, loud, louder than before, as dinners do when the host has left the room. He did not go back in. He stood there and looked up at the beaks, green bronze, on the wall of a house he had not paid for, and thought about what she had said, *you’ve just shown the whole of Rome*; and found that he did not mind her being right nearly as much as he would have minded anybody else.
