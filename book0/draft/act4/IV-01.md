# The Back Room

POV: Cicero · Brundisium, a borrowed house by the harbour · October to December 48

---

The lictors were in the back room, behind the kitchen, with the door shut, and Cicero could hear them through it, playing at knucklebones.

He had put them there himself, on the first evening, when the ship came in. It had been a bad crossing from Patrae, slow and sick, and he had stood at the rail as the harbour of Brundisium opened up in front of him in the evening light, the two arms of it and the town on the hill and the whole of Caesar’s army, it seemed, camped round the water, and he had understood all at once what he was about to do. He was about to land in Italy, in a harbour held by Caesar, with six lictors carrying rods wreathed in laurel, as a general who had been hailed *imperator* by his troops and was awaiting a triumph. A triumph. From the Senate. For Cilicia. With Caesar’s legions on the quay.

He had had the laurels taken off before the ship touched. Then he had had the lictors put in a closed carriage, and brought up to this house, which belonged to a friend of Atticus’s and had been lent to him, and shown into the back room. They had been there six weeks. He could not dismiss them, because a man who dismissed his lictors laid down his command, and a man who laid down his command entered the city as a private citizen, and he could not enter the city, because nobody had told him he might; and he could not let them be seen, because they were absurd. So they stayed in the back room. They played knucklebones. They were very patient about it. He sent them wine.

---

He was waiting for a letter.

He did not put it that way to himself. He said, when he wrote to Atticus, which he did every day and sometimes twice, that he was waiting to see what was decided; that he had come to Brundisium because it was the nearest port to Greece and the furthest from Rome, which was what he needed, a place that was in Italy and not in Italy at once; that he had come back because the war was over and he had no wish to go on fighting for a cause that had lost both its general and its reason. That was all true. What it came to was that he was waiting for Caesar to write to him and say that he might come home.

Caesar had not written. Caesar was in Egypt, men said; he had gone after Pompey to Alexandria, and had found Pompey dead on the beach, and had stayed. Nobody knew why. Some people said he had been shut in by the Alexandrians and was fighting for his life in the palace. Some people said other things, about the king’s sister. Brundisium was full of people saying things. It was full of men like Cicero, Pompeians who had come back after Pharsalus and were waiting to be told what they were, and they met in the street and in the baths and at the harbour and asked each other for news and gave each other rumours, and none of them had had a letter either.

The commander at Brundisium was Vatinius.

That had been, for the first week, the worst of it. Publius Vatinius, whom Cicero had once attacked in the Senate in a speech so savage that people still quoted it, about his neck, which was swollen, and his eyes, and his ancestry, and his conduct as tribune; Vatinius, whom he had later been obliged, at Caesar’s request, to defend in court, and had defended, and had hated himself for a month afterwards. Vatinius was in command of the port for Caesar. Every ship that went out, and every man who landed, and every letter that came in, passed under Vatinius’s eye. And Cicero had walked off the ship on the first evening, with his lictors in a closed carriage behind him, and found Vatinius on the quay waiting for him, swollen neck and all, with his hand out.

He had been kindness itself. That was what Cicero could not get over. He had found him a house. He had sent his own physician when Cicero was ill in the second week. He had come himself, twice, with news, and sat by the bed, and talked about the old days in the courts with great good humour, as if nothing had ever been said about his neck by anyone; and once, leaving, he had said at the door, quite simply, *I owe you my acquittal, Marcus, I don’t forget it*. And Cicero had lain in bed afterwards with his face hot and been unable, for an hour, to make a single sentence about it, even in his head.

---

He walked on the harbour wall in the mornings. It was something to do.

He walked to the end and back, slowly, because he was still weak, and looked at the ships, and at the men on the quays, and at the people who watched the ships; and he saw, after the first week, that he was one of the people watching. There were a good many of them. They stood about on the wall in ones and twos, men in good cloaks, and watched every sail that came in from the east, and when a ship tied up they went down to the quay as if by accident and stood near the gangway and listened to what the sailors said. He knew most of them by sight. Some of them he had dined with in Pompey’s camp. They nodded to each other, on the wall, and did not stop to talk, as men who have been in the same disreputable house do not stop to talk in the street.

He watched them, and he watched himself watching, and he told himself that what he was doing was waiting, which was what a decent man did when he had made a mistake and was waiting to learn its price. It was not abject. It was not like begging. He had not written to Caesar. He had not sent his brother or his son to Caesar, though his brother had gone, by himself, in a way that Cicero preferred not to think about. He had simply come home, as far as he could come, and stopped, and was waiting at the edge of Italy with his lictors in the back room, to be told.

That was decent. He was almost sure it was decent.

He thought sometimes about Corcyra. He tried not to, and then thought about it anyway, at night, when the knucklebones had stopped. It had been after Pharsalus, when what was left of the fleet had come together at Corcyra, and Cato had called a council; and Cato, who had never liked him, had stood up and said that the command ought to go to Cicero, as the senior consular present. Cicero had refused. He had said that the war was lost and he would have no more of it. And Pompey’s elder son, Gnaeus, a big, hot young man, had drawn his sword and come at him across the room, shouting *traitor*, and Cato had got between them and held the boy off, with his bare hands, until somebody took the sword away. Cicero had not moved. He remembered that. He had stood quite still, with the point a foot from his chest, and looked at it, and had not moved, not out of courage, but because he could not think of anything to do that would not look worse.

He had left Corcyra the next day. Cato had arranged it. He had not thanked Cato. He thought now that he should have. He thought he might write. But Cato had gone to Africa, men said, with the rest of them, to go on with it; and a letter from Brundisium to Africa, from a man waiting for Caesar’s pardon to a man refusing to ask for it, was not a letter Cicero knew how to begin.

---

On the forty-first day a letter came, but it was not from Caesar. It was from Antony, in Rome, with a copy of an edict.

Vatinius brought it himself. He came into the room where Cicero was sitting by the window and put it on the table, and stood there with his swollen neck, looking embarrassed.

“I thought you’d rather have it from me,” he said.

Cicero read it. It was short. It said that, by Caesar’s instruction, no person who had served with Pompey was to enter Italy until Caesar had looked into his case in person; and that any such person now in Italy was to leave it; and that the following were excepted from this order by name. There were two names. One of them was Gaius Laelius, whom Cicero hardly knew. The other was his own.

He read it twice. He sat with it in his hands.

“It’s a mark of favour,” Vatinius said, carefully. “You see? You’re named. You’re allowed to stay. Antony’s written in his own hand to say so, at the bottom. It means you’re safe.”

“Yes,” said Cicero. “I see.”

He did see. He saw that he was safe. He saw also that his name was now on an edict, posted in the Forum of Rome and in every town in Italy, in a list of two; that every man who had come back from Greece after Pharsalus and was now being ordered out of Italy would read it, and see his name there, alone, excepted, favoured, by Antony; and that they would draw the conclusion that any man would draw. He saw that he would not be able to walk on the harbour wall tomorrow, or the next day, among the men in good cloaks watching the ships, because they would have read it too. He saw that he had been forgiven, in public, by a man he despised, before he had been forgiven by the man he was waiting for; and that it would be quoted.

“Thank you,” he said to Vatinius. “It was kind of you to bring it.”

Vatinius went. Cicero sat by the window. After a while he got up and went through the kitchen to the back room and opened the door. The lictors were sitting on the floor in a ring with the knucklebones between them, and they looked up at him, all six, and began to get to their feet.

“No,” he said. “Sit. Go on.”

They sat. He stood in the doorway and watched them throw for a while, and then he asked whether there was room for a seventh, and the senior lictor, after a moment’s surprise, moved over.
