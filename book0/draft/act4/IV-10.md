# Under the Spear

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Carinae, Pompey’s house · October 47

---

They had planted the spear in the street outside the door, as the law required, and Hordeonius stood in front of it for some time before he went in, because he had not seen a spear planted for a great man’s goods since he was a boy at another man’s board, and the boy had been frightened of it.

It was an old spear, a cavalry lance with a long ash shaft and a leaf-shaped head, and somebody had driven the butt into the gap between two paving stones and wedged it with a cobble, so that it stood up straight in the middle of the street in front of the house with its head above the height of the door. It meant that the goods in the house belonged to the state, taken in war, and were for sale. Everybody in Rome knew what it meant. The street knew. The street had come out to look at it, and stood about in the doorways of the other houses in the Carinae, servants and children and old men, at a careful distance, as people stand about an animal that has been killed in the road.

“Sulla’s men did it like this,” Hordeonius said. “I saw it once. In the Palatine. A great house. They sold the owner’s shoes.”

“Did you buy anything?” said Sosia.

“I was twelve. I held a man’s tablet.” He looked at the spear a moment longer. “He bought the shoes.”

They went in.

---

The house was very large. Hordeonius had known it was large; everybody knew Pompey’s house in the Carinae, at least from the outside, a long plain front in the old style with a big door and the beaks of ships fixed along the wall of the vestibule, bronze beaks, green with age, taken from the pirates twenty years before and nailed up there as other men nail up the antlers of stags. He had never been inside. He went in under the beaks now, behind a crowd of other men, and found himself in an atrium so tall that the light from the opening in the roof came down into it like water into a well, and fell on the floor a long way below, on a mosaic of a sea battle, with dolphins.

The atrium was full of things. They had been brought out from all over the house and set out on tables and on the floor in rows, with a number chalked on each, the way the auctioneers always did it: furniture, couches, tables of citron-wood and ivory, bronze lamps on stands, statues, chests, tapestries rolled up, silver plate in trays, glass, hangings, a whole row of bronze tripods from somewhere in the East, the household’s kitchen pots in a heap. There were Pompey’s clothes. There was a rack of togas, folded, and a chest of tunics, and in front of the chest, on the mosaic, in a row, his shoes. Somebody had cleaned them.

Hordeonius looked at the shoes and then looked away.

The auctioneer stood on a little platform by the pool in the middle of the atrium, with his clerk beside him at a desk with tablets, and a crier, and two public slaves to bring the things up. The bidders stood round. There were not many. That was the first thing Hordeonius saw, and the thing he went on seeing, standing at the back by a pillar with Sosia, all the time it lasted. There were a great many people in the house; but they were the people who came to auctions to look, freedmen and dealers and idlers and servants sent to see what went for what, and they stood about at the edges of the atrium with their hands behind their backs. Bidders, men who would put up a hand and say a figure and mean it, there were perhaps a dozen. Small men, mostly. Dealers, who would buy the pots and the hangings by the lot and sell them on. Hordeonius knew most of them. None of them would bid for the house.

Nobody would bid for the house. He had known that before he came. He had known it since the sale was posted. No man in Rome who had a name or wished to keep one would put up his hand in Pompey’s atrium, under Pompey’s beaks, in front of Pompey’s shoes, and offer the state money for Pompey’s house, while Pompey was not a year dead and had sons alive in Africa with an army. It would be like buying a man’s widow. Every man in the Forum knew it. Every man in the Forum had stayed away.

Except one.

---

Antony came at the third hour, with his lictors.

Hordeonius heard him before he saw him. He heard the lictors in the street outside, their rods knocking on the paving, and then the voice in the vestibule, loud and warm and laughing, saying something about the beaks; and then the crowd in the atrium parting, the way crowds parted for him, and the Master of the Horse coming through it in a purple-bordered toga worn carelessly, with a cup in his hand that somebody had given him on the way, and three or four friends behind him, laughing. He was a big man. He looked bigger in this room than in the Forum. He looked round the atrium, at the things on the tables and the light coming down the well and the dolphins on the floor, and he laughed again, out of pure pleasure, the way a child laughs in a new house, and Hordeonius saw the dealers at the edges of the crowd look at each other.

The auctioneer stood up very straight on his platform.

They sold the small things first, as they always did, to warm the room. The pots went, and the hangings, and the lamps, in lots, to the dealers, for very little. Antony did not bid. He walked about among the tables with his friends, looking at things, picking them up; he picked up a little bronze statue of a boy with a goose and admired it and put it down; he tried a couch, lying down on it with his boots on while his friends laughed; he went and looked at the shoes, and said something to one of his friends that made the friend laugh so much he had to hold on to a pillar. Hordeonius did not hear what. He did not want to.

Then the auctioneer called the house.

He called it in the old form, the house and its land and its fixtures, the beaks on the wall and the dolphins on the floor, and named the figure at which the state would begin. It was a large figure. It was, Hordeonius judged, about half what the house had been worth before the war, at Caesar’s arbitrators’ own prices; about half what any arbitrator in Rome would have written on a tablet for it, if he had been asked to value it in settlement of a debt. It was the figure of a sale nobody expected to be made.

The crier repeated it. The atrium was very quiet.

Antony put up his hand.

He did not say anything. He put up his hand, the one with the cup in it, quite casually, the way a man at dinner puts up his hand for more wine; and the crier looked at the auctioneer, and the auctioneer looked at the room, and the room looked at the floor. The crier said the figure again, louder, and asked if there was any man who would say more. There was not. He said it a third time. The auctioneer brought his hand down flat on the desk.

That was all. The house was sold.

Antony drank what was in the cup and gave it to one of his friends and turned round and looked up at the light coming down the well, with his hands on his hips, like a man looking at the sky in a field he has just bought.

---

The auctioneer’s clerk came to Hordeonius’s pillar afterwards, when the room was emptying, with his tablet under his arm and a face like a man who has swallowed a stone.

He was a freedman of the auctioneer’s, and he owed Hordeonius six thousand sesterces, borrowed at the New Year to pay for his daughter’s wedding; and he had come, Hordeonius knew before he opened his mouth, to say that the interest would be late.

“It’s the commission,” the clerk said, low. “I’m paid on what’s paid in. On the day the buyer pays. Not on the bid. On the money.” He looked across the atrium to where Antony was standing in the vestibule under the beaks with his friends, laughing, about to go. “If he pays this month, I’ll pay you on the Kalends. If he doesn’t...”

“When will he pay?”

The clerk looked at him. He did not say anything. He did not need to.

Hordeonius considered it. He thought about the figure on the auctioneer’s tablet, half the house’s worth, for which no other man in Rome would bid; and about the man who had bid it with a cup in his hand; and about Bucco’s stool across the Janus arch, with the red seal of Hercules on its strongbox, lending to soldiers at three times his rate. He did the sum, slowly, as he had done the sum for Opimius’s house in the Velabrum, and for Caelius, and for Dolabella. And for the first time in a long while he arrived at an answer that pleased him.

Antony was Caesar’s man. Antony was Master of the Horse, Caesar’s deputy, with Caesar’s seal and Caesar’s lictors and Caesar’s Italy in his hands. A debt owed to the state by Caesar’s Master of the Horse was a debt owed to Caesar by Caesar’s own; it was, in the end, Caesar’s credit standing behind it, and Caesar had never in his life failed to pay what he owed, though he had not always paid it on the day. So Antony would pay. Late, perhaps. In instalments, perhaps. But it would be paid, because Caesar would see that it was paid, because Caesar could not allow his own deputy to sit in Pompey’s house without paying for it. It would look too much like what it was.

“He’ll pay,” Hordeonius said.

The clerk looked at him as if he were mad.

“Not this month. Not the next. But when Caesar comes home.” Hordeonius took the clerk’s arm, kindly. “Caesar will make him. It is Caesar’s house now, in a manner of speaking, and Caesar pays. Your interest can wait for Caesar. I will add nothing for the delay. You may tell your daughter’s husband so.”

The clerk thanked him. He did not look reassured. He went away across the dolphins with his tablet, and Hordeonius stood by the pillar and watched the public slaves begin to carry Pompey’s shoes out to a cart, in a basket, for the dealer who had bought them in a lot with the kitchen pots.

“You believe that?” said Sosia, very quietly. “That he’ll pay?”

“I believe Caesar will make him.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No,” Hordeonius said, after a moment. “But it is what I am going to write.”
