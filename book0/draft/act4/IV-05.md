# The Third Instalment

POV: Cicero · Brundisium · June 47

---

Tullia came on the twelfth of June, in the heat, in a hired carriage, with two maids and one box, and Cicero saw from the window as she got down that she had grown thin, and went down the stairs faster than he had gone down any stairs in a year.

She laughed at him for it, in the courtyard. She held him at arm’s length and said that he looked like a man who had been told the house was on fire, and that the house was not on fire, she had only come to see him; and he held her and felt how thin she was under the travelling cloak, thinner than at Caieta, and said that he had not been told she was coming. She said that was why she had come. If she had written he would have written back telling her not to, and she would have had to come anyway, and it would have been a waste of two letters.

He had not seen her for two years.

---

They sat in the garden in the evenings, because it was too hot to sit anywhere else. The house was small and the garden smaller, a yard with a fig tree and a well and a bench under the tree, and they sat on the bench with the light going and talked.

He had meant to talk about the right things. He had made a list in his head, before she came, of what could be talked about and what could not: Terentia, but not Terentia’s freedman and the money; Marcus, who was well, and Quintus, who was not to be mentioned, because Quintus had written to Caesar from Asia blaming his brother for everything and the letters had been read out at dinners in Alexandria, Cicero had heard, to great amusement; the weather; books. He had meant to keep to the list. And on the first evening she sat on the bench beside him with her feet drawn up under her like a girl and said, looking at the well:

“He’s going to be ruined, Father. I want you to know before you hear it from someone else.”

“Who is?”

“My husband.”

He did not answer at once. He had heard things, of course. Brundisium heard everything, a little late. He had heard about the law: that Dolabella, as tribune, had proposed to cancel every debt in Rome and every rent for a year, and that Trebellius had vetoed it, and that there had been fighting in the Forum over it, and that Antony, who held the city, had first been for it and then against it and then nobody knew. He had heard that Dolabella’s own debts were enormous. He had heard other things, about Dolabella and the houses he visited in the afternoons, which he had decided not to have heard.

“The law,” he said.

“The law. And everything.” She laughed. It was not a happy laugh but it was a real one; she had always laughed at things when they were at their worst, even as a little girl, and he had always loved her for it and been frightened by it. “He owes everybody, Father. He owes more than Curio did, I think, and nobody’s going to pay it for him. He thinks if he can get the law through, he’ll be clear. He thinks if he can’t, Caesar will come back and sort it out, because Caesar likes him. He doesn’t know which. He changes his mind every day. He talks about it at dinner as if it were a play he’d seen.” She stopped laughing. “He doesn’t talk to me about it. He talks about it in front of me. That’s different.”

Cicero was silent.

“And there’s the dowry,” she said.

“The dowry is my concern.”

“The third instalment’s due next month. On the Kalends of July. You owe it to him, Father. To him. While he’s standing up on the Rostra saying debts ought to be cancelled.” The laugh came back, short. “I asked him if he’d cancel yours. He didn’t think it was funny.”

“It’s quite funny,” Cicero said.

“I know. That’s why he didn’t like it.”

He looked at her. She was looking at the well, with her chin on her knees. Her face in the last of the light was very like her mother’s when Terentia had been young, and very like his own, and he could not tell which he was seeing. He thought that she had come all the way from Rome in the heat to tell him this, that her marriage was going on the rocks and taking her dowry with it, and that she had chosen to tell it as a joke so that he would not have to know what to say. And he did not know what to say. He sat there with every sentence he had ever made available to him, and not one of them was any use.

“I’ll find it,” he said at last. “The instalment. Somehow.”

“I know you will. That’s not why I came.” She put her head against his shoulder, the way she had at Caieta. “I came because I wanted to see you. And because I thought you’d be waiting for a letter, and you’d be better company if you had someone to wait with.”

---

He had been waiting for a letter for eight months.

It was the truth. She had seen it at once, in the first hour, the way she always saw things. He had told her nothing about it, and she had looked round his small borrowed house, and at the table by the window where he wrote, and at the way he looked up every time there was a knock at the street door, and she had known. He was waiting for Caesar. Caesar had been in Alexandria all winter, fighting in the streets of a city for reasons nobody in Italy understood, and then he had won, and then he had stayed on, for some reason everybody in Italy understood very well; and then in the spring he had gone at last, not to Italy, but to Syria, and on into Asia, because some king in Pontus had risen and beaten a Roman army. And still there was no letter. And Cicero sat in Brundisium in a house that was not his, with his lictors in the back room, which they had now been in for eight months, and waited for the knock.

He did not tell her. He did not tell her that every morning he went down to the harbour wall and watched the ships come in from the east; or that he had written to Caesar, in the end, in the winter, a letter he had rewritten eleven times and still could not think of without his face going hot; or that he was not waiting for Caesar only, but for Caesar’s people, and Caesar’s people included her husband, who was one of Caesar’s own, and who could, if he chose, say a word for his father-in-law that would be heard in Asia. He did not tell her that he had thought about asking her to ask Dolabella to say that word. He had thought about it on the stairs, coming down to meet her. He had decided not to, at the bottom of the stairs, with her in his arms. He did not tell her any of that.

“I’m not waiting for anything,” he said. “I’m reading. I’m writing a little. Brundisium is very restful.”

“Is it?”

“Extraordinarily.”

She did not lift her head from his shoulder. “Liar,” she said, quite gently.

---

She stayed only a few days; he could not keep her in that heat, and Terentia wrote to say so. In the mornings he wrote, or tried to, and she sat in the garden with a book she did not read; in the evenings they sat on the bench under the fig and talked about anything except the things on his list. On the fourth evening a letter came, at last, by a courier from the east. Cicero heard the knock and was on his feet before the slave had reached the door, and Tullia watched him go, and said nothing.

It was from Atticus. It was full of news and kindness and the price of things in Rome, and it said, near the end, that Caesar was said to be in Asia still and in no hurry, and that Atticus had heard, from a friend of Balbus’s, that a letter from Caesar to Cicero had been written, or was being written, or would be.

Cicero read it twice, by the lamp. Then he gave it to her, which he had never done with any letter of Atticus’s, and she read it, and gave it back.

“Would be,” she said.

“Would be.”

She laughed, and he found that he had laughed too, at the same moment, at the same thing, the two of them on the bench in the dark under the fig tree laughing at *would be*; and it was the best moment of the eight months, and he knew it while it was happening, which was rare.

She went back to Rome two days later. He went with her as far as the gate. He paid the carriage himself, in advance, and over the odds, and did not let her see the figure; and then he went home and wrote to Atticus to ask how much it would cost to borrow the third instalment of his daughter’s dowry, and from whom, at what rate, before the Kalends.
