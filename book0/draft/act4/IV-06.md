# Nicaea

POV: Brutus · Nicaea, in Bithynia, the governor’s hall · August 47

---

The king was very old, and he had dressed for the hearing in the clothes of a suppliant, and Brutus, waiting to speak for him, could not stop looking at his feet.

They were bare. Deiotarus had come into the hall at Nicaea barefoot, in a plain dark robe without ornament, with his white hair uncombed and no diadem, as a man comes who has been condemned and wishes to be seen to know it; and he had walked the length of the hall on the cold marble to the foot of the dais where Caesar sat, slowly, with a stick, and an attendant on either side of him not quite holding his arms. And his feet were an old man’s feet, yellow and thin and knotted, with the nails grown thick, and they were very dirty, because he had walked from his lodging through the streets. He stood on the marble in front of Caesar on those feet, leaning on the stick, with his head bowed. He had been a king for forty years. He had fought for Rome against Mithridates when Caesar was a boy. He had backed Pompey at Pharsalus with six hundred horse.

Brutus had not expected to mind the feet. He found that he minded them more than anything he had seen since Larissa.

---

He had come to Nicaea with Caesar’s staff. He did not quite know how that had happened, either. After Larissa there had been dinner, and then the next day there had been dinner again, and Brutus had been, for those days, a friend, the son of Servilia, a man Caesar was pleased to have at his table; and when Caesar went on after Pompey to Egypt Brutus had stayed in Asia, and when Caesar came out of Egypt in the spring and came north through Syria and into Pontus, and beat the king of Pontus at Zela in four hours, Brutus had been waiting for him in Asia, and had joined him again on the road. Nobody had asked him to. Nobody had asked him not to. He had simply been there, as he had been at Larissa, and Caesar had seemed glad.

And now Caesar was holding his court at Nicaea, on the way west, settling the East: who would be king where, and who would pay what, and who had backed Pompey and would be forgiven and how much it would cost them. And Deiotarus had backed Pompey, and had come to be judged, barefoot, and had asked, through his people, whether Marcus Brutus would speak for him.

Brutus had said yes at once. He had known Deiotarus for years, through his father-in-law, who had governed Cilicia; he had dined with him; the old man had lent him books. And he had been glad to be asked. He had been glad, he admitted to himself in the night, walking in the courtyard of the house where he was lodged, because it was the first time since Larissa that anybody had asked him for anything except his company. He had a thing to do. He had a speech to make, in a hall, before a judge, for a client. He had done it a hundred times in Rome. He knew how.

He had worked on the speech for three nights. He had it by heart.

---

When the time came he stood up and made it.

He spoke, he thought afterwards, as well as he had ever spoken. Not as Cicero spoke; he had never tried to speak like Cicero, and despised a little the men who did. He spoke plainly, as he wrote, in short clear sentences, without ornament and without appeal, the way the Attic orators spoke; and he said that Deiotarus had been Rome’s friend for forty years, and had been the friend of every Roman commander who had come into the East, Sulla and Lucullus and Pompey; and that when Pompey, the commander of the Roman people, had called on him to come to Greece with his horse, he had come, because that was what Rome’s friends did, and he had not asked which Rome. And Brutus said that he did not believe Caesar would punish a man for loyalty, whatever the object of it. And he said, at the end, looking straight at Caesar, without lowering his voice, that if Caesar did punish him, it would be a thing that all the kings of the East would remember, and that they would draw the lesson from it that it was safer to be nobody’s friend.

He had not planned to say the last part. It had come out of him at the end, standing there on the marble, with the old man’s dirty feet in the corner of his eye. He heard it go out into the hall. He heard the hall go quiet.

He sat down.

Caesar had listened. That was what Brutus would remember. Caesar had sat on the dais with his chin on his hand and listened to every word, with that whole attention that Brutus had felt at Larissa, as if there were nobody else in the hall; and he had not interrupted, and he had not smiled, and when Brutus said the last part, about the kings of the East, Caesar’s eyes had not moved from his face, and something in them had changed, very slightly, the way a lamp changes when somebody opens a door in another room.

Then Caesar spoke to Deiotarus.

He was not gentle with him. He said that the king had fought against him at Pharsalus, and that he knew it, and that the king knew he knew it. He said that the king’s friendship with Rome had been a friendship with whichever Roman was nearest, and that this was a thing kings did and a thing Rome did not forget. He said that he would not take the king’s title, because of what had been said for him, and because he was old; but that he would take a part of his lands, and give them to men who had been on the right side, and that the king would pay for the war that had been fought in his neighbourhood, as the other kings were paying. And then he said that the king might go.

Deiotarus bowed. It took a long time; he was stiff. Then he turned and went back down the length of the hall on his bare feet, with the stick, slowly, with his attendants on either side of him not quite holding his arms.

Brutus watched him go.

He had won. That was what he told himself, sitting there. The king was still a king; that was what he had been asked to save, and he had saved it. Caesar had heard him. Caesar had listened to every word, and had answered him, in front of the whole hall, and had changed his judgement because of what Brutus had said; he had said so. *Because of what had been said for him.* Brutus had stood in a hall before the master of the world and spoken to him plainly, as one Roman to another, without flattery, and been heard. As an equal.

He sat with that for a while and found that it was warm, and that he did not like how warm it was, and that he could not make it cooler.

---

The court rose. People stood. Brutus stood with them and went towards the door, among the officers and the provincials and the clerks, and he passed close to the dais on his way.

Caesar had stood up too. He was talking to one of his friends, a man Brutus knew a little, one of the Romans who were always about him, leaning down from the dais to say something with his hand on the man’s shoulder. He was smiling. Brutus heard his own name. He heard it quite clearly, *Brutus*, in Caesar’s light quick voice, and then something after it that he did not catch, because a clerk dropped a box of tablets on the marble just then, and the noise went up into the roof.

He did not turn round. It would not have been fitting. He went on to the door with the others and out into the courtyard, into the white heat of the afternoon, where the king’s litter was waiting by the gate and the king was being lifted into it, very carefully, by four slaves, with his bare feet drawn up under his robe.

Brutus stood and watched until the curtains were closed. Then he went back to his lodging and sat down at the table and wrote to his mother. He told her about the king, and the hall, and the speech, and what Caesar had said in judgement. He told her that he thought he had spoken well. He did not tell her that Caesar had said his name afterwards, to somebody else, with his hand on the man’s shoulder, smiling; because he did not know what had followed it, and he found that he wanted to know so badly that he could not bear to write it down.
