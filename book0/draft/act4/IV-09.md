# Good Ground

POV: Laberius · the camps in Campania, and the road to Rome · September 47

---

The plain by the Volturnus was the best ground Laberius had ever seen, and he saw it every morning for a month from the camp rampart while the legions waited to be told to go to Africa, and would not go.

It went away flat to the south and the east from the river, as far as he could see, to the hills; flat and dark and soft, the colour of a good loaf, and everything on it grew. He had never seen so much growing. There were vines, trained up elms in long rows, so that the trees stood in the fields like men holding the vines up for you to look at; and under the vines there was corn, or beans, or something green he did not know; and between the rows there were olives, old ones, grey. There were farms every half-mile, with white walls and red roofs and a well, and a path between them, and a man at the end of every path with a mule. In the mornings there was mist on the plain, low, so that the elms stood up out of it to the waist, and then the sun came and the mist went and there it was, all of it, flat and dark and full.

He looked at it every morning. He did not say anything about it. He went down off the rampart afterwards and did the morning count.

---

They had been in Campania since the summer. All of them, the old legions: the ones that had been in Gaul and Spain and Greece, that had dug at Dyrrachium and stood on the plain at Pharsalus. They had been brought back across the sea after Pharsalus and put into camps along the Volturnus, between Capua and the sea, to rest; and they had rested. And then, in the summer, word had come that the general was coming home from the East and was going to Africa, where Cato and Scipio and Labienus and the two Pompey boys had got together everything that was left after Pharsalus, and the king, Juba, with his elephants; and the legions in Campania were to be ready to sail.

And the legions in Campania had said no.

Not all at once. It had come up slowly, over the summer, the way the Ninth’s trouble had come up at Placentia, in the evenings first, round the fires, and then in the daytime. Laberius had heard it come. He had sat at the fires and heard what was said, and some of it he had said himself. They had been promised. At Brundisium, the five hundred, which had been paid in the end, most of it, late. And after Pharsalus, more: land, discharge, the rewards for a finished war. And the war was finished. Pompey was dead in Egypt. They had won it. They had been fighting for ten years, some of them, and for fifteen, and they wanted their discharge and their money and their land, and they wanted it before they went anywhere else, because a man who went to Africa might not come back from Africa, and then what was the promise worth.

“It’s not wrong,” Gaius said, at the fire. “Is it.”

“No.”

“Then why are you looking like that?”

“I’m not looking like anything.”

“You’re looking like Vibius at Placentia.”

Vibius, across the fire, did not look up. He had his tablet on his knee, the one with the century’s names on it, and where each man had been on each day; he had kept it since Placentia, every day, without being told. He made a mark on it.

---

The general sent Sallust.

He came to the camps in the second week of the month, a praetor-elect, a short, quick, clever man with a sharp face and an expensive cloak, and he stood on a platform in front of the legions and told them that the general would give every man another thousand, on top of what had been promised, when they got to Africa. A thousand more. He said it as if it were a great deal. It was a great deal.

The legions did not cheer. There was a silence, and then somebody at the back shouted *Now!*, and somebody else shouted *Promises!*, and then everybody was shouting, the whole field, and a stone went past Sallust’s head and hit the post behind him, and another, and then the front ranks were going forward over the ground towards the platform, and Sallust’s people had him off the back of it and onto a horse, and he was gone down the road towards Capua at a gallop, with his cloak flying and men running after him throwing things.

Laberius stood in his place in the line. He did not go forward. He did not throw anything. He stood there and watched the men of the other legions go forward, and his own men round him shouting, and he did not stop them shouting. He saw Gaius shouting. He did not stop Gaius either.

Two senators were not so lucky as Sallust. They had come with him, and they had been slower getting to their horses, and the men caught them by the river. Laberius heard about it that evening. He did not see it. He was glad, again, that he did not see it.

---

Then the legions marched on Rome.

Nobody gave the order. There was nobody to give it. The tribunes had gone; some had been chased out of the camps and some had left before they were chased. The centurions stayed, most of them. Laberius stayed. And one morning the legions were drawn up on the road north out of the camps, by cohort and by century, in full kit, with their standards, as if they were going to a war; and they started walking. To Rome. To find the general when he got there and ask him to his face, all of them together, for what they had been promised.

Laberius walked at the head of his century. It was what he did. He did not lead the march. Nobody led it; it led itself, the way a river does. But a century on the road had to have somebody at the front of it, and that was him, and so he walked there, with Vibius behind and Gaius on his left, along the Appian Way, north.

He was not sure what he was doing. He knew what the others thought they were doing. They thought they were going to be paid. They thought that if they all went to Rome and stood in front of the general, all of them, the old legions, the men who had won him his war, he could not say no. And they might be right. Laberius did not know. He thought about the Ninth, at Placentia, and the helmet with the tokens, and the centurion who had taken the absent man’s place. He thought that the general had been one man then, alone in front of a legion, with eleven other legions round it that had not mutinied. Now it was all of them. There was nobody round them.

He walked. The plain went by on the right, flat and dark, with the elms standing in it holding up their vines.

---

At Capua, where the road went past the walls, they stopped for a night, and Laberius went into the town.

He had not meant to. But the century was settled, and Vibius had the count, and he found himself walking in through the gate with the cup in his pack, wrapped in its rag, under his spare tunic, where it had been since the slope above the stream on the morning they found Domitius. He had never taken it out except to look at it. He had carried it from Thessaly to Brundisium and across the sea and into Campania and it had never once been out of the pack in daylight. He walked down the main street of Capua, which was full of soldiers from the camps doing the same thing he was doing, which was looking, and he found the street of the silversmiths, and he went into the first shop.

The silversmith was an old man with a magnifying stone on a cord round his neck. He looked at Laberius, and at his belt, and at his boots, and then he looked at the cup when Laberius took it out of the rag and put it on the counter, and his face did not change at all, which Laberius thought was probably a skill.

He picked it up. He turned it. He looked at the men and the horses going round the side, through the stone, for a long time. He weighed it on a small bronze balance, with weights, carefully. Then he put it down.

“Where’s it from?”

“Greece.”

“Where in Greece?”

Laberius did not answer. The old man looked at him, and then nodded, slowly, as if he had been told.

“Pharsalus,” he said. “We’ve had a lot of it in, this summer. Your lot. Cups, mostly. Some plate.” He touched the rim of the cup with one finger. “It’s good. Old work. Athenian, I think, or made by somebody who’d seen Athenian. Somebody’s grandfather’s.”

“What’s it worth?”

The old man told him.

Laberius stood at the counter and heard the figure, and did a thing he had not done before, which was to turn it into land. He did not know he was going to do it until he had done it. He thought: that’s a field. That’s a field of that ground, out there, the dark ground by the river, with the elms. Not a big one. Four iugera, perhaps, the way prices were. A field with an elm in it and a vine up the elm. He stood there with his hands on the counter and saw it, quite clearly, the elm and the vine and the ground, flat and dark, and a path to it, and a wall.

“Well?” said the old man.

Laberius picked up the cup and wrapped it in the rag again.

“Not today,” he said.

The old man shrugged. “It’ll be less tomorrow,” he said. “There’s a lot of it coming in.”

---

He walked back to the camp outside the walls in the dusk. The plain was going blue. He did not look at it. He looked at the road, and at his feet, which were all right, and at the men round the fires as he came in, who were talking about Rome, and what the general would say, and what they would say back.

“Where’ve you been?” said Gaius.

“Capua.”

“What for?”

“To see it.”

Gaius looked at him, at his face, and then at the pack on his back, and did not ask anything else. Laberius sat down by the fire and took the pack off and put it behind him, against his back, where he could feel it.
