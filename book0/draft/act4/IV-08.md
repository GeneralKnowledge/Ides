# The Road from Tarentum

POV: Cicero · the road between Tarentum and Brundisium · September 47

---

The dust on the road was white, and fine as flour, and it had got into his shoes by the second milestone, and Cicero walked on in it with his head up because the alternative was to ride, and he would not meet Caesar on a mule.

There were a great many of them on the road. That was the worst of it. Word had come into Brundisium two days before that Caesar had landed at Tarentum from Greece and would come round by road, and every man in the town who had been waiting on Caesar’s pardon, which was most of the men in good cloaks on the harbour wall, had decided in the night, separately, without consulting each other, to go out along the Tarentum road to meet him. So they had all come. They had come in litters and on horses and on foot, in their best togas, with their slaves and their freedmen and their sons; and they were strung out along the road for a mile in the white dust in the morning heat, in little groups, not talking to each other, each group a careful distance from the next, like mourners at a funeral where nobody is sure who is chief mourner.

Cicero walked. He had his son beside him, and Tiro behind, who had come back from Patrae in the spring at last, thin and well, and had taken charge of everything within a day, as if he had never been away. The lictors he had left at the house. He had thought about it for most of the night. A man could not go out to meet the dictator with six lictors carrying laurelled rods, as if he were another general coming to treat; and he could not dismiss them, because nobody had told him he might; so he had left them in the back room, where they had now been for eleven months, with a jar of wine and the knucklebones, and had told them that if he did not come back they were to consider themselves discharged.

He had said it as a joke. The senior lictor had not laughed.

---

He was ashamed. He knew it, walking. He had known it since he woke. Not of the waiting, or of the war, or of Pompey’s camp; he had made his peace with all of that, in the eleven months, in a thousand letters to Atticus. He was ashamed of this: of walking along a road in the dust in his best toga, in a crowd of men he despised doing the same thing for the same reason, to stand at the side of the road when Caesar came by and be looked at, and be judged, in front of all of them. He would be judged in public. Caesar would see him, or not see him; would stop, or not stop; would speak, or ride by with a nod; and every man on this road would see which, and carry it home, and write it to Rome. He was going to be tested in front of a crowd of hostile witnesses, and he could not prepare for it, because he did not know what the question would be.

He had had a letter. That was the strange thing. A letter from Caesar had come at last, in the summer, from Asia, and it had been generous. More than generous. It had said that Cicero might keep his lictors and his title and his hopes, and come and go as he chose, and that Caesar looked forward to seeing him. Cicero had read it fifty times. He had it in the fold of his toga now. And still he was ashamed, walking, because a letter was a letter, written in a tent at the end of a day to a man a thousand miles off; and this was a road, with witnesses.

“Father,” said Marcus. “Horses.”

---

They came round the shoulder of the hill ahead, at a walk, in a cloud of their own dust: a troop of cavalry first, Germans, very big, very fair, on horses that looked too small for them; and then a group of officers; and then a carriage, and then more horses; and then nothing for a while, and then the baggage, a long way back.

The men on the road began to move. Cicero saw it happen all along the line, the way a field of barley moves when the wind comes: the little groups stiffening, the slaves stepping back, the togas being twitched straight, the faces turning. Nobody went forward. Nobody wanted to be the first. They stood at the side of the road in their groups and waited, and the horses came on.

Cicero was ahead of most of them. He had walked faster than he meant to, because he had not wanted to be walking among them, and so he was nearly at the front, with only a few groups between him and the bend. He stopped. He stood at the side of the road with his son on one side and Tiro behind him, and the dust settling on his toga, and watched the Germans go by, and then the officers.

Caesar was among the officers. He was on a horse, a bay, in a plain travelling cloak, bareheaded, as always, and he was thinner than at Formiae, and browner, and his hair was greyer, and he was talking to the man beside him and laughing at something. He looked along the road at the men waiting, quickly, the way he looked at everything, and Cicero saw him see him.

Caesar reined in.

He reined in, and said something to the officers, and swung his leg over and got down off the horse, in the road, in the dust, and gave the reins to somebody, and came walking along the road towards Cicero on foot. Past the first group, who had begun to bow. Past the second. Not looking at them. Looking at Cicero, with that whole attention, the way he had looked at him at the foot of the steps at Formiae; and his face was open and pleased and tired, and he had his arms out.

Then he was there, and he had Cicero in his arms.

Cicero smelled horse and sweat and dust and something under it, oil, a man’s body after a long journey in the heat. He felt the thin hard arms. He stood in the road with his own arms hanging, for a moment, because he had prepared for everything except this; and then he put them round Caesar, as one does, and felt under his hands the shoulder-blades through the cloak, and held on.

“Marcus,” Caesar said, letting go of him, holding him by the shoulders, looking at him. “You’ve been ill again. You’re thin.”

“So are you.”

“I’ve been in Egypt. Everyone’s thin in Egypt, except the king, and he’s dead.” He laughed, the short quick laugh. “Walk with me. Will you? I’ve been on that horse since dawn and I’d like to use my legs. Send your people on.” He turned to the officers. “Go on ahead. I’ll walk a little with Cicero.”

---

They walked a long way.

Cicero did not afterwards know how far. Miles, perhaps. Caesar’s horse was led behind them, and behind the horse came the carriage, and behind the carriage the officers, at a distance, and behind the officers, at a greater distance, the whole straggling line of men in good cloaks from the side of the road, who had fallen in at the rear because there was nothing else to do, and who walked along behind in the white dust and watched the two figures in front walking together, and could not hear a word.

Cicero was aware of them the whole time. He could not help it. He felt them on the back of his neck like the sun.

They talked about everything. Or Caesar talked, and Cicero answered, and then Cicero talked, and Caesar listened; and it was easy. That was what he could not get over, then or afterwards. It was so easy. Caesar asked about Tullia, and about Terentia’s health, and about Marcus, who was walking somewhere behind with Tiro; and he talked about Alexandria, a little, in a way that told Cicero nothing at all, and about the king of Pontus, whom he had beaten in four hours and was rather ashamed of having had to beat at all; and he asked what Cicero was writing, and said that he had reread the old dialogue on the orator, the one in three books, in the winter, in the palace, during a siege, and had enjoyed it more than anything in years, and named a passage. He named it correctly. Cicero felt his face grow hot with pleasure and could do nothing about it.

They did not talk about Pharsalus. They did not talk about the war, or Pompey, or the camp at Dyrrachium, or the edict, or the eleven months in Brundisium with the lictors in the back room. Caesar did not mention any of it. Nor did he say, at any point, *I forgive you*. He did not need to. He was walking with Cicero alone along the Tarentum road in front of every Pompeian in Brundisium, talking about prose style. That was the sentence. Every man behind them in the dust could read it.

Cicero walked beside him and felt something he had not felt in two years, a kind of lightness in the chest, as if a strap had been undone; and he did not let himself name it, because if he named it, it would be relief, and he did not wish to be a man who was relieved.

Instead he told himself that it was friendship. That Caesar had always been his friend, in his way, since they were young men in the courts; that Caesar admired his work and valued his company and had come along this road looking for him because he wished to talk to the one man in Italy who could talk back. That was true. He was nearly sure that was true. It was a very good feeling, to be sure of it.

---

At the milestone where the Brundisium road came in, Caesar stopped and called for his horse.

“I’ll go on,” he said. “I’ve a great many people to see in Brundisium and they’ll all want to be seen first.” He took Cicero’s hand. His grip was quick and dry. “Come to Rome when you like. Come as you like. Bring your lictors, if you want them. I’d rather you didn’t. They make people nervous, and you don’t need them.” He smiled. “You never did.”

He got up on the horse and rode on, with the officers closing round him, towards the town.

Cicero stood at the milestone in the dust with his hand still out. Behind him the line of men in good cloaks came up, slowly, and passed him, one group after another, on their way back to Brundisium; and some of them nodded to him as they passed, and some of them did not, and some of them, he saw, were looking at him with an expression he knew very well, because he had worn it himself on the harbour wall, when the edict was posted with his name on it, alone.

Tiro came up beside him and stood there, saying nothing, and after a while held out a cloth for the dust. Cicero took it and wiped his face.

“Well,” he said. “The lictors.”

“I’ll see to it,” said Tiro.
