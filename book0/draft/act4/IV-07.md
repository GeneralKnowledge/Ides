# The Towers

POV: Nysa · Rome, the chief priest’s house on the Sacred Way · August 47

---

The towers went up in the Forum in three days, and Nysa watched them grow from the roof, because by then the roof was the only place in the house from which the Forum could be seen at all.

She had found that out in the spring, when the trouble about the law began. If you went up the ladder from the kitchen yard onto the flat roof where the washing hung, and then along the gutter to the corner where the roof of the house met the roof of the Regia, and then lay down on the tiles, you could see between the Regia’s roof and the Vestals’ wall a slice of the Forum: the bottom of the Sacred Way, the steps of the temple of Castor, and a piece of the open paving by the Rostra. It was not much. But it was more than anybody else in the house could see, and nobody else in the house knew about it except Dama, who had shown her.

So she lay on the tiles in the evenings, when the washing was in, and watched the towers.

They were made of timber. Men brought it in carts, in the night, beams and planks and ladders, and in the mornings there was more of it, and the towers were higher. There were two of them that she could see, one at the end of the Sacred Way and one by the Rostra, square, of rough planks nailed to a frame, with a platform on top where men stood with stones. They looked like the siege towers in the pictures on the walls of the master’s long room, except that they were not going anywhere. They were there to stop the soldiers coming in. Dama said so. Dama said that the tribune Dolabella had built them, or his people had, the men from the Subura who wanted their debts let off, so that when the other tribune came with his people to stop the law being voted, they could not get into the Forum, and the people could vote.

“Who’s the other tribune?” said Nysa.

“Trebellius.”

“Which side is the master on?”

Dama thought. He was ten now, and could explain most things. “Nobody knows,” he said at last. “He’s in Asia.”

---

She counted the people in the house that week, because the steward asked her to.

He had come to the bench by the side door on the second day of the towers and stood in front of her with his tablet and said that he wanted to know, every evening at dusk and every morning at first light, exactly who was in the house; every slave, every freedman, every guest, every child. He wanted it written down. He wanted it from her, because she counted, and because she did not lose count when people moved about, which the porters did. He said it in his flat voice, not looking at her, as if it were a thing about oil.

“Why?” she said, before she could stop herself.

He looked at her then. “Because if anything happens in the Forum,” he said, “the mistress will want to know whether everyone is in. And I don’t want to have to go looking.”

So she counted. It was easy, in a way. The house had forty-three people in it that summer, counting the mistress and her two women and the steward and Phileros and the porters and the kitchen and the wool room and the stable and Dama; she knew them all, and where they slept, and she went round at dusk with her tablet and saw each one and made a mark. Forty-three. At first light she went round again. Forty-three. She gave the tablet to the steward at the door of his room and he looked at it and nodded and gave it back.

On the fourth night the noise began.

---

It began very early, before light, when she was asleep. She woke because the house was awake round her: feet on the stairs, voices, a door. Then she heard it from outside, from the Forum. Shouting, and a sound under the shouting that she had never heard before and did not know, a regular sound, like a great many men stamping together on stone. Then a crash, of timber. Then screaming.

She got up. Chreste’s pallet was empty.

She stood and looked at it. The blanket was thrown back. Chreste’s sandals were gone from under the shelf, and her cloak from the peg. Nysa knew where she had gone. She had known for a week that Chreste had a soldier, one of the men from Antony’s guard who stood about on the Capitol, a big laughing Samnite who bought her cakes from the stall by the temple of Saturn; and that she went out to him, late, by the side door, which Phileros locked at dark, with a key she had got from somewhere; and came back before first light. Nysa had heard her go and heard her come back, every night that week, and had said nothing. And now it was nearly first light and the pallet was empty and there was screaming in the Forum.

She went up to the roof.

She did not decide to; she went, up the ladder in her shift with her feet bare, along the gutter to the corner, and lay down on the cold tiles. It was getting light. The sky over the Palatine was grey. She looked down through her slice.

The tower at the end of the Sacred Way was down. It lay across the paving in pieces, beams and planks, as if a giant had trodden on it. There were soldiers in the Forum, a great many, in armour, with shields, more than she could count; they had come down from the Capitol, she could see, because more of them were still coming, down the steps from the Capitol into the Forum in a column, with the light catching their helmets. They were in a line across the paving. In front of them the people from the towers were running. Some were not running. Some were lying on the paving.

She saw soldiers at the Rostra pulling something down. Boards: the white boards where the laws were posted, where Dolabella’s law had been painted. They were tearing them off the Rostra and breaking them on the paving with the butts of their spears.

And she saw, at the top of the Capitol steps, on the edge of the rock, against the sky, men being pushed. Two soldiers holding a man by the arms, and walking him to the edge, and then letting go. And the man was gone. She did not see him fall. She saw the edge, and the soldiers standing at it, and then turning round and going back for another.

She lay on the tiles. She did not count them. It was the only time in her life she could remember not counting, when there was something there to count.

---

She was still on the roof when Chreste came over the wall.

She saw her first: a shape at the back of the house, in the lane behind the stables, where the wall was lowest, climbing. Chreste had her cloak bunched up round her waist and her sandals in her teeth and she came over the top of the wall the way a cat comes, quick and badly, and dropped into the stable yard and fell over, and got up again, and stood there in the grey light with her hands over her mouth.

Nysa went down the ladder. She got to the stable yard as Chreste was trying the stable door, and took her by the arm, and Chreste turned round with a face like chalk.

“They were throwing people,” Chreste said. “Off the rock. I saw. I was on the steps. He told me to run, he pushed me, he said run, and I ran.”

“Be quiet.”

“They were *throwing* them.”

“Be quiet,” Nysa said, “and come in.”

She took her through the stable, and out by the little door into the kitchen yard, and in at the back of the kitchen, where the fire was not yet lit and the cook was not yet up, and nobody was there. She sat her down on the flour bin. She took the sandals out of her hand and put them on her feet. She took the cloak off her and folded it and put it under the bin. Then she went to the passage door and stood in it, and listened, and heard the steward’s voice at the front of the house, and Phileros’s, and the porter’s, all talking at once, about the soldiers.

She had the tablet in her belt. She had made the evening count, the night before: forty-three. She took it out, and the stylus. Then she went round the house, as she did every morning at first light, room by room, and looked at each person and made a mark.

When she came to the room under the stairs she made a mark for Chreste, against the place where Chreste slept, though Chreste was sitting on the flour bin in the kitchen with her cloak under it. She made it neatly. Forty-three.

She gave the tablet to the steward at the door of his room. He looked at it and nodded.

“All in?”

“All in.”

“Good.” He gave it back. His hands were not quite steady. “The soldiers have cleared the Forum,” he said, not to her, to the passage. “Antony’s. From the Capitol. He’s broken the law tablets.” He stopped. “There’ll be no more of it now.”

He went away to the mistress. Nysa stood in the passage with the tablet. It said forty-three, all in, all where they slept, and it was true now, and it would be true on any day that anybody looked at it.

In the kitchen Chreste was still on the flour bin. She had stopped shaking. She was looking at the cold hearth.

“He pushed me,” she said. “He said run.”

“I know.”

“He was one of them, Nysa. He had his sword out. He was one of the ones going up the steps. And he saw me and he pushed me and said run.” Chreste looked up. “What does that make him?”

Nysa did not know. She sat down on the bin beside her and they waited together for the cook.
