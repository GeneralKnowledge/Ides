# Quirites

POV: Laberius · Rome, the Field of Mars · October 47

---

The general came out to them on foot, from the city side, in the middle of the morning, with nobody but his lictors and a few friends, and the whole Field of Mars stood up.

Laberius stood up with the rest. He had been sitting on his shield in the grass, as he had sat for three days, in the place where his century had stopped when the march stopped, between the river and the voting enclosures, with the city wall on the right and the temples behind it going up the Capitol, the first time in his life he had seen Rome. He had thought it would look bigger. It looked like a town with a great many roofs. The legions were camped all over the Field, the old legions from Campania, all of them, by cohort and by century, with their standards planted in the turf, in their kit, with their swords; they had been let in as far as this armed with nothing but their swords, and they had come no further. They had sat here three days on the grass in front of the city, waiting for him. And now he was coming.

He came through them. Laberius watched him do it. He came through the middle of the Field with his lictors in front of him and the legions on either side, and the men stood up as he passed and fell silent, cohort after cohort, the silence going along in front of him like a wave in front of a ship. He did not look at them. He walked to the tribunal, which somebody had set up at the river end of the Field, a platform of planks, and went up onto it, and stood there, and looked out at them all.

Laberius was not near. He was perhaps a hundred paces off, in the middle of his century, with Gaius on his left and Vibius behind. He could see the general’s head, bare, and his shoulders, and a red cloak. He could not see his face. He did not need to. He knew what it would look like. It would look the way it had at Placentia, and at Dyrrachium after the old camp: calm, and level, and attending, and not angry; and he stood there in the grass and waited for whatever was going to happen, the way he had waited at Placentia for the helmet.

---

The general asked them what they wanted.

He asked it quite simply, in the clear voice that carried to the back of the Field without seeming to be raised. What did they want? They had marched on Rome. They had come here, armed, to the gates of the city, and sat down. They must want something. Let them say what.

There was a pause. Then somebody near the front, in the Tenth, shouted that they wanted their discharge. And the shout was taken up, along the front ranks and then back through the Field, *discharge, discharge*, every man shouting it, Gaius shouting it, Laberius hearing his own mouth open on it; because that was the word they had agreed on, over the summer, round the fires in Campania. Not money. Not land. Discharge. Because if they asked for their discharge he could not give it to them, not with Africa waiting and the Pompey boys and Juba’s elephants; he needed them; he would have to say no. And then he would have to give them what they really wanted to make them stay. Everybody knew that. It was the plan. Vibius had explained it.

The general let them shout. Then he held up his hand and they stopped.

“Very well,” he said. “I discharge you.”

---

Laberius did not understand at first. Nobody did. There was a silence over the whole Field, a different silence, as if every man in it had stopped breathing at once.

The general went on. He said that they were discharged. All of them, every legion, every man. That they had served long and well and were tired, and that he would not keep tired men in his army against their will. He said that they would have every reward he had promised them, every sesterce and every iugerum, in full, when he came back from Africa and triumphed with the other soldiers, the ones who had gone with him. He said that he was grateful to them for what they had done. He said that he wished them well.

And he called them *Quirites*.

Laberius heard the word go out over the Field, in the general’s level voice, and did not at first know why it went into him like a cold blade. *Quirites.* Citizens. It was what the magistrates called the people in the Forum, at an assembly: the men in togas, the shopkeepers and the bakers and the men who voted. It was not what you called soldiers. You called soldiers *soldiers*, *comrades*, *men*; the general had always called them *comrades*, at Brundisium on the mole, at Placentia, at Dyrrachium, since Gaul. He had never once called them anything else. And now he was standing on a platform of planks at the edge of the Field of Mars calling them *citizens*, as if they were not his any more, as if they were the crowd in the Forum, as if he had already sailed for Africa with other men and they were standing on the shore.

Somebody near the front made a sound. Then somebody else. Then the whole of the front of the Field, the Tenth, was making it: a groan, low, the sound the Ninth had made at Placentia when the general said *one in ten*. And then men were shouting, not *discharge* now, something else, *no*, *general*, *not that*, *take us back*; and men were going down on their knees in the grass in front of the tribunal, the whole front rank of the Tenth on their knees with their hands out, weeping, shouting that they would go to Africa, they would go anywhere, they would go now, only let him take them back, only let him not call them that.

Laberius was on his knees too.

He did not know when he had gone down. He was on his knees in the grass of the Field of Mars with his hands out in front of him like a beggar’s, and his mouth open, and his face wet, and he was shouting something; he did not know what. He heard Gaius beside him shouting. He heard the whole century round him shouting, and the whole legion, and the whole Field, one noise, rolling up towards the platform of planks where the general stood with his hands behind his back and looked at them and let them do it.

He let them do it for a long time.

---

Afterwards there were terms. The general came down off the tribunal at last and walked along the front rank of the Tenth, and spoke to some of them, and the word came back through the Field from mouth to mouth, broken up and put together again, so that by the time it reached Laberius’s century in the middle it had been told forty times and was nearly smooth.

He would take them back. All of them, except a few, who had been the worst; he would not say who. They would go to Africa with him. And when the wars were over, every man would have what he had been promised, and more: the money, the rest of it, part now and part afterwards, with interest for the waiting. And land. Every man would have land. From the public land, and from the general’s own estates, his own, where the public land did not stretch. And it would be given out so that no man in Italy lost his farm for it. Not like Sulla. Nobody would be turned off. It would be done properly, the plots set apart, here and there, wherever there was land that belonged to nobody or to the general himself, so that the soldiers would have their farms and the farmers would keep theirs and nobody would curse a veteran’s name at a boundary stone.

Land. Laberius heard the word come down the Field, and something in his chest that had been clenched for three days, for a month, for a year, since the dark plain by the Volturnus and the old man in Capua with the stone round his neck, let go.

Land. Not taken from anybody. His own.

Gaius was laughing beside him, still on his knees, with his face wet, laughing and wiping his face with the back of his hand. Vibius was standing up, behind, brushing the grass off his knees, with his tablet already out. The whole century was getting up off the ground, slowly, the way men get up after a thing they will not talk about afterwards; and they were not looking at each other, any of them, and Laberius was not looking at them.

---

The clerks came round in the afternoon.

They came in pairs, with tablets and lamp-boys, though it was not yet dark, going along the lines of the legions from century to century, taking names. Laberius saw them coming a long way off. He stood at the head of his century and waited. When they came to him he gave his name, all three of it, and his century, and his legion, and the years he had served, and watched the stylus go into the wax.

“Is this for the land?” he said.

The clerk did not look up. He was a young man, a freedman by his ring, with ink on his fingers and a cold. “It’s the roll,” he said.

“For the land.”

“For everything.” The clerk sniffed. “Discharge, rewards, the lot. When it comes.”

“When will it come?”

The clerk looked up then. He looked at Laberius, at his face, and his belt, and his boots, the way the silversmith in Capua had looked at him, and then down at his tablet again, and wrote the next line.

“Next,” he said.

Laberius stood there. The clerk had already moved on to Gaius, who was giving his name, all three of it, and laughing at something, and the lamp-boy was holding the lamp up so the clerk could see, though there was light enough. Laberius did not ask again. He stepped back into his place.

A little way along the line, in front of the Tenth’s standards, a man was standing by himself with his arms folded. He was a big man, older than Laberius, grey, with a scar that pulled his upper lip up on one side so that he seemed always about to say something. Laberius knew him to nod to. He had seen him at Pharsalus, on the right, and on the road out of Campania, and they had nodded to each other on the road, as men do. He was looking at the clerks going along the lines with their tablets. He watched them for a while. Then he turned his head and spat in the grass, carefully, the way a man spits who has decided to, and went back to watching.

Laberius looked at the spit in the grass. Then he looked at the man. The man did not look back.

---

That night the century slept on the Field, as it had for three nights, under the city wall with the temples going up behind it and the lamps coming on in the roofs. In the morning, they said, they would march back to Campania, and from there to the coast, and from the coast to Sicily, and from Sicily to Africa.

Laberius lay under his cloak by the fire with his boots off and his pack under his head. He could feel the cup in it, through the spare tunic, wrapped in its rag, against the back of his skull: hard, and cold, and the shape of men and horses going round. He did not take it out.

He thought about a field. A field of good ground, flat and dark, with an elm in it, and a vine up the elm. He did not know where it was. Nobody had said. Nobody had said when, either. But he could see it, quite clearly, lying there by the fire on the Field of Mars, with Gaius snoring beside him and the city’s lamps going out one by one in the roofs; and he lay and looked at it for a long time, the elm and the vine and the dark ground, and the wall round it, and the gate, until he was asleep.
