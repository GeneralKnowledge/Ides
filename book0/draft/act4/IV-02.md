# Plinths

POV: Nysa · Rome, the chief priest’s house and the Sacred Way · October 48

---

The statue at the corner by the Regia had been Pompey’s, and on the morning after the news it was not there any more, and Nysa had walked past the empty place three times before she noticed.

It was the plinth she noticed, in the end. She was going down to the fountain with the small jars, as she did every morning, and something about the corner was wrong; it was too light, as if a tree had been cut. She stopped. The plinth was still there, a block of white stone as high as her shoulder, with letters cut in the front of it that she did not stop to read. But there was nothing on top of it except two iron pins sticking up out of the stone, bent, where the feet had been fixed. And at the bottom of the plinth, on the paving, there were chips of white marble, and a long scrape where something heavy had been dragged.

She stood and looked at the pins. There was a man sweeping the chips into a pile with a broom, slowly, a public slave, and he looked up at her and shrugged.

“In the night,” he said. “They had ropes.”

“Who?”

“Everybody.” He went on sweeping. “There’s another one gone in the Forum, by the Rostra. And Sulla’s. They took Sulla’s too, while they were at it.” He seemed to think that was funny. “Sulla’s been dead thirty years. Nobody’s ever touched him. Then last night, ropes.”

---

The news had come the day before, in the afternoon, by the Ostia road; and like the false news in the summer it had come into the house by the side door, with the fish. Pompey was dead. He had fled after the battle in Greece, the big one, the one that had finished it; he had gone by sea to Egypt, because the king there owed him something, and the king’s people had come out to his ship in a little boat to fetch him ashore, and had killed him in the boat, in front of his wife, who was watching from the ship. They had cut off his head. They had kept it, men said, in salt, to give to Caesar when he came.

The kitchen had been very quiet after the fishmonger’s boy told it. Then the cook had said that she supposed that was the war over, and nobody had said anything, and the cook had gone back to the fish.

This time the mistress did not wait for a letter. She sent the steward out into the Forum to find out, and he came back in an hour with his face set, and went in to her, and was in there a long time. When he came out he said only that it was true, and that the master was in Egypt, and that the house would go on as it was.

Nysa thought that it meant the end. She thought it all that night, lying on her pallet. The war had been between the master and Pompey; she knew that, everybody knew that, it was the one thing about it that even Dama could explain. And Pompey was dead. So there was nobody for the master to fight. So he would come home, and the soldiers would come home, and the lamps would be doubled again, and then they would stay doubled, and the house would be the master’s house with the master in it, every day. She thought about that for a long time. She found she was not sure whether she wanted it.

---

The second thing that happened that week was the boy.

She saw him by accident. It was the fourth morning, and she was at the street door, which was open again now, with the door slaves on their bench outside it in the sun; she had been sent to bring in the lamp from the porch. And there was a little procession going up the Sacred Way past the house, towards the Forum and the Capitol beyond it: not a big one, a family, a dozen people and their slaves, walking slowly, the women in front in their good cloaks and the men behind. In the middle of it, walking by himself, there was a boy in a new toga.

She knew him. It took her a moment. Then she did: it was the thin boy from the dinner last winter, the one at the end of the lowest couch whom nobody had talked to, with the thin shoes. His mother was in front of him, the handsome woman with the gold; and the stout quiet husband beside her; and some others Nysa did not know.

He was going up to the Capitol to put on the man’s toga. She knew what it was because Chreste’s brother had done it, the year before, in the household where he belonged, and Chreste had talked about it for a month: the boy’s toga with the purple border taken off and laid on the altar, and the plain white one of a man put on, and the sacrifice, and the family going home to dinner. And there he was, going up to do it, past the door of the house with the lamps, at the foot of the hill.

The toga was too big for him. She saw that at once, because it was the kind of thing she saw. It had been made by somebody who had expected him to grow into it, or who had made it for a bigger boy, and it hung off his thin shoulders in folds that were too deep and dragged on the ground at the back, so that he had to walk carefully, a little stiffly, holding it up with one hand at his side the way a woman holds a skirt. He was very pale. He looked straight in front of him. He did not look at the house as he passed it, or at the door, or at her.

His mother turned and said something to him. He nodded. Then the procession went on round the corner by the Regia, past the empty plinth with the two bent pins, and was gone.

Nysa went in with the lamp. She did not tell anybody about the toga. There was nobody to tell; nobody in the kitchen knew who the boy was, and if she said *the thin boy from the dinner*, they would not remember the dinner.

But the mistress knew. In the afternoon the steward came to the wool room and said that the mistress wished a length of the good linen, the fine Egyptian, to be cut and hemmed and sent across the city that evening, to the house of the master’s niece, with the mistress’s compliments, for the boy. And Nysa cut it and hemmed it, because her hems were the straightest, and folded it in a cloth, and gave it to the boy who carried messages; and she thought, folding it, that it was a lot of linen for a thin boy, and then that it was probably not for him to wear.

---

On the seventh day there was a joke about the head.

Chreste brought it home. She had been to the Velabrum for cheese and she came in at the side door with her face red from laughing and her basket over her arm and could hardly tell it for laughing. It was going round the markets. The king of Egypt, it said, had sent Caesar Pompey’s head in a jar of salt as a gift. And Caesar had looked at it, and wept, and turned away; and the king’s man had asked, very anxious, whether there was anything wrong with it. And Caesar had said: *No. But I asked for the rest of him as well. He owes me a great deal of money.*

The kitchen roared. The cook laughed so much she had to sit down. Even Phileros, who was passing with the keys, stopped in the doorway and made a noise in his nose.

Nysa did not laugh. She stood by the bench with the wicks in her lap and looked at the floor and pressed her lips together very hard. It was funny. That was the trouble. It was very funny, because everybody knew that Pompey had owed money all over the East, and Caesar had owed money all over Rome, and the idea of the one trying to collect from the other, from a head in a jar, was so stupid and so horrible that it went straight through her before she could stop it; and she knew, at the same time, with a part of her that was entirely cold, that it was a joke about the master. About the master’s debts. Told in the master’s kitchen. And that the steward was in the next room.

“What’s the matter with you?” said Chreste, wiping her eyes. “Don’t you get it?”

“I get it.”

“Then laugh.”

“I’m laughing,” Nysa said, and bent over the wicks, and went on with them until Chreste gave up and went off to tell the girls in the wool room.
