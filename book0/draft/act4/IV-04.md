# A New Stool

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Forum, by the Janus · March 47

---

The stool appeared on the Kalends of March, on the other side of the Janus arch, opposite Hordeonius’s board, at about the distance a man could throw a cup.

He noticed it at once, because nobody had put a stool there in thirty years. It was a plain folding stool of the kind soldiers carry, with a leather seat, and on it sat a short, broad, bald man in a good tunic with a soldier’s belt and no toga, and beside him on the paving was a strongbox with iron corners, and leaning against the strongbox was a board with a tally of figures painted on it in red, like a wine shop’s prices. The man sat with his hands on his knees and looked out at the Forum with an expression of great contentment. He did not look at Hordeonius.

“Who is that?” Hordeonius said.

Sosia had already found out. Sosia always found out. “Bucco,” he said. “Gaius Septimius Bucco. He’s been an army contractor, in Gaul and in Greece. Mules. Then he lent to the soldiers, in Greece, against their pay. Now he’s here.” Sosia lowered his voice, though there was no need. “They say it’s Antony’s money.”

“Who says?”

“Everybody on the colonnade. They say Antony’s people brought him the box.”

Hordeonius looked at the box. It had a seal on the hasp. From this distance he could not see whose. He looked at the board with the red figures. They were rates, he saw, for small sums, a month at a time, to be paid out of pay. The rates were very high. Much higher than his. They were the rates of a man who expected a great many of his borrowers not to come back from wherever they were going, and had priced it in.

“Soldiers,” he said.

“Soldiers and their widows. And the men coming back from Greece, waiting for their discharge money.” Sosia shrugged. “It’s a trade.”

“It is not my trade.”

“No,” said Sosia. “But it’s opposite.”

---

It was the month of Dolabella’s law.

Dolabella was tribune. He had been tribune since the winter, and he had spent the winter making speeches, and the speeches had all been about the same thing, which was that the poor of Rome were crushed by debts they could not pay and rents they could not pay, and that the Republic could not stand while its citizens were the slaves of their creditors and their landlords; and in the spring he had proposed a law. It cancelled debts. Not some debts. All of them. And rents, for the year. The law was posted on the Rostra and read out in the Forum, and the Forum had heard it, and since then the Forum had been divided into two halves, roughly along the line of the Sacred Way, with the men who owed on one side and the men who were owed on the other, and Hordeonius, at his board, in the shade of the basilica, sitting very precisely in the middle.

He had read the law. He had read it on the day it was posted, as he had read Caesar’s, and Caelius’s, and a dozen others in thirty years. It was not well drafted. It was long and angry and full of words like *crushed*. It would not pass. Trebellius, the other tribune, had already said he would veto it, and Trebellius was a sensible man with debts of his own, but small ones, and a strong sense of what would happen to him if every debt in Rome were cancelled and every debtor in Rome came to thank Dolabella for it.

What troubled Hordeonius was not the law. It was Dolabella.

Dolabella was Caesar’s man. He had gone over to Caesar in the first week of the war, and fought for him, and been honoured by him, and was married to Cicero’s daughter. He was not a Caelius, a clever young man with nothing to lose. He was Caesar’s own. And if Caesar’s own man stood up on the Rostra in the middle of Caesar’s dictatorship and proposed to cancel every debt in Rome, then either Caesar wanted it done, or Caesar did not know, or Caesar was in Egypt and nobody in Rome could ask him; and none of the three was a thing Hordeonius could price.

And Dolabella owed him eighty thousand.

He did not say this to Sosia. Sosia knew; Sosia kept the tablet. But they did not speak of it. Dolabella had borrowed in the year before the war, to pay for his marriage, a modest sum by the standards of young men who married consulars’ daughters, and had paid the interest for a year and then stopped, as young men did, and Hordeonius had waited, as he did. And now the young man who owed him eighty thousand was standing on the Rostra in front of the whole of Rome proposing to cancel it, and the eighty thousand with it, and every other line on every other tablet in the Forum; and Hordeonius sat at his board and watched him do it, and listened to the crowd cheer, and thought that he had never before been in the position of hearing one of his own debtors applauded for not paying him.

---

On the Ides there was a crowd round Bucco’s stool.

Hordeonius watched it gather. It was soldiers, mostly: men just back from Greece, discharged or waiting to be, in their old cloaks and their army boots, with their faces burnt brown, standing about the stool in a loose queue and counting on their fingers. Bucco sat in the middle of them like a farmer at a fair. He lent to them one at a time. He did not use a tablet; he had a clerk beside him with a tablet, and the clerk wrote, and Bucco looked at each man and said a figure, and the man nodded or did not, and the strongbox was opened and the coin counted out and the strongbox shut. When it was shut Hordeonius could see the seal on the hasp.

He got up. He said to Sosia that he was going to stretch his legs, and he walked across the paving, slowly, past the Janus, the way a man walks who is going nowhere in particular, and passed close behind the queue, and looked at the hasp.

It was a ring seal, pressed into a lump of red wax. A head of Hercules, bearded, with the lion-skin over it. Antony’s family claimed descent from Hercules; everybody knew that; Antony said it at dinners, and wore the lion on his ring, and was proud of it, and his enemies said it was the only ancestor he had who could be found. It was Antony’s seal. Or a seal made to look like it.

He walked back to his board. He sat down.

“Well?” said Sosia.

“Hercules.”

Sosia whistled under his breath.

Hordeonius sat for a while, thinking. Then he said what he thought, slowly, to Sosia and to himself. “Antony is Master of the Horse. Antony has Rome while Caesar is away. And Antony has put money on that stool, to be lent out at a rate, to be paid back.” He turned it over. “A man who lends does not want debts cancelled. A man who lends wants them paid. If Antony is lending, then Antony is a creditor, and Antony will not let Dolabella’s law through.” He folded his hands. “That stool is the best news we have had all month.”

Sosia looked at him doubtfully. “Or it’s a stool.”

“It is never only a stool,” Hordeonius said. He was pleased with that, and said it again to himself, and wiped his fingers.

---

Visellius came that afternoon, which was not the Kalends. He had never come on any other day than the Kalends. He came to the board with his satchel and stood in front of it, neat and narrow, in a clean toga, and said that he wished to pay off a part of the principal, ten thousand, early; and he counted it out on the board in stacks of ten, squaring each one with his thumb.

Hordeonius watched him.

“Why now?” he said, when the stacks were down.

“Because of the law.” Visellius closed the satchel. “My own debtors are all waiting for it to pass. None of them will pay me a copper till they know. So I have money that I would have lent, and nobody to lend it to, because nobody will borrow, because they’re all hoping not to have to pay.” He looked at Hordeonius with his clear, careful eyes. “If the law passes, I lose what they owe me. I would rather owe you less on the day it does.” He paused. “That’s all.”

Hordeonius looked at the stacks. He thought about Dolabella’s eighty thousand, which would never be paid now whatever happened to the law, he was nearly sure, because a man who had proposed to cancel his own debts in front of the whole city would never again be in a position to pay them without looking a fool. And here was Visellius, who owed a fraction of it, paying it down early in case the law let him off.

“Sosia,” he said. “Write it.”

Across the arch, Bucco’s queue had grown. A soldier at the front of it, a big man with a sunburnt neck, had his hand out for his coins and was saying something to Bucco that made Bucco laugh. Hordeonius did not hear what. He watched the strongbox shut, and the red seal on the hasp, and the soldier go away with his money, counting it.
