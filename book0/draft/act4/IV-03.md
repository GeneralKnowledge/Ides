# Master of the Horse

POV: Antony · Rome, his house below the Esquiline · December 48

---

The edict had two names on it, and Antony had added the second himself, at the bottom, in his own hand, because Caesar’s letter said *Cicero and others as you think fit* and Antony could think of only one other.

He had done it at the table in the morning, with his secretary waiting, and then he had looked at it and laughed and signed it, and the secretary had taken it away to be copied and posted, and he had forgotten about it until the evening, when somebody at dinner mentioned it.

“Laelius,” said the man on the next couch. “Why Laelius?”

“He asked.”

“That’s all?”

“He asked nicely.” Antony held out his cup. “Cicero didn’t ask at all. That’s why he’s on it. If I’d waited for him to ask I’d be waiting till the Saturnalia after next, and Caesar wants him home. Caesar likes him. The gods know why. He makes jokes about everybody and they’re all about Caesar.” He drank. “So now he’s home. Or as near as he’ll come. He’ll sit in Brundisium with his lictors and write to Atticus that I’ve insulted him by sparing him.”

The table laughed. Antony liked that. He liked the table. He looked down it, at the faces, red in the lamplight, and the cups, and the girls, and the two mimes from the theatre who had come with Cytheris and were doing something with a napkin at the far end, and he counted them without meaning to, as he always did, and there were twenty-two at dinner tonight, in his house, which had three dining couches meant for nine. He had had couches brought in from the garden. Some of the guests were sitting on the floor. Nobody minded. He was the Master of the Horse.

---

He had been Master of the Horse for two months. He was still not used to it.

Caesar had made him so after Pharsalus, from Thessaly, in a letter: Caesar was dictator again, for a year, and a dictator must have a Master of the Horse, and it was to be Antony, and he was to go to Rome and hold it. Hold Rome. Hold Italy. While Caesar went after Pompey, wherever Pompey had gone. That had been in the autumn. And then Pompey had turned out to have gone to Egypt and been killed on the beach, and Caesar had gone to Egypt after him and found him dead, and had stayed. And nobody in Rome knew why. And Caesar’s letters, when they came, were short and few and said nothing about when he was coming back.

So Antony held Rome.

It was easier than he had expected and harder than it looked. Easier because there was nobody to stop him. The consuls had gone. The Senate did what it was told, mostly, and when it did not he did not ask it. He had six lictors, as a Master of the Horse did, and he took them everywhere, with their axes in the rods, inside the city, which no magistrate had done in Rome since anyone could remember; and when people stared at the axes he stared back. He wore a sword in the Forum. He wore it because Caesar had told him to keep order, and because when he walked through the Forum with a sword at his hip and six lictors with axes in front of him the Forum got out of his way, and he liked to feel it get out of his way, like water round a ship. Harder, because he did not know what anything cost. He did not mean money. He meant the other thing. He did a thing, and then three days later somebody came to him with a face, and told him what it had cost, and he had not known it would.

---

Cytheris was at the far end of the table with her mimes.

She had come with him from Tibur in a litter, as she went everywhere with him now; he took her about Italy in an open litter when he went to look at the towns, with his lictors in front and the litter behind and her friends from the theatre in carriages after that, and the towns came out to look. He knew what they said about it. He knew what Cicero would say about it, if Cicero ever came back to Rome to say anything: that the Master of the Horse of the Roman people went about Italy with an actress in a litter, a freedwoman, a woman who had been owned. He did not care. She was funny and she was beautiful and she was quick, and she made him laugh, and she knew how a room was going before he did, which was saying a good deal. She was watching him now, down the length of the table, over the heads of the guests, with her chin on her hand. She lifted one eyebrow. He lifted his cup.

He was not looking at her, really. He was looking, past her, at the door to the garden, where his wife was not.

His wife was upstairs. She did not come to these dinners. She had come to the first two, in the autumn, and had lain on the couch beside him with her hands folded and said very little and eaten very little and gone up early, and after that she had stopped coming. He did not blame her. She was his cousin, and his uncle’s daughter, and a quiet, sensible, well-read woman who had been a good match for a man of his family when he had had no money and needed a wife who would not cost him any, and she did not like noise, or actresses, or mimes doing things with napkins. She had her own rooms. She read. She had her own friends, who came in the afternoons.

One of them was Dolabella.

He did not know that. He had no reason to know it. There was no reason for Dolabella, who was a tribune this year and a young man and married to Cicero’s daughter, to come in the afternoons to the rooms of the Master of the Horse’s wife; except that Dolabella’s house was not far, and Dolabella was a charming man who was charming to everyone, and Antonia was a sensible woman who liked to be talked to about books. A slave had said something to a slave, who had said something to Antony’s steward, who had said it to nobody, but who had a way of not saying it that Antony had begun to notice.

He did not believe it. He found that he thought about it a great deal for a thing he did not believe.

---

“Marcus.” It was the man on the next couch again, leaning over, low. He was a knight Antony owed money to, a little, which was why he was on the next couch. “The edict. Can I ask. There are men of mine in Brundisium. Pompeians. Good men, family men, who only went over because their patron did. They’ve been told to leave Italy, under your edict. They have nowhere to go. If there were any way that they could be added. To the names.”

Antony looked at him.

He did the sum before he could stop himself, as he always did. What the man was worth. What he was owed. What the men in Brundisium were worth to the man, and therefore what a line added to the edict would be worth, at the bottom, in his own hand. And what Caesar would say, from Egypt, if he heard that the Master of the Horse was adding names to an edict for knights he owed money to. He arrived at a figure. It was a good figure. It was so good that he laughed out loud, and the knight laughed too, nervously, not knowing why.

“Write me their names,” Antony said. “Tomorrow. I’ll see.”

“Thank you. I’m grateful. Truly.”

“Don’t be grateful. I haven’t done it.” He drank. “I might not.”

The knight lay back on his couch, satisfied. Antony did not look at him again. He looked down the table at Cytheris, who was watching him with her chin on her hand and an expression that said she had heard every word; and then at the garden door; and then at the ceiling, where above the painted plaster and the beams, in her own rooms, his wife was reading by a lamp, or was not.

He called for more wine. Somebody started a song, and he sang it, loud, louder than anyone, the way he did, so that the whole table could hear him and would know where he was.
