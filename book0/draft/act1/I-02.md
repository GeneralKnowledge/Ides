# The Empty Street

POV: Nysa · Rome, the chief priest’s house on the Sacred Way · January 49

---

The litters started going past before it was light, and Nysa counted them because she was awake and there was nothing else to do with her hands.

She was on the bench by the side door with the lamp baskets, trimming wicks. It was the work she was given when the cook did not want her in the kitchen, and the cook had not wanted anyone in the kitchen since yesterday, when the fish had not come. Through the grille in the side door she could see a slice of the Sacred Way, wet stones and the corner of the Vestals’ wall, and the litters going across it from left to right, downhill, towards the Forum and whatever was beyond the Forum. She did not know what was beyond it. A gate, she supposed. All the roads out of Rome ended in a gate.

Eleven. Twelve. She counted in Greek, under her breath, and moved the wick scissors on to the next lamp. Thirteen had a man walking beside it with a lantern on a pole and a strongbox on his shoulder, and the strongbox was heavy, she could see it in the way he walked, bent to the side like a man with one short leg.

“What are you doing?” said Chreste.

Chreste had come up behind her with her hair still down, in her blanket. She slept on the pallet next to Nysa’s in the room under the stairs and she woke badly, always, as if waking were something that had been done to her on purpose.

“Wicks.”

“You were counting. Your mouth goes.”

“Litters,” Nysa said. “Thirteen.”

Chreste put her face to the grille. She was taller and could see more of the street. She watched for a while without saying anything, which was not like her, and then she said, “That’s the Metelli. That one. With the blue curtains. I know the boy who walks in front.”

“Where are they going?”

“Away.” Chreste pulled the blanket closer. “Everyone’s going away. Didn’t you hear the cook?”

Nysa had heard the cook. The cook had said a great many things yesterday, most of them about fish, and somewhere in among the fish she had said that the consuls had gone and the Senate had gone and Pompey the Great had gone, all of them in a day, as if somebody had shouted fire. Nysa had not known what to do with that, so she had done nothing with it. It had seemed to her a thing that happened in the Forum, to men in togas, and the Forum was at the bottom of the street and had always been full of men in togas shouting, and they had always gone home again afterwards.

“They’ve left the treasury open,” Chreste said. “The consul. He went in to get the money out for Pompey and somebody ran in and said Caesar’s horsemen were at the gate, and he ran out again and left the door standing. The fishmonger’s boy told Dama. Dama told me.” She laughed, but quietly. “There aren’t any horsemen. Dama went and looked.”

“Dama’s eight.”

“Dama’s the only one who went and looked.”

Fourteen. Fifteen. A cart, not a litter, with a mule that did not want to go downhill and a man hitting it with a stick. Nysa did not count the cart. She did not know whether carts counted.

---

Phileros came down at the second hour, which was early for him, and stood in the atrium with the big ring of keys in his hand and looked at the street door for a long time.

The house was full of people who had nothing to do. That was the strange thing. Nobody had been told to stop working, but nobody had been told to do anything either, and the steward had gone out at first light to see the master’s agents and had not come back. So the girls from the wool room were in the colonnade, sitting, and the two door slaves were sitting on their bench inside the door instead of outside it, and the boys from the stable had come in from the back and were standing about by the kitchen with their hands under their arms, and the cook was in the kitchen banging things. Dama was on the flour bin with his knees up. Everyone was pretending to be somewhere for a reason.

Phileros turned round. He looked at all of them in the colonnade, one after the other, as if he were counting too, and then he went to the street door and put the bar across it himself. It was a heavy oak bar and it went into its iron brackets with a noise that went through the whole house. Then he locked the small door in the big one, and put the key on his ring.

“Nobody goes out the front,” he said. “If you have to go out, you go out the side and you come back the side, and you tell me first. Nobody stands in the street. Nobody talks to anybody in the street.” He looked at the door slaves. “If anybody knocks, you don’t open. You ask through the grille who they are, and you come and tell me, and I’ll say.”

“Who’s going to knock?” said one of the stable boys.

Phileros did not answer that. He went away towards the back of the house, where the mistress’s rooms were, with the keys in his fist so they would not make a noise.

It was noon. Nysa knew because the sun had come round onto the colonnade roof and was lying along the edge of the tiles, thin, the way it lay in winter, and because the cook had stopped banging. In all the time she had been in the house the street door had never been barred at noon. It was barred at dark and unbarred at first light, and the door slaves took it in turns to sleep across it, and that was all. She went and looked at the bar after Phileros had gone. It was very old. The brackets had been painted once, red, and most of the paint had come off where hands had held them.

---

In the afternoon the mistress sent for a boy.

Nysa was in the storeroom by then. She had gone there because it was a thing she knew how to do, and because nobody else would. The steward kept the tallies on a board inside the door, oil and wine and grain and wool and lamp wicks, and she counted against the board, jar by jar, in the half dark. Twenty-two big jars of oil, sealed. Four broached. Of the four, one was nearly done. Wine was the steward’s business and she did not touch it. Grain was in the loft and she could not reach it. The wool was there in its sacks, and she counted the sacks.

She heard the mistress’s voice in the passage, not raised. It was the voice she used for linen. “Go to my father’s house. Ask him what he would have me do. Wait for an answer and bring it back in his words, not yours.”

“Yes, lady.”

“By the side door. Don’t run.”

The boy went past the storeroom door, walking very fast in the way that is nearly running. Nysa went on counting. The mistress was a tall quiet woman who said what she wanted once. She had asked, at the morning meal, for her wool basket to be brought to the small room off the garden, and then when it was brought she had not touched it, and later she had asked for it to be taken away. Nysa had carried it both ways. She had looked in it the second time. The spindle had not moved.

The boy came back before the light went. Nysa was in the passage with the lamp basket, and she saw him go in to the mistress with his cloak still on.

“He says stay in the house,” the boy said. “He says the house is the safest place in Rome. He says Caesar’s wife has nothing to fear from Caesar, and nobody else will dare.”

There was a pause.

“Were those his words?”

“Yes, lady. The last part he said twice.”

---

By dark the street was quiet in a way Nysa had never heard it.

The Sacred Way was never quiet. There were carts at night, because carts could not come in by day, and there were drunks coming up from the taverns behind the Forum, and dogs, and the Vestals’ watchman going round the wall with his stick, knocking. Tonight there were no carts. She lay on the pallet under the stairs and listened for them and there was nothing, only now and then a single pair of feet going past fast, and once a man’s voice, a long way off, calling a name that nobody answered.

Chreste was asleep. She slept the way she woke, all at once and completely, on her side with her knees up and her blanket over her head. Her breath went in and out under the wool.

Nysa got up and lit the lamp.

It was the small clay one that lived on the shelf over the pallets, with the chipped spout. They were not to light it. Phileros said that slaves who could not find their way to a pallet in the dark could sleep in the stable, where the horses would show them, and the steward said that oil was oil. She lit it from the night lamp in the passage, and brought it back with her hand round the flame, and set it on the floor between the two pallets where the light would not reach the door.

She did not know why she had done it. She sat on her pallet with the blanket round her shoulders and looked at it. It was a very small flame and it made the room smaller.

The quiet was good, she thought. Quiet meant nobody was coming. If the men who had gone had wanted to do anything to the house, they would have done it before they went, and they had not; they had gone past it in their litters with their curtains shut and their strongboxes, and not one of them had so much as looked at the door. And the man the house belonged to was a long way off in the north, with his army, and the mistress’s father said nobody would dare. So the house was the safest place in Rome. It had been said twice.

Someone came along the passage. She knew the step; it was Phileros, going round the doors the way he did every night, slower than usual. She put the empty grain measure over the lamp, quickly, the way she had seen the cook cover a pot, and the light went to a red line round the rim of the measure and then to nothing.

Phileros stopped outside the room under the stairs. She heard him breathe. He had been drinking, but not much.

Then he went on to the side door, and she heard him try the bar, and try it again, and go away.

She sat in the dark with her hands round her knees until her feet were cold. When she lifted the measure the lamp had gone out, and the room smelt of hot clay and burnt wick, and Chreste said something in her sleep about a goose and turned over.
