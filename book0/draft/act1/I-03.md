# The Kalends of February

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Forum, by the Janus · 1 February 49

---

On the Kalends of February Hordeonius set up his board at first light, as he had on every Kalends for thirty years, and waited for the stewards.

There should have been eleven. He knew it without looking at the tablet, the way a farmer knows how many cows ought to come in at evening. Eleven households paid him interest on the Kalends of February, and most of them paid through stewards, solid freedmen with satchels and their masters’ seals, who came to the board in the first two hours and stood in a little queue under the arch with their backs to the wind and talked about the price of firewood. Hordeonius liked the queue. He liked to see it from a distance when he came down from the Palatine side, eleven men in good cloaks waiting for him, before he had so much as unrolled a tablet.

This morning there was nobody under the arch. The wind came through it and moved the dead leaves about.

“They’ll be late,” Sosia said. He had brought the stools and the money box and was setting them out with more care than they needed. “It’s the cold.”

“It was colder last year.”

“Then it’s the other thing.”

Hordeonius sat down. The basilica behind him was half shuttered. The money-changers along the colonnade were there, most of them, but their boards were bare, or nearly; the old Greek at the corner had put out a single tray of foreign silver and was sitting in front of it with his arms folded, looking at the Forum the way a man looks at the sea from a harbour wall. The Forum was nearly empty. A few clients on the steps of the Rostra, waiting for a patron who would not come. Two boys with a ball. The public slaves sweeping round the Senate house, because they had been told to sweep and nobody had told them to stop.

“Fannius,” Hordeonius said.

Sosia found the line on the tablet. “Gone with Pompey. His people went with him. The house is shut.”

“Postumius. The younger.”

“Gone. Shut.”

“The Aemilius at the Carinae corner.”

“Gone, I think. There was a cart at the door on the ides of last month, with the plate in it.” Sosia stopped. “Do you want all of them? I can tell you which. I went round yesterday.”

Hordeonius did not want all of them. He looked down the tablet himself, at the eleven names and the sums against them, and saw the thing he had known since the consuls went out of the city on a wet morning a fortnight ago without even pausing to sacrifice, and had not let himself look at. He had lent for thirty years to men who stood for office. A man borrowed to be quaestor, and paid out of his province. He borrowed again to be praetor, at a better rate because he had been quaestor, and paid out of his next province. It was the oldest business in the Forum and the cleanest, because the security was the man’s own ambition and every man in Rome could see it. And this morning there was no ambition in the city to lend against. There would be no elections in the summer, or there would be elections nobody believed in. The men on his tablet had picked up their ambitions and their plate and taken them to Capua or wherever Pompey was, and their stewards had gone with them, and the queue under the arch was eleven men short.

“It will be a week,” he said. “Two. These things settle.”

Sosia looked at him and said nothing, which was a kindness Hordeonius noted and would not repay.

---

At the second hour a man came across the Forum from the direction of the Sacred Way and Hordeonius knew him before he could see his face, by the walk. It was a neat walk, short steps, as if the paving were a ledger and he did not wish to blot it.

Aulus Visellius stopped at the board and said good morning. He was a young man, thirty or so, son of a freedman, with a clerk’s shoulders. He had been on Hordeonius’s tablet for a year and a half, for a sum that would not have bought one of the Aemilius plates, and he lent it on again up three flights of stairs in the Subura, in smaller sums, to people Hordeonius would not have let near the board. He did it well. He had paid on every Kalends since the first.

He set his satchel down and counted the interest out on the wood in stacks of ten, squaring each one with the side of his thumb.

Hordeonius watched him do it. He could have told him that it was not necessary. Sosia would count it again afterwards in any case. But it was, he found, a pleasure to watch this morning, more of a pleasure than it ought to have been, and he let it go on.

“You are the first,” he said, when the last stack was down.

Visellius looked along the empty colonnade. “I thought I might be.”

“Your own people have paid you?”

“Most.” He closed the satchel. “The ones who rent rooms, yes. They’re more afraid of the landlord than of Caesar. Caesar is a long way off and the landlord is on the stairs.” He said it without smiling. “A dyer on the third floor has gone to Capua with Pompey’s levies. He owes me forty. His wife says he will pay me out of the spoils.”

“Whose spoils?”

“She didn’t say.”

Hordeonius laughed, which he did not often do at the board. Visellius looked pleased and a little surprised, and took his leave, and walked away across the empty paving with his short careful steps. Sosia swept the stacks into the box.

“Exact,” he said.

“Of course.”

---

The man who came at the third hour did not come to pay.

He came along the colonnade from the Argiletum end, a short, square, dark man in a very good cloak with a Spanish brooch at the shoulder, and two slaves behind him carrying nothing, which in Hordeonius’s experience was the mark of a man who did not need to carry anything because it would be sent. He stopped at the corner board and looked at the single tray of silver and said something that made the old Greek unfold his arms. Then he came on to Hordeonius.

“Lucius Hordeonius,” he said. His Latin was perfect, and a little too careful, the Latin of a man who had learned it to win lawsuits in. “I am Cornelius Balbus. I think we have never been introduced.”

“We have not.” Hordeonius stood up. Everyone in the Forum knew who Balbus was: a Spaniard from Gades who had been made a citizen by Pompey and had made himself the man who kept Caesar’s accounts in Rome, and who was richer, they said, than half the Senate, without having held any office at all. “You do me honour.”

“I do you an inconvenience. I am going along the boards this morning asking the same question, and I am sorry for it, because it is not a pleasant question and I would not like it put to me.” Balbus looked at the empty arch. “You lend to senators.”

“I lend to men who stand for office.”

“Some of whom have left the city.”

“Some.”

“Caesar,” Balbus said, “has no quarrel with any man’s creditor. I want you to understand that before I ask. He is not Sulla. I would put my house on it. There will be no lists. No man’s debts will be called in by the state, and no man’s goods will be sold, merely for having gone to Capua in a hurry.” He paused. “But he would like to know, for his own purposes, how deep the men who have gone are in. Which of them owe the boards of this Forum, and how much. It would help him to know who will need to come home.”

Hordeonius did not answer at once. He put his hand flat on the closed tablet with the eleven names on it.

“For his own purposes,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I have a tablet here,” Hordeonius said slowly, “with the names of men who owe me money. Some of them are in Capua this morning, and some of them are in this city, and one of them, I am glad to say, has paid. If I were to give the names of the ones in Capua to the man who is coming down the Flaminian Way with an army, and they came to hear of it, as they would, then the ones in this city would very reasonably ask themselves whether their own names were safe on my tablet, and they would take their business to the Greek at the corner. And he would deserve it.” He took his hand off the tablet. “I have lent for thirty years. I have lent to the friends of Marius and the friends of Sulla, and to Clodius and to Milo, sometimes in the same month. I am still here because no man in Rome has ever learned from me what another man owes. I would not like to begin with Caesar. I do not think Caesar would like it either. He may want a banker one day himself.”

Balbus looked at him for a moment. Then he smiled, which changed his face a great deal.

“That is almost word for word what the man at the corner said,” he said. “Though his was shorter, and in Greek.” He drew his cloak round him. “Thank you. I will tell Caesar that the boards of the Forum are honest and know nothing. He will be pleased. It is what he would have said himself.”

He went on along the colonnade, to the next board, and the two slaves followed him with their empty hands.

Sosia let out his breath. “You could have given him the Aemilius,” he said, low. “Nobody loves the Aemilius.”

“I could have given him all of them.” Hordeonius sat down. “And next month he would have asked me for the ones who stayed.”

---

Marcus Cicero’s freedman came at the fourth hour, with the interest and a letter of apology for its being by the hand of a freedman. His master was at Formiae, the letter said, and could not with propriety enter the city, and hoped that Hordeonius would accept the sum with his compliments and his regret that the times did not allow him to bring it himself.

“He has lictors,” Sosia said, reading over his shoulder. “With laurels on. He’s waiting for a triumph. He can’t come in.”

“Then he has done the right thing.” Hordeonius gave the freedman a receipt and a cup of the hot wine that Sosia kept in a jar wrapped in straw for the stewards, which this morning nobody else had drunk. “Two,” he said when the man had gone. “Two of eleven.”

“And the Spaniard.”

“The Spaniard did not pay.”

“He came, though,” said Sosia. “That’s three people at the board. The Greek’s had one, and that was the Spaniard too.”

By the sixth hour the light had gone grey and flat and the wind had got up. Nobody else came. Hordeonius sat in his cloak and looked at the arch, and at the leaves, and turned over in his mind a thing he had not said to Sosia, and would not: that he had sent Sosia round the boards three days ago to see who was buying paper, any paper, at any discount, on any name in the Senate, and that Sosia had come back with nothing, and that he had told Sosia it was because it was the end of the month. It had not been the end of the month. Nobody was buying. A tablet of good names was a tablet of wax this week, and he held eleven of them, and two had paid.

“Pack it up,” he said. “We will come again on the Nones. Some of them owe on the Nones.”

Sosia stacked the stools. Hordeonius wiped his two fingers on the cloth, though there was no ink on them; he had not written anything all day.
