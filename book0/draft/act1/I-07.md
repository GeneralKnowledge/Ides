# A Copy of a Letter

POV: Brutus · Tarsus, in Cilicia, the governor’s house · spring 49

---

The letter was a copy of a copy, and the clerk who had made the last copy had a habit of joining his letters at the top, so that every *t* leaned on the letter after it like a tired man on a friend.

Brutus read it at the table in the colonnade, with the light coming through the vine on the trellis and lying in coins on the page. It had come by sea from Brundisium to Rhodes and from Rhodes on a grain ship, and it had come with three letters from his mother and a parcel of books, and he had opened the books first and then his mother’s letters and then, because his mother’s third letter told him to, this. *I send you what everyone in Rome is reading,* she had written. *It is in the hand of Atticus’s people, so you may trust the text if not the spelling. Read it twice.*

He had read it twice. He was reading it a third time.

It was a letter from Caesar to two of his agents in Rome, Oppius and Balbus, written in the first days of March from somewhere on the road south, after the business at Corfinium. It was not long. It said that he was glad they approved of what he had done there. It said that he had resolved to show all the mercy he could, and to do his best to reconcile Pompey. It said: *Let us try whether by this means we can win back the goodwill of all and enjoy a lasting victory, since others have not been able by cruelty to escape hatred or to make their victories endure, except Lucius Sulla, whom I do not mean to imitate.* It said: *Let this be the new way of conquering, to strengthen ourselves by mercy and generosity.* And then it said, at the end, in the plainest sentence in the letter, that one of Pompey’s officers had fallen into his hands that week and he had sent the man back to Pompey at once, unharmed.

Brutus put the page down on the table and laid his hand flat on it, because the breeze was moving the vine and the page with it.

Across the colonnade, at the far end, Sestius was dictating to two secretaries at once, walking between them, in the voice of a man counting grain. He had been doing it since the second hour. The province did not stop because Italy had: there were the tax farmers’ accounts, and a dispute between two cities about a river, and a request from a king in the hills for something Brutus had not followed. Sestius dealt with all of it as if it mattered, and perhaps it did. He was a big, slow, honest man who had been a tribune once and had been beaten nearly to death in the Forum by Clodius’s gangs for it, and he walked a little lopsided still, and had never been heard to complain of it.

He came down the colonnade now, between one letter and the next, and stopped by the table.

“You have it too,” he said, looking at the page. “Everybody in Asia has it. The Rhodians are making copies for sale.”

“My mother sent it.”

“Yes.” Sestius sat down on the bench opposite, heavily. “She would.” He said it without any edge at all; it was simply a fact about Servilia that everyone knew, like her house or her eyes. “Well. What do you make of it?”

Brutus considered how to answer. The answer he wished to give was a long one, and he could feel it beginning in him, the way a period begins: that the letter was very fine; that it was finer than anything Pompey had written since the war began, and that this was itself a kind of argument against it, because a man who writes so well about mercy at the head of an army in Italy has already put himself in the position where mercy is his to give; that the Republic did not consist in the kindness of any one man, however great, but in the laws, which were not kind or unkind but simply the laws; that the consuls and the Senate were with Pompey, and that where the consuls and the Senate were, there was the state, whatever one thought of the man whose camp they were sitting in; and that therefore.

He had got as far as *therefore* when a slave came down the colonnade with a basin and a towel, for Sestius’s hands, which were inky. Sestius held them out and the slave poured, and the water ran off his fingers into the basin grey, and Brutus watched it, and lost the rest of the sentence.

“It’s very well written,” he said.

Sestius laughed. “It is.” He dried his hands. “So was Sulla, I’m told, at the beginning.”

---

In the afternoon Brutus walked by the river.

The Cydnus came down through Tarsus very cold and very fast, from the snows in the mountains behind the town, and people said it had nearly killed Alexander when he bathed in it, which was the sort of thing people in Tarsus said. He walked along the bank with his hands behind him and a slave a few paces behind with nothing to carry, because he did not know how to walk without one.

He was thinking about his father.

He did not remember him well. He had been eight. He remembered a big laugh in another room, and a smell of horses, and being lifted up onto a man’s shoulders to see something, he did not know what, a procession, perhaps. The rest he had been told. His father had held Cisalpine Gaul for Lepidus in the troubles after Sulla’s death, and had been besieged in Mutina by Pompey, who was then young and very much Sulla’s man, and had given himself up on terms; and had been allowed to leave, under escort, to a small town on the river; and on the next day Pompey had sent a man after him who killed him. It had been done quite quietly. There had been some talk afterwards of a misunderstanding. Pompey had written to the Senate one letter saying that his father had surrendered and another, a little later, saying that he had been put to death.

Brutus had never spoken to Pompey. He had passed him in the Forum and in the Senate house and at other men’s dinners, and had looked at him, and had not spoken, and Pompey had understood, and had never once tried to make him. It was the one thing Brutus had always liked about Pompey.

He stopped and looked at the river. It was very clear. He could see the stones on the bottom, green and brown, and a fish holding itself still against the current with small movements of its tail, staying in the same place by working very hard.

He was going to go to Pompey.

He had known it, he thought, since the first reading, and perhaps before, since the news of the crossing had come to Tarsus in the winter and Sestius had read it aloud at dinner and put the letter down and said nothing for a long while. He was going to go to Greece, where the consuls had taken the Senate, and offer himself to the man who had had his father killed, because that was where the state was. It was not difficult. That was what surprised him. He had expected, if it came to it, to feel something like a weight. He felt nothing of the kind. He felt clear and light, like the water.

He thought about the letter. *Let this be the new way of conquering.* He thought about the officer sent back to Pompey unharmed, in the plainest sentence. He had read that sentence three times and each time something had moved in his chest, under the breastbone, quite small, the way a child moves in its sleep. He did not examine it.

He turned and walked back along the bank towards the town, and the slave turned and followed him.

---

Sestius was at dinner when he came in, alone, eating lentils from a bowl with a spoon, like a farmer. He had taken off his toga and was in his tunic with his elbows on the table.

“I shall need a ship,” Brutus said, standing.

Sestius looked up at him and went on chewing. Then he swallowed. “Sit down,” he said. “Eat something. There’s a ship at Soli that goes to Rhodes on the Ides, and the master is a thief, but he’s a thief I know.” He pushed the bowl of bread across. “Thessalonica?”

“Wherever the consuls are.”

“Thessalonica, then. Or Dyrrachium. It’ll depend on Pompey.” Sestius took another spoonful. “Your mother will be very angry.”

“I shall write to her.”

“Yes. Write to her before you sail. Not after.” Sestius pointed his spoon at the bench. “Sit.”

Brutus sat. He took a piece of bread, out of courtesy, and held it. There was a lamp on the table between them, and moths at it, and outside the colonnade the sound of the river, which he had not noticed before he walked beside it and now could not stop hearing.

“I shan’t come,” Sestius said after a while. He said it to the lentils. “Somebody has to keep the province. The cities will be at each other’s throats in a month if there’s nobody here, and the tax farmers will strip them while they do it. Whoever wins will want Cilicia in one piece.” He looked up. “You understand.”

“Of course.”

“You’re doing the right thing, I expect. For you.” Sestius wiped the bowl with a crust. “For me it would be the wrong one. I’ve been beaten in the Forum for the Republic once. I find I didn’t care for it.”

Brutus said that he understood perfectly, and that he thought none the worse of him, and he meant it, and Sestius nodded and called for more lentils. Brutus ate the bread. He had not been hungry; he found that he was. Later, in his room, he wrote to his mother by the lamp, a careful letter, four times over, and in none of the four versions did he mention Caesar’s, though it was lying on the table beside him the whole time, with its tired *t*’s leaning on each other, and he knew it almost by heart.
