# A Farm at Aricia

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Forum, by the Janus · April 49

---

The money came out of the treasury on carts, and the carts came through the Forum at a walk, and everybody at the boards stood up to watch them go by as if they were a funeral.

Hordeonius stood up with the rest. There were six carts that morning, each with a mule and a driver and four soldiers walking beside it, and the chests on them were not covered; there was no reason to cover them. Everybody in Rome knew what was in them. They came out from behind the temple of Saturn, where the door was still lying on the top step on its face, and went along the Sacred Way past the Regia and out of the Forum towards the Flaminian gate, and from there, the soldiers said, to the camps and to the road north. Caesar had gone. He had stayed in the city six days, and on the seventh he had gone to Spain, and the carts went after him every morning in the same order at the same hour, like a procession for a god nobody was quite sure of.

“Bars,” said Sosia, beside him. “That cart. Look at the axle.”

“I see the axle.”

“That’s gold. Silver doesn’t sit an axle like that.” Sosia watched it go with his head on one side. “They say there were fifteen thousand bars. They say a clerk counted them twice and got different figures both times and was afraid to say.”

“Who says?”

“The water-carrier at Saturn’s. He was there.” Sosia sat down again. “Fifteen thousand. That’s every man in Rome out of debt, that is. Twice over.”

“It is not going to every man in Rome,” Hordeonius said. “It is going to Spain.”

The last cart went by, and the soldiers, and the Forum let its breath out and went back to its business, what there was of it. There was more than in February. The boards along the colonnade were mostly set out again, and some of them had trays; men had come back from their country houses, a few, when they heard that Caesar was not burning anybody’s house down; there was a queue at the praetor’s tribunal where Lepidus sat with his lictors in the mornings and heard cases, a long, fair, handsome man with a beautiful voice, whom nobody in the Forum would have lent a hundred sesterces to without a guarantor, and who was now, so far as anybody could tell, the government of Rome. But nobody was borrowing to stand for anything, because there was nothing to stand for. And nobody was buying anything that could not be carried, because a man who might have to leave in a hurry did not want a vineyard.

---

The man from Aricia came at the third hour, with a boy carrying a box of tablets, and Hordeonius knew before he sat down what he had come about.

His name was on the tablet: a knight, of a respectable family from the Alban hills, sixty years old, who had borrowed four years ago to help a son stand for quaestor. The son had been elected. He was with Pompey now, in Greece, and had taken the household silver with him, and had not paid the interest since the autumn. The father sat down on the client’s stool in front of the board and put his hands on his knees and looked at Hordeonius with great dignity and a sort of patient shame, the way an honest man looks when he is about to ask for something.

“I can’t pay you in coin,” he said. “I want to say that first. I haven’t got it, and if I had it I’d not be able to get it out of the man who has it, because the man who has it is my son, and my son is in Thessalonica, and I don’t know when the post goes. I’ve tried to sell. You can’t sell. I’ve had a man from Tibur and a man from Rome look at the farm, and they both stood in the middle of it and said what a beautiful farm, and went away again. Nobody will put coin down for land this spring. They all want to keep their coin where they can pick it up.”

“I know.”

“So I’ll give you the farm.”

Sosia looked up from his stylus.

“Not give.” The old man coloured. “Pledge. As security, against the whole of it, the principal and what’s owing, and you to have it if the boy doesn’t pay by the Kalends of next year. I’ve brought the plan.” He nodded to the boy, who opened the box and took out a tablet and laid it on the board, carefully, as if it might break. “That’s the boundary. The stones are marked. Forty iugera of vine and olive and a wood. A spring. Good house. My grandfather built the press.”

Hordeonius looked at the tablet.

It was a good plan, drawn by somebody who knew what he was doing, with the boundary stones marked in small circles and the name of each neighbour written along each side: on the east a Valerius, on the north the road, on the west a man called Aufidius, on the south a stream. The vines were drawn as little crosses in rows. The house was a square with a smaller square beside it for the press. There was a tree marked by the spring, with a note in a different hand, older, that said *the big oak*.

He found that he did not want to touch it.

It was a strange feeling, and he examined it while the old man waited. He had lent on land before, of course; every banker in the Forum had. But not for years, and not like this. He lent on men. On a man’s prospects, on the office he would hold and the province he would get and the friends who would stand behind him if he faltered, and all of that could be weighed; a man’s ambition could be weighed to the last sesterce by somebody who had been weighing ambitions for thirty years. He did not know how to weigh forty iugera and a press and a big oak at Aricia in a spring when nobody would buy a farm. A farm had been worth a certain sum in the autumn. In the autumn it would be worth something again, presumably. This spring it was worth what a man would give for it, and no man would give anything, so it was worth nothing, and also worth everything it had always been worth, because the vines did not know there was a war. He did not know how to write that on a tablet. There was no figure for it.

“What do you think it is worth?” he said.

“Two hundred thousand. Before.”

“And now?”

The old man opened his hands. “You tell me.”

Hordeonius did not tell him. He did not know. He sat with his hand on the edge of the board, not on the plan, and thought about it as carefully as he had ever thought about anything, and could not find the bottom of it. Then he did the thing he had always done when he could not find the bottom of a thing, which was to go back to the man.

The man had paid for four years. He had paid on the Kalends, by his steward, and the steward had never once been short, and when the son was elected the father had come to the board himself, in his best toga, to say so, and had brought a jar of his own oil. The man was sixty and honest and ashamed. And the man was here, in Rome, in front of the board, and his son was in Thessalonica.

“I will take the farm as security,” he said. “Against the whole. To the Kalends of next year, as you say. Sosia will draw it up.” He moved the plan towards Sosia with one finger, by the very edge. “And I will add nothing to the interest for the months unpaid. You may tell your son that. When the post goes.”

The old man looked at him for a moment as if he had not understood. Then he did, and his face did something, and he stood up and bowed, quite low, and said a great many things that Hordeonius let go past him. The boy put the box of tablets under his arm. They went away along the colonnade, the old man walking very upright, and the boy behind him looking back.

---

“You’ve never done that,” Sosia said, when they were out of earshot. He had the plan in front of him and was copying the boundary into the bond, neighbour by neighbour, Valerius and the road and Aufidius and the stream. “Taken land. Not since I’ve been here.”

“No.”

“Why now?”

Hordeonius considered the question. He could give a true answer, which was that the man was honest and the times were bad and the farm was the only thing on the man’s side of the board, and he would rather have the farm than nothing. Or he could give the answer he would have to live with on the Kalends, if the son did not pay, and he had to go out to Aricia in a cart and stand in the middle of forty iugera and a wood, in his good shoes, and tell an old man to leave his grandfather’s press.

“Because land does not run away, Sosia,” he said. “A senator runs to Greece. A farm stays where you left it. It is the one security in Italy this spring that will not take ship.”

Sosia wrote the stream in, and the big oak. “That’s what I’d have said,” he said. “If I’d been you.”

“Then why did you ask?”

“To see if you’d say it.”

Hordeonius did not answer. He looked out across the Forum, where Lepidus’s lictors were standing in a row at the foot of the tribunal in the sun, and the queue of litigants went back nearly to the Rostra, and a man was sweeping up the dung the mules had left on the Sacred Way. He found that he had his hand out flat on the board where the plan had been, as if it were still there. He took it away and wiped his fingers on the cloth, and told Sosia to put the bond with the ones that waited on the Kalends, and to put the plan somewhere else, by itself.
