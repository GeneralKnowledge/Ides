# Home Water

POV: Laberius · Picenum: Cingulum and the road to Asculum · February 49

---

The legion came down out of the mountains into Picenum with snow on its cloaks and nothing in its bread bags, and at Cingulum the gates were open.

Laberius saw them from a long way off, because the road came round the shoulder of a hill and there was the town on its ridge across the valley, with the smoke going straight up from it in the cold, and the gate in the near wall standing wide. There were people on the wall. There were people on the road outside the gate, too, a crowd of them, and some of them had branches. He had marched into a good many towns in Gaul and he had never seen that. In Gaul the gates were shut and you sat down outside them until somebody inside decided otherwise.

“They’re pleased to see us,” said Gaius, on his left.

“They’re pleased to see somebody.”

“Same thing, if it’s us.” Gaius had his scarf up over his nose and his voice came out of it thick. He had been talking since the Alps. He had talked over the pass, in snow to the knee, about a girl at Ravenna who had promised him things; he had talked down the other side about the price of boots; he had talked all the way along the coast road, where the wind came in off the grey sea and went through mail as if it were a net. Laberius did not mind. It meant he did not have to. “I’ll tell you what it is. It’s the bread. They’ve heard we’ve got no bread and they’re afraid we’ll come and take theirs. So they come out and give us some first and we say thank you.”

“Then it’s welcome.”

“Then it’s sense.” Gaius pulled the scarf down to spit. “Welcome’s what you get when they don’t need to.”

The column closed up and went down into the valley, and up the other side, and in at the gate.

It was bread. There were tables in the little square inside the gate, and loaves on them, and women behind the tables in dark wool with their heads covered, and the town’s magistrates standing to one side in their bordered togas looking as if they had not slept. A man with a writing tablet was counting the loaves out by the century and making a mark for each one. Laberius took the century’s share and gave it to Vibius, who divided it, because Vibius could divide a loaf into eighty-three pieces in his head and get every one the same, and the men stood in the square and ate it with the snow melting off their shoulders.

One of the women behind the table said something to the woman next to her.

Laberius heard it with the back of his head. It was a word for a thing a child does wrong, a small word, not rude, that his mother had used to him when he was five, and he had not heard it said since he went for a soldier. He turned round. The woman was not looking at him. She was folding up the cloth the loaves had been on, talking to her neighbour about somebody’s son, and she said the word again, and laughed.

He stood there with the bread in his mouth.

“What?” said Gaius.

“Nothing.”

“You’ve gone a colour.”

“It’s the cold.”

---

The town had been built by Labienus.

Everybody knew it. The men knew it before they had been there an hour, because the townspeople told them, the way people tell soldiers about the one rich man they have. Labienus had been born in the hills near here. He had come back with Gaulish money and paid for the walls, and the forum, and the paving in the forum, and the colonnade along one side of it, and there was an inscription over the colonnade with his name on it in letters as long as a man’s hand, so that even the men who could not read could see where the name was.

Labienus had been Caesar’s right hand in Gaul for ten years. Laberius had seen him a hundred times, on a horse, on a ridge, in front of the legion with his helmet off, a hard dark man with a voice like a door. Once at Alesia he had ridden along the line so close that Laberius could have put out his hand and touched his boot, and had shouted at the men in the front rank to stop looking at the Gauls and look at him, and they had. That was the kind of thing a man remembered.

Now he had gone to Pompey. He had gone, they said, a few days after the crossing, by night, with his own horses and his own slaves, and Caesar had sent his baggage after him.

His house was at the top of the town, by the forum. Laberius went and looked at it in the afternoon, when the century had been put into a granary for the night and there was nothing to do until the trumpets. It was a big house for a hill town, with a plain front and a good door. The door was shut. The shutters were shut. There was a dog on the step, an old yellow dog with a grey muzzle, lying with its chin on its paws, and when Laberius stopped in front of the house it lifted its head and looked at him and then put it down again.

He did not know what he had come to see. He stood there for a while with his thumbs in his belt. Two of the townsmen were sitting on the steps of the colonnade across the way, under the long letters, and one of them called out to him.

“He’s not in.”

“I can see.”

“Gone to the other side.” The man said it with a kind of pride, as if Labienus had gone to Rome to be consul. “Our Titus. He’ll be back.”

“Which side will he be on when he gets back?”

The man thought about that. “Ours,” he said, and the other one laughed.

---

They marched for Asculum two days later.

Asculum was held for Pompey, men said, by Lentulus Spinther with ten cohorts, and there would be a fight for it, and then on the morning of the march a rider came back down the road to say that Lentulus had heard the legion was coming and had gone out of the other gate in the night, and the cohorts were coming apart behind him like a wet loaf. So it was only a march. The road went south along the valleys, crossing one river and then the next, through country that got higher and colder and closer together as it went, with the snow lying in the gullies and the oak woods bare.

Laberius knew the country before he knew he knew it.

It was the shape of the hills. Then it was the way the farms sat up on the ridges with their backs to the north wind, and the walls round the fields made of river stones without mortar, and the women at the fountains with the water jars on their heads, walking straight-backed with their hands at their sides, not touching the jars, the way his mother had walked. He watched one of them come down a path to the road to see the column go by. She stood at the edge of the road with the jar on her head and her arms folded and looked at the soldiers without any expression at all. The jar did not move. He could have told the men around him that she had been carrying it since she was eight, and that her neck was as strong as a mule’s, and that if one of them said anything to her she would not need to put the jar down to answer. He did not tell them.

In the late afternoon the road came down to a river and went along beside it, and on the far side, on the right, there was a valley going up into the hills.

It was a narrow valley with a stream in it and a mill at the bottom, and above the mill a track going up between walls, and up on the slope where the track bent there was a walnut, very big, bare now, the branches black against the snow on the hill behind. There was smoke from somewhere above the walnut that he could not see.

Laberius walked past it.

He watched it for as long as the road let him. It was about a quarter of an hour, at the pace the column was going. He watched the mill and the track and the walnut and the smoke, and then the road went round a spur and the valley went behind it and there was only the river.

He had not been up that track in fifteen years. He did not know who was at the top of it now. He thought about falling out and asking the tribune for a night, which a centurion could do, in country like this, with no enemy in front of the column for thirty miles. And he would go up past the mill and the walnut in the dark and knock, and somebody would come to the door with a lamp, and then he would have to say something. He did not know what he would say. He did not know whose face would be behind the lamp. And in the morning he would have to come down again and catch the column up, and everyone would know where he had been, and Gaius would talk about it from here to wherever they were going.

He kept walking.

“Was that it?” Gaius said, a mile further on.

“Was what what?”

“You’ve been looking at that hill since the river like a dog at a kitchen door.” Gaius had his scarf down. He was looking at Laberius sideways, not unkindly. “Asculum’s not far now. Your lot are up here somewhere. You said.”

“I said near Asculum.”

“That’s near Asculum.”

“Everything’s near Asculum, up here.” Laberius shifted his shield on his back. The strap had worn a place on his shoulder over the Alps, and the place had started to bleed again, and he could feel the tunic sticking to it. “There’s a mule up there that knew me. It’s dead.”

Gaius waited to see if there was any more. There was not. He put his scarf back up.

That night they slept in the fields outside Asculum, which had opened its gates like Cingulum and was giving out bread like Cingulum, and the magistrates came out to the camp to see the general with their faces all the same. Laberius lay under his cloak by the fire with his boots off and his feet to the warmth. He heard the cohorts that Lentulus had left behind come in in the dark, in twos and threes, to give themselves up; he heard them talking to the picket in the voices of the hill towns, asking where they should go. Some of them, he thought, would be from up the valley.

He did not go and look.
