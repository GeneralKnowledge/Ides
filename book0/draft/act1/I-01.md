# The Carriage

POV: Antony · Ariminum, the forum · January 49, the morning after the crossing

---

The carriage had no springs and the driver had no conversation, and by the time it came down off the last hill into Ariminum Antony had stopped feeling his backside somewhere south of the Rubicon.

He had not seen the river. It had been dark, and raining, and the driver had said, “That’s it,” over his shoulder in the voice of a man pointing out a ditch, and they had rattled over a wooden bridge and he had felt nothing at all, except that the plank under the left wheel was loose. Quintus Cassius had been asleep against the side with his mouth open. Antony had thought about waking him so that one of them would remember it, and had not, and now it was too late.

The town was full of soldiers. They were in the colonnades and the doorways and sitting on the edge of the fountain with their boots off, and they had the look men have on the first morning in a town they have walked into without a fight: pleased, and a little suspicious of being pleased. A cookshop on the corner was doing more trade than it had ever done in its life. The smell of frying came through the leather curtain of the carriage and Antony’s stomach woke up before the rest of him.

“Where do you want to be put down?” the driver said.

“Wherever Caesar is.”

“Forum, then.” The man spat sideways into the road. “It’ll be the same price.”

It was the price that had been arranged at the stable behind the Quirinal two nights before, when Antony and Cassius had come in at the back door with their hoods up and asked for a closed carriage and a man who did not talk. The man had not talked. He had looked at their tunics, which were the brown wool of the house slaves of a decent family, bought that afternoon from a dealer who had not asked why two men wanted them, and at their hands, which were not slaves’ hands, and he had named a figure. Cassius had paid it. Antony did not carry money. He owed too much of it to too many people to like the feel of it on his belt.

The tunic was too short for him. Whoever it had been made for had been a smaller man, and it stopped well above his knees, and the sleeves cut him under the arms. He had been cold in it the whole way. He had made a joke about it at the start, at the stable, putting it on: that he had always wanted to see how the other half lived and had not expected the other half to be so badly tailored. Cassius had not laughed. Cassius did not like any of this. He was the kind of man who would have preferred to be driven out of Rome in a toga, and to be killed in it.

The carriage stopped. Antony pushed the curtain back and got down into the forum of Ariminum, and something happened that he felt before he understood.

There were soldiers all round the square, more than he had seen in the streets, cohorts standing in loose order as if they had been told to wait for something, and they had seen him. He knew it on his skin, the way he always knew when a room had turned towards him, before he had found a single face. The noise in the square changed. It did not stop. It went down, as a crowd’s noise goes down when it is waiting to find out what it thinks.

He looked down at himself. Brown wool to the thigh. Bare legs, mud to the calf. A slave’s cheap sandals, the strap on the left already gone at the toe.

Then Curio was there, coming across the square with his quick step and his hand already out, and behind Curio, not hurrying, a thin man in a general’s red cloak with his head bare in the drizzle.

“Marcus,” Caesar said. He took Antony by both arms and looked at him, at the tunic and the legs and the sandal, with great attention and no expression at all. “And Quintus. You came.”

“We were asked to leave,” Cassius said, getting down stiffly behind. “We thought it would be a discourtesy to stay.”

“Did they touch you?”

“They threatened to.”

“That will do.” Caesar let go of Antony’s arms. He looked past them both, at the waiting cohorts, and then back at Antony, and something in his face was pleased. “No. Don’t go and change. Stay as you are. Come up with me.”

There was a tribunal of a kind at the top of the square, a platform that the town used for its magistrates, and Caesar went up the three steps and stood on it and did not call for silence. He did not need to. Antony went up after him because he had been told to and stood where Caesar put him, a little to the front, with Cassius on the other side. The rain came on harder for a moment and then thought better of it.

Caesar spoke to them for a while about the law. He had a clear, carrying voice that did not seem to be raised. He told them that the Senate had passed its last decree, the one it kept for traitors and for Catiline, and had passed it against the men who had won Gaul for Rome. He told them that the tribunes of the people, whose persons were sacred, whom no Roman might lay a hand on since the people first walked out to the Sacred Mount, had been threatened in the Senate house and driven from the city. And then he did not say anything more about that. He turned, and held out his hand, palm up, towards Antony, the way a man presents a horse he is selling, and he let them look.

Antony felt them look.

He had stood in front of soldiers before. He had led cavalry in Syria and Egypt and he had stood with Caesar at Alesia, and he knew what men in ranks sounded like when they liked an officer and when they did not. This was not that. This was a sound he had never heard from below, a low sound that came from everywhere at once and went up the back of his neck, and it was not for him. It was for the tunic. It was for the bare legs and the broken sandal and the mud, and somewhere in the third rank of the cohort nearest the steps a big grey centurion with a face like a boot had his teeth bared and his hand on his sword and was looking at Antony’s knees as if he would like to kill whoever had made a tribune of the people show them.

It was a beat later, standing there with Caesar’s open hand still beside him, that Antony understood he was the reason. He had not been the reason for anything before.

The cohorts were shouting now. He did not catch the words. They were the words men always shout, and they had his name in them, and Cassius’s, and the general’s. Caesar let it go on long enough and then lifted his hand an inch and it stopped, all of it, the way a dog stops.

“Now go and eat,” Caesar said to him quietly, not turning his head. “Both of you. Curio will find you clothes.” He was already looking past them, at a dispatch rider who had come in at the far side of the square on a horse that was finished. “Tomorrow, wear these again.”

Antony went down the three steps. Soldiers stood back to let him through, and some of them touched him as he passed, on the arm or the back, quickly, the way men touch an altar going by. One of them gave him a piece of bread from inside his tunic. Antony ate it walking. It was warm from the man’s body and had a burnt crust, and it was the best thing he had eaten since Rome.

At the cookshop on the corner he stopped and asked the woman for whatever was in the pan, and two of it, and sat down on the step outside to wait with his knees up and the tunic riding to the top of his thighs. A boy came and looked at his sandal.

“You want that sewn?”

“How much?”

The boy told him. Antony looked round for Cassius, who had the money, and Cassius was still on the far side of the square with Curio, being talked at.

“I’ll pay you tomorrow,” Antony said.

The boy considered him. Then he sat down on the step and took the sandal off Antony’s foot and began on the strap with an awl he had in his belt, and did not ask again.
