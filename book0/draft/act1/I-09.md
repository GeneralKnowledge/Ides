# The Temple of Saturn

POV: Antony · Rome, the Forum, the temple of Saturn · April 49

---

The keys had gone with the consuls, and so Antony went for smiths.

He went himself, because Caesar had looked round for somebody to send and Antony had been the nearest, and because he was tired of standing at the bottom of the temple steps being looked at by a crowd that did not know what it thought. He took four men from the escort and went down the Vicus Tuscus at a jog, in his toga, holding the folds up out of the mud with one hand like a woman crossing a street, and the people in the doorways watched him go by and did not say anything. There was a forge at the bottom of the street, by the cattle market, that he had used when he was a boy to have his first horse shod. It was still there. The smith was not the same smith, but he had the same leather apron, or one like it, and the same burnt forearms, and he looked at Antony’s toga with its purple border and at the four soldiers behind him and took his apron off.

“What is it?”

“A door,” Antony said. “Bring the heavy hammers. And a bar. And your boy.”

“What door?”

“Saturn’s.”

The smith stood with the apron in his hands. Antony watched him think about it: about the temple, and the treasury in the vault under it, and the god, and the men with swords in the doorway of his forge; and then about whatever a smith thinks about when he has a wife and a forge and a street full of neighbours. It took him about as long as it would take to drink a cup of wine.

“Who’s paying?” the smith said.

Antony laughed. He could not help it; it came up out of him before he knew it was there, and the soldiers laughed too, because he had. “The treasury,” he said. “Who else?”

---

When they came back up into the Forum with the smiths behind them carrying their hammers on their shoulders, the crowd had got bigger, and it had gone quiet.

It was the quiet that Antony noticed. He always noticed a crowd; he felt it on his skin, the way he felt weather. This one had been noisy when he left, the noise a crowd makes when there is going to be something to see, people calling to each other, boys up on the bases of statues. Now it was not noisy. It stood in the Forum from the Rostra to the steps of the temple of Castor, a great many people, more than he would have thought were left in Rome, and it was standing quite still and looking at the temple steps, and the only noise was a sort of breathing.

He pushed through to the front with the smiths and saw why.

There was a man on the temple steps. He was standing in front of the treasury door, at the top, by himself, a young man in a toga with his arms spread, one hand on each side of the doorframe, the way a man stands across a door to keep his children in. Antony knew him. Lucius Metellus. A tribune of the people, of this year’s college, Antony’s own colleague, a pleasant, serious young man with a long neck, who had sat two benches away from him at the games last summer and talked about horses. He was very white. He was saying something, and his voice was carrying, because it was the only voice in the Forum.

“... laws,” he was saying. “Under the law, no magistrate may lay hands on the person of a tribune of the people, or remove him from where he stands, or hinder him in the exercise of his office. And this money is the people’s money and it is not to be touched except by a law of the people, and there is no such law.”

Caesar was at the bottom of the steps. He was not in armour; he was in a cloak, bareheaded, as usual. He was standing with his arms folded, looking up at Metellus, and listening, with the expression he had when a junior officer was explaining why a bridge had not been built.

“There is a time for laws,” he said, when Metellus stopped, “and a time for arms. This is not the first.”

“Then you admit it.”

“I admit nothing. I’m telling you what time it is.” Caesar unfolded his arms. “If you don’t like what I’m doing, you may go away and say so. In the Forum, to anyone. I’m not stopping anyone saying anything. But a war has no use for speeches, and I need what’s in there to pay for this one, and you are standing in front of it.”

Metellus did not move. He looked down at Caesar and his long throat moved, but he stayed where he was, with a hand on each side of the door.

Antony stood at the front of the crowd with the smiths at his back and watched. He found that he was enjoying it. He did not know at first what he was enjoying, and then he did. It was the noise. Or not the noise, the absence of it: a whole Forum holding their breath at once, so that a man could hear a pigeon land on the temple roof. He had never heard a silence that big. He had heard crowds roar, for a gladiator, for a speaker, for himself in Ariminum in a slave’s tunic; but he had never heard a whole city shut its mouth at once, and he could feel it go up his arms like the cold off a river.

Caesar took a step up. Then another. He did not hurry. He stopped two steps below Metellus, so that their faces were nearly level, and he said something too quiet for the crowd to hear, but Antony was close and he heard it.

“Young man,” Caesar said. “I’ll kill you if I have to. You should know that this is harder for me to say than it would be to do.”

Metellus looked at him for a long moment. Then he took his hands down from the doorframe, one and then the other, and came down the steps past Caesar with his toga clutched at his chest, not looking at anybody, and the crowd made a lane for him, and he went away across the Forum in the silence until Antony lost him behind the Rostra.

Antony found that he had laughed again. Out loud, once, short. One or two people near him turned their heads.

He knew what was funny. He had known it at once, at the moment Metellus took his hands down. Three months ago he had been driven out of the Senate house for being a tribune, or said he had, and had come up the Flaminian Way in a hired carriage in a slave’s tunic so that an army could look at his legs and be angry on behalf of the sacred person of a tribune of the people. And here was a tribune of the people on the steps of Saturn, and here was the army’s general telling him to his face that he would kill him; and here was Antony at the bottom of the steps with four smiths and a crowbar, waiting to be told to break the door. It was the funniest thing he had seen all year. He could not think of anyone he could tell it to.

“Marcus,” Caesar said, not turning his head. “The door.”

---

It took the smiths most of an hour. The treasury door was oak, with iron bands, and the lock was old and very good, and in the end they did not break the lock but took the door off its hinges, the smith working the bar in behind the upper pin while his boy hit it, and the crowd stood and listened to the hammer, every stroke, as if it were counting something. When the door came away it fell outwards onto the top step with a noise like a building going down, and a cloud of dust went up out of the dark behind it, and the smith stood back and wiped his face and looked at Antony.

Antony went in with the quaestor’s clerks and two lamps.

He had never been in the treasury. Not many men had. It went down, under the temple, by a stair that turned on itself, and at the bottom there were rooms, low and cold and smelling of stone and old wax, with shelves along the walls; and on the shelves there were boxes, and on the floor there were chests, and at the far end, behind a second door, which the clerks unlocked with a key they did have, there was the gold.

It was in bars. They were stacked like bricks on a wooden pallet, on the floor, the height of a man’s knee and the length of a man lying down. The lamplight went into them and came back out yellow. There was a tablet hung on a nail above the pallet, and one of the clerks held a lamp up to it so that Antony could read it, and it said that this was the reserve laid up by the fathers against a war with the Gauls, not to be touched for any other purpose.

“He says that’s done,” the clerk said. He was an elderly freedman with a cough, and he had been very frightened coming down the stairs and was not frightened now; he was looking at the gold the way a man looks at a field he has ploughed for forty years. “Caesar. He says he’s conquered the Gauls, so it’s his. It was there for the Gauls, he says, and the Gauls are done.”

Antony looked at the bars. He was working out, without meaning to, what they came to. He was not good at sums on paper; but he was good at this one, the way a man who has been hungry is good at loaves. So many bars, so many pounds to a bar, so many denarii to the pound, so many denarii to a legionary’s year. It came to a great many legions for a great many years. It came, he thought, to everything he owed every man in Rome, and a house worth living in, and the house after that, and still left enough to pay the legions. He did the sum twice. Then he smiled at the clerk, who was waiting, and the clerk smiled back uncertainly.

“Well,” Antony said. “That’s the Gauls paid for. Get it up the stairs.”

---

That evening, in the house where Caesar was staying, which was not his own, Caesar told him what he was to have.

He told him walking, between one dispatch and the next, as he told everyone everything. He was going to Spain in two days, to deal with Pompey’s legions there, because a man could not go east after Pompey with seven legions at his back in Spain. Lepidus was praetor, and would have the city. Curio would go to Sicily, and then to Africa, if Africa could be got. And Antony would have Italy, and the troops that were in it, as tribune, with the powers of a propraetor, and would hold it for him until he came back.

“Don’t break anything,” Caesar said, at the door.

“I broke a door today.”

“I mean anything I need.” Caesar looked at him, and something in his face was amused, as it had been at Ariminum. “You laughed on the steps.”

“Did I?”

“Twice.” Caesar went out.

Antony stood in the middle of a room that was not his, in a house that was not his, with Italy, and found that the first thing he wanted was a drink, and the second was somebody to tell, and that the somebody he wanted to tell was not in the house and was not his wife. He sent for the drink.
