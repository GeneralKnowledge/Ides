# Caesar at Formiae

POV: Cicero · Formiae, his house by the sea · 28 March 49

---

Caesar came at the fourth hour, with a great many more people than he needed, and was charming to all of them, and to the lictors, and to the steward, and to the dog.

Cicero watched it from the top of the steps. He had gone out to meet him there because it was the proper place, the top step of his own house, and because from there a man could see the whole of the approach, the road coming along the shore from Minturnae and turning up between the cypresses to the gate, and could see what was coming before it saw him. He saw it. There were horsemen first, Gauls, very tall, on small shaggy horses, who reined in at the gate and sat looking at the house as if they were valuing it. Then a litter, and two carriages, and a crowd of men on foot and on mules who did not look like soldiers and did not look like anything else; and among them, on a horse, in a plain cloak, the man himself, getting down at the gate before anybody could help him and coming up the drive on foot, with his head bare, looking at the cypresses.

At the foot of the steps he stopped and looked up.

“Marcus,” he said. “You’ve had the trees cut back. It’s better. You can see the sea.”

It was the first thing he said, and Cicero had prepared for a great many first things, and not for that. He came down two steps, which he had not meant to do. “Last autumn. The gardener said they were taking the light.”

“He was right.” Caesar came up the steps and took both his hands. His hands were cold from the reins and very dry. He looked at Cicero with the whole of his attention, which was a thing Cicero had seen him do to other people across the Senate house and had never quite had done to himself at this distance. “You look well. Better than I’d heard. Atticus told me you’d been ill.”

“I’ve been at the sea.”

“So I hear.” The eyes went past him for an instant, to the vestibule, where the six lictors stood against the wall in a row with their rods and their laurels, fresh from the kitchen garden that week. Caesar looked at the laurels. Then he looked back at Cicero and smiled, very slightly, and said nothing about them at all, which was worse than anything he could have said, and also, Cicero thought, rather well done.

---

They talked in the library, alone, for the best part of two hours.

Caesar would not sit at first. He walked, as Cicero walked when he was working, up to the window and back, and he looked at the books on the shelves as he passed them and once took one down and read the label and put it back. He talked about the road. He talked about Brundisium: that Pompey had got away by sea with the consuls and most of his army ten days before, and that he, Caesar, had had no ships to stop him, and that he regretted it more than he could say, because it meant the war would go on, and he wanted it ended. He said that he wanted peace. He said it several times, in different ways, and each time it was a different sentence, and each sentence was good. Cicero, listening, found that he was admiring the sentences in the way he would have admired a pleader’s in a difficult case: the variety, the restraint, the way each one came back to the same point from a new side without any sign of strain. He wondered whether Caesar knew he was doing it. He decided that Caesar knew exactly.

“And so,” Caesar said, stopping at the window, “I’m calling the Senate. Such of it as is in Italy. On the Kalends, in Rome. I want men there who can speak for peace and be listened to. I want you there.”

There it was.

Cicero had known it was coming since the letter from Balbus in February, and since the letters from Matius, and since Caesar’s own two lines from the road last week, asking to see him. He had walked up and down the terrace with it for a fortnight. He had said what he would say to Atticus, in writing, and Atticus had said in writing that he should say it. He said it.

“I’ll come,” he said, “if I may speak as I think.”

“Of course.”

“Then I should say,” Cicero went on, and he could hear his own voice and was pleased by it, by how level it was, “that the Senate does not approve of your taking an army into Spain, or of your sending armies into Greece. And I should say a good deal about Pompey. In his favour. And regret.”

Caesar turned round from the window.

“I don’t want that said,” he said.

“No. I thought you wouldn’t.” Cicero spread his hands. “And that is why I would rather not come. Because either I should say it, and you would not like it, or I should not say it, and I should not like myself. I am too old to begin not liking myself, Caesar. I have only just got used to it the other way.”

He had prepared that last sentence the night before, and was afraid for a moment that it would sound prepared. Caesar laughed. It was a real laugh; he had a short quick laugh that seemed to surprise him. Then he stopped laughing and sat down for the first time, on the couch by the window, and put his hands on his knees and looked at the floor between his feet.

“If you don’t come,” he said, “others won’t. They’ll say that you have judged me. That you’ve looked at what I’m doing and condemned it, and they’ll stay in their country houses and wait, as you’re waiting. You know that.”

“Their position is not mine.”

“Isn’t it?”

Cicero did not answer that, because there was no good answer, and he had no intention of giving a bad one.

They went round it again. Caesar asked him to think about it. Cicero said he had thought about it. Caesar said that he would not ask him to say anything he did not believe; Cicero said that that was exactly the difficulty, since he did not believe anything Caesar would want to hear said. Caesar was patient. He was courteous. He was, Cicero thought, as courteous as any man he had ever spoken to, and he was courteous in the way a good swordsman is relaxed, from the shoulder, without any waste. And at the end of the second hour, when there was nothing more to be said on either side, Caesar stood up and took his cloak from the back of the couch and said, quite pleasantly, putting it on:

“Well. If I can’t have your advice, I’ll have to take advice where I can get it. And go as far as that advice takes me.”

He fastened the brooch at his shoulder. He looked at Cicero once more with that whole attention, and Cicero met it, and neither of them said what the sentence meant, and Caesar went out.

---

The people he had brought with him had spread through the house while they talked. Cicero found them in the atrium and the colonnade and the garden, eating his figs. Some of them he knew. Most of them he knew of. There was a man who had been prosecuted for extortion and acquitted by a jury Cicero could have named, man by man, with their prices. There was a young man of good family who had run through two fortunes and was now, it appeared, a military tribune. There was a man he was nearly sure had been in Catiline’s house the night of the meeting at Laeca’s, though fourteen years made a difference to a face. They were all very civil to him. They called him *imperator*, because of the laurels. One of them asked him, with real interest, how much the house had cost.

He stood at the top of the steps and saw them off, as he had seen them in. The Gauls went first again, and then the litter, and then Caesar on his horse, who turned at the gate and lifted his hand, and then the crowd on foot and on mules, looking back at the house as they went down between the cypresses. When they had gone the drive was full of dung and the gardener was standing at the side of it looking at the box hedge, which had been trodden.

Cicero went in. He walked up and down the library for some time, eleven paces each way, as he did. He was very pleased with himself. He could not remember when he had last been so pleased with himself. He had said what he meant to the most powerful man in the world, in his own house, in good Latin, and the man had laughed, and had gone away without what he had come for. He had not been rude. He had not been servile. He had found the narrow place between and walked along it from one end to the other.

He thought that he would write to Atticus at once, while he had it all fresh, every sentence of it, both sides.

Then he thought about Caesar’s last sentence. *Go as far as that advice takes me.* He thought about the men eating his figs, who would be the ones giving the advice. He stopped walking.

After a while he called the Bithynian boy and dictated the letter to Atticus anyway, all of it, both sides, every sentence, and it went very well; and at the end, where he would usually have put the news of the household, he had the boy write that he had been firm, and that he thought Caesar had not liked him for it, and that he had liked himself for it, which had not happened in some time.
