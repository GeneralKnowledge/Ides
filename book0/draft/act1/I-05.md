# The Laurels

POV: Cicero · Formiae, his house by the sea · February 49

---

The laurels on the fasces had gone brown at the edges, and the senior lictor wished to know whether they should be renewed.

He asked it in the vestibule, at the second hour, when Cicero came through on his way to walk on the terrace before the letters. The lictors were there every morning. There were six of them, and they stood in the vestibule in a row against the painted wall, with the rods and the axes on their shoulders and the laurel twined round the rods, because a man who had been hailed *imperator* by his army in Cilicia and had not yet been granted his triumph kept his lictors and his laurels until he was, or until he gave up hoping. He could not enter the city with them. He could not enter the city without giving them up. So they stood in the vestibule at Formiae, six large patient men from the Subura in clean tunics, and every morning he walked past them and they straightened, and every morning he wished they would not.

“The leaves have dried, sir,” the senior lictor said. He was a grey heavy man who had carried rods for three governors and spoke of all of them with the same respect. “With the wind off the sea. They crack. If you want them fresh there’s a laurel by the wall of the kitchen garden, I’ve had a look. Or if you’d rather leave them, sir, we’ll leave them.”

Cicero looked at the rods. The leaves had certainly dried. They had curled at the tips, and one or two had fallen from the bundle nearest the door and lay on the mosaic, a dark green going grey, like the leaves of a bay tree on a kitchen shelf.

“What would you advise?”

The lictor considered the question with great seriousness. “People come to the door, sir,” he said. “They see the rods. If the leaves are brown they think it’s been a long time.”

“It has been a long time.”

“Yes, sir.”

There was a pause, in which Cicero heard, as he had heard every morning for a month, the sea against the rocks below the terrace and the gulls, and somewhere in the house his son’s voice raised in the reading room, protesting.

“Renew them,” he said. “From the kitchen garden. And pick the leaves yourself, if you would. Not the garden boy. He’ll strip the tree.”

---

On the terrace he walked up and down with his hands behind him, eleven paces each way, and composed in his head the letter he would not write to Pompey.

It began well. It always began well. *If I have been slow to come to you, Gnaeus, it is not because I am slow to love you, for you know that no man in Italy has been more constant to your dignity, and if I have stayed by the sea it is because you yourself asked me to watch this coast.* That was true; Pompey had asked him, in a note of four lines, to have an eye to the coast from Formiae and to the levies at Capua, and he had had an eye to it. He had watched the sea every day. Nothing had come up it. The levies at Capua were boys, and few, and badly armed, and the man in command there had looked at him, when he came, as if he were a tax. *And if I am not with you now, it is because I do not know where you are.*

That was also true. Pompey had been at Capua, and then at Luceria, and was now somewhere in Apulia, moving south, towards the sea, and every letter from him or about him came a day or two later than the place it described, so that it was like trying to answer a man who was walking away from you down a corridor. Cicero had written to ask what the plan was. He had been told there was a plan. He had asked for it, and been told that it would be clear in time.

It was clear. It was clear to every fisherman at Formiae. Pompey was making for Brundisium and for ships, and he was going to cross to Greece and leave Italy to Caesar, as Sulla had once come home from Greece to take it back. Cicero walked up and down and admired the strategy as a strategy, because it was the strategy of a great commander who had done this kind of thing before, in the East, on a scale no other living Roman could imagine; and he would not let himself finish the sentence, because the end of the sentence was that Rome and his house on the Palatine and the Senate house where he had spoken against Catiline were to be left behind, like the treasury, with the door open.

He stopped at the end of the terrace and looked at the sea.

“Father.” Marcus had come out of the house behind him, with a book under his arm and ink on his chin. He was fifteen and growing out of his tunics faster than they could be made. “Uncle Quintus says that if we go to Pompey, Caesar will take the house.”

“Your uncle says a great many things before his breakfast.”

“Will he, though?”

Cicero turned round. His son was looking at him with that frank, expecting look the boy had, which he had learned, Cicero supposed, from being answered. “Caesar,” he said, “is a man of great courtesy, who has always been my friend, and who writes to me in the warmest terms. He will take nothing from us. Nor will we go anywhere in a hurry, whatever your uncle says. We will go where duty points, when it points. At present it points nowhere in particular, and so we will stay here by the sea and you will read your Xenophon. How far have you got?”

“The Persians are crossing a river.”

“Then go and see whether they get across.”

The boy went. Cicero watched him go, and was pleased with the answer, and then less pleased; it had been a good answer to a boy and no answer at all to the question, and the boy had known it and been kind enough not to say so. He thought he would tell Tiro about that. Tiro would have seen it at once.

---

The letters came at noon, by the post-boy from the Appian Way, and he read them in the library with the shutters half open and the light coming in off the water.

There was one from Atticus, long and careful and saying nothing that could be repeated, in which the only sentence worth having was a question about whether Cicero had yet decided where he would be in a month. There was one from Balbus, very warm, assuring him of Caesar’s regard and of Caesar’s earnest wish for peace, and saying that Caesar would think it the crown of all his hopes if Cicero, of all men, would help him to it. Cicero read that twice. It was a beautiful letter. It had been written by a man who did not think in Latin, and you could see the joins, but the joins had been made with great care, and somewhere behind them was a man who did not need to write to him at all and had. There was one from Pompey, four lines, asking him to come to Luceria, which was a week out of date: Pompey had gone on from Luceria. There was nothing from Tiro.

He sat back. Then he called for the boy who did the copying, a thin Bithynian, because Tiro was lying ill at Patrae, across the sea, where he had been left in the autumn with a fever, and nobody in the house could take dictation the way Tiro could.

“To Tiro,” he said, and walked.

The letter went well. It went the way his letters to Tiro always went, which was easily, because he did not have to be anyone in them. He told Tiro to eat, and to be careful of the doctor, who was a Greek and would be pleased to keep a Roman’s freedman ill for as long as a Roman’s freedman could pay. He told him that the house missed him, and that Marcus missed him, and that he himself was reduced to dictating to a Bithynian who could not spell *Pompeius* and wrote down everything twice. He told him the news, as much of it as was news: that Rome was empty, that the consuls had gone, that Caesar was in Picenum and the towns were opening their gates to him one by one like a row of oysters. He told him he was well. He told him that he was at Formiae because it was his duty to be at Formiae, watching the coast, as Pompey had asked.

“And,” he said, and stopped walking.

The boy waited with his stylus up.

He had been going to say: *and I am waiting to be asked.* He had it whole, the sentence, as he stood there by the window. It was the truest thing in the letter. He was waiting for Pompey to send for him in terms that a consular could not refuse, and he was waiting for Caesar to send for him in terms that a consular could accept, and he was waiting, if he were honest, for one of them to do it first so that he would not have to choose between them himself; and Tiro, who knew him, would read it and understand it and not think less of him, because Tiro never did.

“And?” said the boy.

“And take care of yourself,” Cicero said. “Above everything. Farewell.”

The boy wrote it, and read it back, and there was *Pompeius* spelled wrong in the fourth line, and Cicero corrected it himself and sealed it. Outside in the vestibule he could hear the lictors at the rods, unwinding the old laurel and putting on the new, and laughing about something in the low voices of men who had been told to be quiet in a great man’s house. The leaves would be green for a week, perhaps, in this wind. Then they would have to be done again.
