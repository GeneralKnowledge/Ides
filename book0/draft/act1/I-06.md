# Corfinium

POV: Laberius · outside and inside Corfinium · February 49

---

On the seventh night the men on the wall of Corfinium started calling down to the men in the ditch, and Laberius, who had the watch, let them.

It was very cold. The valley was full of frost and the mountains all round it were white to the foot, and the town sat in the middle of the valley on its rise with the wall going round it and the fires along the top of the wall, and the legion sat round the town in its lines with its own fires, and for six nights the two sets of fires had looked at each other and nobody had said anything. On the seventh the voices began at about the third hour of the night. One voice first, from the stretch of wall over Laberius’s part of the ditch, a young man’s voice, asking if anybody down there was from Firmum.

Nobody in the century was from Firmum. But a man along the line from the next century was from Cupra, which was close, and he shouted that up, and the voice on the wall shouted back, and after that it went on, quietly, the way men talk across a stream. Laberius stood in the ditch with his cloak round him and listened to it. He did not stop it. He had no orders to stop it, and the tribune was asleep, and the voices on the wall were Picene voices, most of them, the voices of the hill towns, and he had not got used to that yet.

“What’s he saying?” said Gaius, who had come up beside him with his hands in his armpits.

“He’s asking what we get paid.”

“Tell him.”

“I’m not going to tell him.”

“Then I will.” Gaius cupped his hands to his mouth and shouted the figure up to the wall, and added that it came late, and in bad coin, and that the general owed him two months of it. There was a pause on the wall, and then laughter, and the voice called down that their general had promised them land.

“How much?”

“Four iugera. Each man. From his own estates.”

There was a silence in the ditch while the men who could count worked out what four iugera was, and the men who could not asked the others.

“And where is he?” Gaius shouted.

There was a longer pause this time. Then a different voice on the wall, older, said: “In the house of the town council. With a guard on the door. Ours.” And somebody else on the wall laughed, and it was not a pleasant sound, and the young voice said something Laberius did not catch, and the fires along the top of the wall moved as men moved in front of them.

---

The word came round before dawn. The garrison had found out that Domitius meant to slip away by night with a few friends and leave them to it, and they had taken him, and put a guard on him, and sent to Caesar to say that they would open the gates and give him up. Caesar would not go in. He would not go in in the dark, the word said, in case the men went in after him and took the town apart, and so he had the gates watched and the walls watched and waited for light.

So they waited. When the light came it came slowly, grey over the white mountains, and the frost on the ditch-banks turned from black to white, and the gate opened.

They brought the gentlemen out first.

Laberius was in the cordon. The general had his tribunal set up on the flat ground in front of the lines, and the century had been put across the approach to it in a line, with orders that the men were to keep their mouths shut and their hands to themselves, and Laberius stood at the end of the line where he could see the gate and the tribunal both. He watched them come out. There were fifty of them, perhaps a few more. Some were old and some were young, and some of the young ones were holding the old ones by the arm, and they walked in a kind of loose column across the frozen ground between two files of the garrison’s own soldiers, who were taking them out to Caesar the way you take a man to the magistrate when he has been caught in your barn.

He looked at their shoes. He did not mean to; it was what he looked at. They had senators’ shoes, some of them, the red ones with the straps going up the calf and the little ivory crescents, and the others had the shoes of men who had never walked further than from a litter to a door. Every pair was wet through. The frost had got into the leather and turned it dark, and the men in them were walking carefully, picking their way, the way men walk who know the ground is going to hurt them.

The legion did not keep its mouth shut. Not all of it. There was a noise along the line as they passed, a kind of growl, and some words in it, and a man two places down from Laberius said something about Domitius’s mother. Laberius did not turn his head. Vibius, behind the line, put the end of his staff into the man’s back, once, quite hard, and the man stopped.

Caesar stood up on the tribunal when they reached him. He was bareheaded in the cold. He spoke to them, not long, in the voice Laberius had heard him use to tribunes who had lost a cohort’s baggage: not loud, not angry, the voice of a man who would like to know how it had happened. He said something about how he had been treated, and about the people who had driven tribunes out of Rome, and about the favours some of the men in front of him had had from him in the past and the use they had made of them. Laberius did not follow all of it. And then Caesar said that they could go. All of them. Where they liked. He said that he would not stop them, and that nobody in his army would touch them, and he looked along the cordon when he said it.

The gentlemen stood there. For a moment none of them moved. One of the old ones, a heavy grey man with a red face, who was being held up by a young one, began to cry. Laberius saw his mouth go square. Then the young one said something to him and turned him round, and they all began to walk back towards the gate, on their wet shoes, picking their way.

“That’s it?” said the man two down, the one Vibius had poked.

Nobody answered him. Then the chests came out.

There were six of them, on a cart. They came out of the gate after the gentlemen had gone back in, and the cart was pulled up in front of the tribunal, and a clerk climbed up on the back of it and opened the lids one by one, and the men in the cordon could see what was in them, because the clerk tilted each lid back so that the general could see, and the sun had just come up over the mountains and caught it.

It was silver. It was coin, in bags, and some of the bags had split. The clerk stood on the cart and called out a figure to the general that Laberius did not understand, because it had more noughts in it than any figure he had ever heard spoken aloud. Then Domitius was brought out, by himself, between two of his own men, a big fair man with his face swollen as if he had slept badly or been crying, and Caesar said something to him, and the clerk shut the lids, and the cart was turned round and taken back towards the gate. Domitius walked behind it.

“Is that his?” Gaius said, very low.

“It’s his now.”

“Where did he get it?”

“Pompey gave it him,” said Vibius from behind. “To pay the garrison with. That’s the garrison’s pay, that is. Going home with him.” He was quiet a moment. “Lucky garrison.”

---

In the afternoon Domitius’s soldiers took the oath to Caesar.

They were drawn up on the same flat ground, cohort by cohort, more of them than Laberius would have thought the town could hold, and an officer read out the oath and they said it after him, all together, in a noise like surf. Then they were broken up and told off to their new legions, a few hundred here and a few hundred there, and a hundred or so were marched over to Laberius’s cohort to be shared out among the centuries. They stood about in front of the tents with their kit at their feet, looking at the men they had been looking at across the ditch for a week, and the men looked back.

One of them was the young man from the wall. Laberius knew him by his voice when he asked where he was to sleep. He was from Firmum, he said, and he had a cut on his chin from shaving in the cold, and his shoes were very bad.

“What happened to the land?” Gaius asked him.

The young man looked at Gaius, and then at the ground. “He was going to give it us,” he said. “Four iugera. He told us so himself, in the forum, the day before Caesar came. He stood up and said it, from his own estates, and we cheered him.” He picked at the cut on his chin. “Then the next night he was going to go off over the wall with his friends and leave us. So we went and got him.”

“And now he’s going home,” Gaius said. “With the money.”

“Yes.”

“And you’re here.”

“Yes.” The young man looked up. He did not seem angry. He seemed to be trying to understand something, the way a man tries to understand a sum he has been given the answer to. “They said we can’t be hurt. Caesar said. Nobody in his army’s to touch us. Same as them.”

Laberius found that he was angry. He did not know why. He stood there in front of the tents with his thumbs in his belt and looked at the young man’s bad shoes, and at the cut on his chin, and thought about the six chests going back in through the gate on the cart with Domitius walking behind them, and he could feel it in his chest like a stone. It was not his money. He had not been owed it. Nobody had promised it to him. It was nothing to do with him at all, what gentlemen gave each other at the end of a siege.

He opened his mouth to say something and did not know what it would be.

“Well,” Vibius said, before he could. He had come up behind them with the tablet, to write the new men in. “You’ve done better than him, lad. He’s got six chests and no army. You’ve got an army.” He wrote the young man’s name down without asking how it was spelled. “Which is the one you wanted, I expect. Since you went and got him.”

The young man laughed, uncertainly. Gaius laughed. Laberius heard himself make a sound, and then Vibius had moved on to the next new man, and it was done.

They were on the road south by the ninth hour, with the new men in the middle of the column where they could be watched, and Corfinium behind them on its rise with its gates open and the smoke going up straight in the cold.
