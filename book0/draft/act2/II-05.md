# Letters

POV: Nysa · Rome, the chief priest’s house on the Sacred Way · July 49

---

When a letter came from Spain the mistress did not eat, and when a letter came from Massilia she did, and by the end of the month Nysa could tell which had come by what went back to the kitchen on the tray.

She was on the trays that summer. It was not her work. It was Chreste’s, but Chreste had burned her hand on the big pot in the spring and it had gone bad, and while it healed the cook had given Nysa the trays because she was quick on the stairs and did not talk to the mistress’s women. So she carried them up to the small room off the garden, where the mistress took her meals alone when the master was away, which was always, and set them down on the table by the window and went out again; and an hour later she went back and carried them down. That was all. But she saw what was on them going up and what was on them coming down, and the cook did not, because the cook never looked at a tray once it had left her hands.

On the day the letter came about the floods, the tray came down as it had gone up. The bread was whole. The eggs had not been cut. The little dish of olives had not been touched, and there was a fly on them.

“She’s not eating,” Nysa said to the cook.

“She never eats.” The cook was gutting fish and did not look up. “She’s thin. Thin people don’t eat. It’s how they stay thin.”

Nysa scraped the eggs into the bucket for the pigs and did not say anything. She had heard the steward in the passage that morning, reading the letter out to the mistress in his flat voice, because it had come from one of the master’s officers and was in a bad hand. She had been on the stairs with the tray and had slowed down. *The river has risen.* That was what she had heard. *Both bridges are down. We are between the rivers and the corn is short and the convoy cannot reach us.* And then the steward had heard her on the stairs and stopped, and waited until she had gone past.

The street knew by the afternoon. The street always knew. Dama came back from the baker’s and said that men were going to Afranius’s house to tell his wife how glad they were, because her husband had Caesar shut up in Spain with nothing to eat, and that there were so many of them the porter had shut the door.

“How many?” said Nysa.

Dama shrugged. “Lots.”

“How many is lots?”

“More than came here,” Dama said, and the cook laughed, and Nysa went out to the storeroom.

---

Six days later a letter came from Massilia, and the tray came down with nothing on it but the stones of the olives.

This one Nysa had read herself. Not all of it; she could not read fast. But it had come up from the side door with the courier while the steward was in the bath, and the courier had given it to Phileros, and Phileros had put it on the table in the steward’s room to wait for him, open, because the courier had already broken the seal to read it to the men at the gate. And Nysa had been sent to the steward’s room for a lamp-wick tally. And she had stood at the table with the tally in her hand and looked down at the letter, and read it.

Nobody had taught her. She had picked it up the way she picked up most things, by watching, and then by herself with whatever was written down that she could get her eyes on: the jar labels in the storeroom, the steward’s tallies, the notices painted on the walls in the street about games and elections and lost slaves. She could read Greek better than Latin. She read Latin like a woman crossing a river on stones, one stone and then stop, and then the next.

*Brutus,* the letter said. There were Brutuses who came to dinner; she did not know which this was until the next word. *Decimus Brutus with the fleet has met the ships of the Massiliots off the islands and has*. And the next word was long and she did not have it. Then *taken*. Then a number she did have, because numbers were easy, numbers were the same in any hand. *Nine.* *Sunk*, she thought, though she was not sure. And something about the city, and something about Domitius, which was a name she knew from the kitchen, from the winter, from the business at Corfinium.

She heard the steward’s feet on the stairs and put the tally down on top of the letter, and then took it off again, and was standing by the lamp shelf with her back to the table counting wicks when he came in with his hair wet.

“What are you doing?”

“The tally. The cook sent me.”

He looked at her and then at the letter and then at her again. She counted wicks. He picked the letter up and read it, standing, and his face changed, and he went out of the room with it fast, towards the mistress, and forgot to ask her anything.

---

After that the house started buying.

It was the steward’s doing, but it was the mistress’s order; Nysa heard her give it. She was in the garden room with the steward and the tray had just gone up, and she said, in the voice she used for linen: “Oil for the year. Wool for the year. Not for the season. And wine, whatever you think. And lamp oil. Buy it now, while it’s to be had.”

“Lady. If the news is good the price will come down.”

“Then we’ll have paid too much for it. Buy it now.”

So for the next week the carts came to the side door every morning, and the house filled up. Nysa had never seen the storeroom so full. There were oil jars in two rows and then three, and then in the passage outside the storeroom, against the wall, with straw between them; there was wool in sacks up to the ceiling of the room behind the kitchen, so that you had to go into it sideways; there were wine jars in the cellar where the old wine had been, and the old wine came up to the dining room and was drunk by the steward and Phileros in the evenings. She counted all of it. Nobody asked her to. She did it because it was there, and because she liked to know, and because the steward’s tally board had got so crowded with marks that she did not trust it.

Chreste came and watched her count, with her bad hand held up against her chest in its bandage.

“Why are we buying all this?”

“For the year.”

“I know for the year. Why?”

Nysa thought about it. She had thought about it, carrying jars. “Because the news is good,” she said.

Chreste looked at her as if she were mad.

“If the news is good,” Nysa said, “it means it’s nearly over. And when it’s over everyone will want oil and wool at once, to make up for not buying it, and the price will go up. So we’re buying it now, before the end. Before everyone else knows.”

She was rather pleased with that. She had worked it out on the stairs.

Chreste thought about it for a while. “Then why didn’t she buy it after the bad letter?”

Nysa did not have an answer for that. She went on counting.

“Anyway,” Chreste said. “How do you know the news was good? Nobody told the kitchen anything.”

“She ate the olives.”

“Is that all?”

“And the bread,” Nysa said. “And the eggs. Both.”

Chreste laughed so much that she had to sit down on a sack, and held her bad hand up out of the way while she did it, and said that Nysa should be a soothsayer, and read the future in egg-shells, and charge for it. Nysa laughed too. She did not say anything about the letter. She did not know why not, exactly; it was not that Chreste would tell. Chreste told everything, but she told it the way a fountain tells, to whoever was standing near, and nobody listened to a fountain. It was more that if she said she had read it, she would be a girl who read the master’s letters. And she did not know what happened to a girl who read the master’s letters. She had never heard of one.

That night she lay on the pallet and said the words over to herself in the dark, the ones she had. *Brutus. Fleet. Islands. Taken. Nine.* And the long word she did not have, which began with a *sup* or a *sub*, and which she tried in her head with every ending she could think of, until she was asleep.
