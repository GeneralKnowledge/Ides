# Polybius

POV: Brutus · Pompey’s camp in Macedonia · August 49

---

Pompey stood up.

Brutus saw it happen from the door of the tent, and for a moment did not understand what he was seeing. The tent was very large, the largest he had ever been in, with its sides rolled up for the heat and a floor of planks laid over the earth, and it was full of men: officers, senators, a consul, two former consuls, men he had sat with in the Senate house and dined with and gone to law against, all of them standing or sitting on the couches along the sides in the white light that came in under the awning. And at the end of the tent on a raised chair, with a table in front of him covered in maps, the man who had had his father killed. Brutus had come in with the officer of the day, who announced him. He had been ready to cross the tent, and stop at the proper distance, and say what he had prepared on the ship, which was short and grave and said nothing he would have to take back.

He did not get to say it. Pompey looked up from the maps and saw who it was and stood up, quite suddenly, so that the chair went back on the planks with a scrape, and came down off the dais and across the floor towards him through all those men, with his arms already out. He was a big man, and he had grown heavy, and he moved like a big man who had once been very quick. The men in his way stepped back. The whole tent watched him do it.

Then Brutus was being embraced. Pompey had him by both shoulders and then round the back, the way a father takes a son who has come home from a long journey, and Brutus smelt wine and sweat and the oil in the great man’s hair, and felt the big hands, and stood there.

“Marcus Brutus,” Pompey said, letting him go and holding him at arm’s length to look at him. His face was red and pleased and very open. “Marcus Brutus. Here. I was told you would come. I did not believe it.” He turned round to the tent, still holding one of Brutus’s arms. “Look who’s come to us.”

There was a sound from the men in the tent, a murmur, and then some of them began to clap.

Brutus found that he was smiling. He had not meant to smile. He had meant to look grave and courteous and a little distant, as was fitting for a man who had come not for Pompey but for the Republic, and he found that his face was smiling without asking him, and that he could not stop it, and that he was glad, quite simply glad, in a way he had not been glad since he was a boy. He did not understand it. He let himself be led up to the dais and given a seat beside the great man’s chair, and a cup of wine, and he drank the wine, and answered questions about the voyage and about Cilicia and about his mother, and did not hear half of what he said.

---

Afterwards, in his own tent, he sat for some time on the edge of the camp bed with his hands between his knees.

It was a good tent. Somebody had seen to it before he arrived; there was a bed and a table and a chair and a chest, and a lamp on the table, and a jar of water with a cloth over it. His books had been brought in from the baggage and stacked on the floor beside the chest. His slave was unpacking them, very carefully, wiping the dust off each roll with a cloth before he put it on the table.

Brutus looked at his hands. He was thinking about what had happened in the big tent, and trying to find the right way to think about it, and he could not.

He had expected Pompey to be civil. Pompey was always civil; it was the first thing anybody said about him. He had expected him to receive Brutus as a senator of good family who had come over, and to thank him, and to find him something to do. He had not expected him to get up. A commander-in-chief, in his own headquarters, in front of the consuls, did not get up for a man of thirty-six who had held no office above the quaestorship. He sent for him, or nodded to him, or let him wait. Pompey had got up as if Brutus were his superior. And he had done it, Brutus thought, knowing perfectly well what Brutus’s father had been to him, and what he had been to Brutus’s father, and that the whole tent knew it too.

It had been very gracious. It had been, he supposed, meant as a kind of apology, or as a kind of statement to the camp about the kind of man Pompey was. It had certainly been meant to be seen.

He knew all that. He had known it while it was happening. And still he had smiled.

“Master,” the slave said. “Which first?”

Brutus looked at the table. The slave had the rolls in a row. “Polybius,” he said. “The third book. And the wax tablets, and a stylus. Two.”

---

He worked in the afternoons, in the worst of the heat, when the camp slept.

He was making an epitome. It was a thing he had wanted to do for years, and had never had the time: to take the whole of Polybius, the forty books of him, all the wars and treaties and digressions and arguments with other historians, and reduce them to what a man needed to know, in a form a man could carry. It was dull work and he loved it. He read a passage, and read it again, and then wrote down in a few lines on the wax what it said, plainly, without the arguments; and then, in the evening, if the lamp was good, he copied the day’s wax onto a roll in ink. He was on the third book. The Carthaginians were crossing the Alps with their elephants, and Polybius was explaining at great length why the other historians were wrong about which pass they used.

Outside the tent, the camp did not sleep as much as it was supposed to.

There were officers in the next lines, young men of good family who had come out with the consuls, and they sat in the shade of an awning and drank and talked through the afternoon, and Brutus could hear them through the leather as clearly as if they were in the tent with him. He did not try to listen. It was impossible not to. They talked about Caesar’s men. Not about the soldiers; about the senators and knights who had stayed in Italy, the men who had gone over to Caesar or had simply stayed in their houses with the shutters closed, waiting. They talked about what would happen to those men when Pompey came back to Italy with the army, which was a matter of time.

“The neutrals first,” one of them said. “Every man who stayed at home. If he wasn’t with us he was against us. Pompey said so himself, at Luceria.”

“He said it at dinner.”

“He said it. The goods of every man who stayed behind. There’ll be enough for everyone.”

“I’ve got my eye on a house on the Palatine,” said another voice, lazier. “Belongs to a man who’s been dining with Lepidus all summer. Very good house. Lovely fishponds.”

“You’ll have to fight Domitius for it. He’s had his eye on half the Palatine since Corfinium. He says he’s owed.”

“Domitius is in Massilia.”

“Then I’ll fight his steward.”

They laughed. Somebody called for more wine. Brutus sat with the stylus in his hand and looked at the wax, where he had written *Hannibal* and nothing after it, and found that he had lost his place.

It was not, he told himself, that he disapproved. Or not only that. Of course he disapproved; it was not fitting, it was not the way the Republic should be restored, by young men drinking under awnings and dividing the houses of other senators before a single battle had been fought; and if he had been asked, he would have said so, and said it well. But nobody had asked. And what he felt, sitting there with the stylus, was not disapproval at all, or not chiefly. It was a kind of tiredness, as if he had walked a long way to a house and found, at the door, that the people inside were having the same conversation he had left behind.

He did not go out and say anything. He thought about it, and then he thought that it would be absurd, a man of his name and age going out in the heat to lecture boys about their manners, and that they would laugh at him afterwards, kindly, in the same voices; and he thought that he was above it. He was here for the laws. Not for houses. That was the difference between him and them, and it was enough to know it.

He found his place in Polybius. The Carthaginians were still in the Alps. He read the passage, and read it again, and wrote down what it said, plainly, in four lines, without the argument about the pass; and the voices outside went on about fishponds.

At the evening meal Pompey sent a slave with a dish from his own table, two quails and a honey cake, with his compliments. Brutus sent back his thanks. He ate the quails. He gave the cake to his slave.
