# Caieta

POV: Cicero · the shore at Caieta · June 49

---

The ship was smaller than he had been promised and larger than he had feared, and Cicero stood on the shingle at Caieta in the late afternoon and looked at it for some time before he allowed himself to be pleased.

It lay a little off the beach with its sail furled, a merchantman with a high stern and an eye painted on the bow, rocking very slightly on a sea that was, for once, behaving. The master had been paid. The master had been paid twice, in fact, once by Cicero’s steward and once by Terentia’s, because neither had known the other had done it, and the master had said nothing about it to either of them, which Cicero thought showed a proper understanding of the times. The boats were going out to it now with the baggage. There was not a great deal of baggage. He had told them to bring only what a man needed who was going to a camp, and they had brought, he could see from here, two chests of books.

“Father.” Tullia had come down from the villa behind him and stood at his elbow. “The boys are in the boat already. Marcus has been sick twice.”

“We haven’t left.”

“He’s practising.”

He laughed, and turned to look at her. She had come down on her own feet, which she should not have done; she was still pale, and she held her shawl round her with both hands, though the evening was warm. She had come out from Rome ten days ago against the doctor’s advice and his own, a month after the birth, because she had wanted to see him before he went. He had told her not to. She had come anyway, as she always did, and he had been so glad of it that he had been angry with her for a whole morning, and she had let him be.

The child had come at seven months, a boy, very small. It was alive. It was in Rome with a wet-nurse and a doctor, and the doctor’s letters said that it took the breast and that one must not hope too much, and Tullia read them in the mornings at breakfast and said nothing about them, and folded them, and put them in the box with her combs.

“You should sit,” he said. “There’s a rock.”

“I’ve sat for a month.” She looked at the ship. “It’s not very large.”

“It’s large enough for Greece.”

“Yes.” She was quiet a moment. Behind them on the terrace of the villa he could hear his brother’s voice, raised, telling some slave how a chest should be carried, and his nephew’s voice disagreeing. The Ciceros were travelling together, all four of them, as if to a wedding. “Father. You won’t wait?”

“For what?”

“For Spain. To see.” She said it carefully, looking at the ship, not at him. “Everyone says it will be decided in Spain by the end of the summer. One way or the other. If you were to wait until then you’d know where to go. You’d go to the man who’d won. Or if Pompey won in Spain you’d go to him with a whole skin and he’d be glad of you. And if it went the other way.” She stopped.

“If it went the other way,” Cicero said, “I should be the man who waited to see which way it went.” He said it gently. “There are a good many of those in Italy this summer, my dear. They are all in their country houses, with their shutters closed. I have been one of them since January. I find I am not good at it.”

She did not answer. She held the shawl.

He knew what she was not saying, or thought he did. Her husband was with Caesar. Dolabella had gone over to Caesar in the first week of the war, with a great deal of noise about it, and was now commanding something on the Adriatic, and writing to her, when he wrote, in a hand that sloped forward like a man running; and when her father sailed for Pompey’s camp, her father and her husband would be on two sides of the sea, and there would be nobody who could stand between them. She was not saying that. She was saying wait for Spain, which was a reasonable thing to say, which Atticus had said too, more than once. And she was saying it because she was frightened, and she was too proud and too much his daughter to say that she was frightened, and so she had found a reason instead, a good one, with a precedent attached. He was so proud of her for it that he could hardly look at her.

“Dolabella,” he said. “When he writes. You’ll tell him I spoke of him kindly.”

“Did you?”

“I’m about to.” He took her hand, the one that was not holding the shawl. It was cold. “He’s young, and he’s chosen the side that will be good for his debts, and he will do very well out of it, and I hope he does, for your sake. You may tell him that. Leave out the part about the debts.”

She smiled for the first time. It was a small smile and it did not last. “He’d know it was there anyway.”

“Then he’ll know I meant it.”

---

The steward came down from the villa with a satchel and a face, and Cicero knew what the face meant before the man opened his mouth.

“From the mistress, sir. From Rome. The sum for the voyage.” The steward held out the satchel. “Philotimus sends it, with her compliments. Sir, it’s short.”

“How short?”

The steward told him. Cicero did the sum in his head and arrived, without much surprise, at a figure about a third less than the one he had written to Terentia for, and a good deal less than the figure Philotimus would put in her accounts. He had arrived at this kind of figure before. He had arrived at it every year for ten years, whenever money came from his wife’s side of the household through the hands of his wife’s freedman; and he had never yet said so to her, because she was his wife, and because Philotimus was hers, and because the one occasion on which he had come near saying it had been the worst month of their marriage.

“Put it with the rest,” he said.

“Sir. It will not be enough for the camp. Not if we’re there through the winter.”

“Then we shall not be there through the winter.” He waved the man away. “Or we shall eat what the army eats. I’m told it’s very good for the character.”

Tullia was watching him. She had heard all of it. She said nothing; she looked at the satchel going away up the path in the steward’s hand, and then out to sea, and he saw her lips move once, as if she had begun a sentence and decided against it. He knew which sentence. It was the one about her mother. She had her own reasons, he knew, to think about money that went through other people’s hands: her dowry was in Dolabella’s, all of it, and Dolabella had never once paid a debt on the day.

He did not say anything about that either. There was a good deal, that evening on the beach, that neither of them said; it seemed to him that they had agreed to it, without speaking, as a kindness, and that it was the best conversation he had had with anyone in a year.

---

The light went. The boats came back for them. His brother came down from the terrace still talking, and his nephew behind him with a face like thunder, and the slaves carrying the last chest between them, and they all stood on the shingle together in the dusk while the boatmen held the boat against the stones.

“You’ll write,” Tullia said.

“From every harbour.”

“Write about yourself. Not about Pompey. I can hear about Pompey from anyone.” She let go of the shawl, finally, and put her arms round his neck, quickly, and he held her and felt how thin she was under the wool. “And eat. Whatever the army eats.”

“For my character.”

“For me.”

He got into the boat. It rocked and he sat down more suddenly than he had meant to, and somebody laughed, and it was Tullia. He was glad of that. He sat in the stern with his hands on his knees and watched her on the shingle, a pale shape with a shawl, standing quite still, until the boat came round the stern of the ship and she was hidden.

On deck it was very crowded and smelled of tar and of the boys being sick. He stood at the rail. The shore was a dark line now with the villa’s lamps on it and one lamp lower down, at the water’s edge, which he decided was hers. He thought about what he would say in Pompey’s camp, when he got there. He had it nearly ready. It was a good speech, and grave, and it began with the reasons a consular who had saved the Republic once could not stand aside while it was in danger, and it went on through the claims of friendship and the obligations of gratitude, and it ended with the duty of a man to be where the best men were. It said nothing about how he would be received. It did not need to. He would be received, he knew, as a man is received who has come late but has come; and Pompey would embrace him; and the best men would make room for him by the fire; and that, if he was honest, was the whole of it.

He stood there until the lamp on the shore went out. Then he went below, and was sick, quite violently, a great deal of bile, and felt very much better, and slept.
