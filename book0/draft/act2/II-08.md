# The Lot

POV: Laberius · Placentia, the camps by the Po · October 49

---

The five hundred had been promised at Brundisium, in the spring, on the day Pompey’s last ships went out past the harbour mole, and by the time the army came back over the mountains from Spain to Placentia there was not a man in it who could not have told you the day and the hour and what the weather had been.

Laberius could. It had been raining. The general had stood on the mole in the rain with the empty harbour behind him and the last of Pompey’s sails going away grey into the grey, and he had spoken to them, the legions that had come down from Gaul and through Picenum and Corfinium in the snow, and he had said that they had done everything he had asked and more, and that when it was over every man would have five hundred denarii from his hand. Five hundred. More than two years’ pay. Laberius had heard the men round him say it after, under their breath, the way men say the name of a girl.

That was in the spring. Then there had been Spain, and the river, and the hunger, and the Lusitanians on their goatskins; and Afranius had given up, in the end, in the hills with his water cut off, his men so thirsty, it was said, that they were licking the stones; and it had been won without a battle, as the general liked, and nobody had paid anybody anything. And now it was the end of the summer, by the sun, and the army was in camp by the Po outside Placentia, waiting for the general, who was at Massilia taking the surrender; and the plain was flat and hot and full of dust, and the river was low and brown and smelt, and there was nothing to do.

“Five hundred,” Gaius said. He was lying on his back outside the tent with his arm over his eyes. He had said it every day for a month. “I’ve spent it. In my head. Three times.”

“What on?”

“First time, on a girl at Ravenna. Second time, on a vineyard. Third time I bought the girl a vineyard.” He moved his arm and squinted up at Laberius. “You?”

“I haven’t spent it.”

“That’s why you’re a centurion. You can wait for things.” Gaius put his arm back. “I can’t. And the Ninth can’t either, by the sound of them.”

---

The Ninth was in the camp next to theirs, down towards the river, and you could hear it. That was what Laberius would remember afterwards, when he thought about Placentia, which he did not do often: that you could hear it coming for days, the way you hear a storm in the mountains before you see it. At first it was only noise in the evenings, singing, and a lot of it, and shouting. Then the shouting started in the daytime. Then one morning there was a crowd of men from the Ninth outside their own tribunes’ tents, not in ranks, not in kit, standing about in the dust with their arms folded, and a man on a cart in the middle of them talking, and the tribunes inside their tents with the flaps down.

Laberius went and stood at the edge of his own lines and watched it, with Vibius and Rufrius, who had come over from the next tent to see.

“What are they saying?” said Rufrius. He had been in the army a quarter of a year and still asked what things meant, which Laberius liked.

“The five hundred,” said Vibius. “They want it. Now. And their discharge, some of them. They say the war’s going on too long and they were promised it would be short. They say they’ve done Gaul and Spain and they won’t do Greece, not without being paid for Spain first.”

“Is that wrong?”

Vibius looked at him. “It’s not wrong. It’s stupid.”

“Why?”

“Because they’re saying it out loud,” Vibius said, and turned round and went back to the tent.

Laberius stayed. He watched the man on the cart. He could not hear the words from here, only the shape of them, the voice going up and the crowd answering. He knew the shape. He had been in the middle of it himself, more or less, every evening for a month. He had sat outside his own tent at night with Gaius and Vibius and the others, and Rufrius from next door, and a jar, and he had said, as everybody said, that the general was the best general in the world and never paid on the day; and he had said that a man could not eat a promise; and once, late, he had said that if the money did not come before the army went to Greece, he for one would want to know why. He had said it in front of eight or nine men. One of them might have been anybody.

“Cordus,” Rufrius said beside him. “You’ve gone quiet.”

“I’m always quiet.”

“Quieter.” Rufrius looked at him, and then at the crowd round the cart, and then back. He did not say anything else. After a while he went back to his own tent, and Laberius heard him take his boots off.

---

The general came from Massilia faster than anybody had thought a man could.

He came in at the end of the afternoon, with a few horsemen, dusty to the eyes, and did not go to his tent. He went to the Ninth’s camp. And the next morning, early, every legion at Placentia was paraded in the open ground between the camps, in full kit, by cohorts, in the heat, with the Ninth in the middle and the others round it on three sides, so that every man in the army could see the Ninth’s faces and the Ninth could see every man in the army.

Laberius stood at the right of his century in the front rank, where he could see the tribunal. It was not far. The general came up onto it and stood with his hands behind his back and looked at the Ninth for a long time before he said anything.

Then he spoke.

Laberius did not remember afterwards most of what he said. He remembered the voice, which was the same voice it had been on the mole at Brundisium, clear and level and carrying, and he remembered that it did not get angry. It said that the Ninth had been promised a reward and would have it, when the war was over, as every legion would; that the general had never once failed to pay what he had promised, though he had not always paid it on the day the soldiers would have chosen; that soldiers did not choose the day. It said that a legion which held its general to ransom in the middle of a war was not a legion but a crowd of armed men. And it said that he would now do what the law of their fathers laid down for a legion which mutinied in the field, which was to take one man in ten.

There was a sound from the Ninth then that Laberius had not heard from soldiers before and did not hear again for a long time. It was not a shout. It came up out of the whole legion at once, low, a kind of groan, and it went on.

The Ninth’s officers went down on their knees. All of them. The tribunes and the centurions, in front of their men, in the dust, with their hands out. Laberius watched them do it. He could see the face of the nearest one, a centurion of about his own age, grey and big, with his helmet off and sweat running into his eyes, saying something over and over that Laberius could not hear.

The general let them stay there for a while.

Then he said that he would not take one in ten. He would take the men who had led it. He would have their names.

---

There were a hundred and twenty names in the end. Laberius did not see how they were got. He heard afterwards that the Ninth’s centurions had been made to give them, each for his own century, and that some of them had given them at once and some had not, and that the ones who had not had been told that they would be on the list themselves if they did not. The hundred and twenty were brought out in front of the legion and made to stand in a line, with their swords taken from them, and an officer went along the line with a helmet.

It was the lot. Laberius knew what it was before he saw it. He had heard of it, as every soldier had, the way children hear of wolves: the helmet, and the tokens in it, and every man putting his hand in. Twelve tokens were marked.

The officer went along the line. Each man put his hand in. Some of them did it quickly and some of them could not make their hand go in and had to be helped, and one of them was sick on the ground before he did it, and when they drew the token out they held it up and the officer looked at it. Laberius found that he was counting them. He did not mean to. He counted the hands going into the helmet and the hands coming out and the men who stepped forward, one, and then nobody for a long time, and then two, three together. He could not stop counting. It was like counting the javelins coming down at a hill.

When the officer got to the eighty-first man in the line there was nobody there.

There was a gap. The line closed up on either side of it, but there was a place where a man should have been and was not, and the officer stopped in front of it with the helmet. A centurion of the Ninth came forward, the big grey one Laberius had watched on his knees, and spoke to the officer, and the officer spoke to the general. Laberius could not hear what they said. He saw the general look at the gap in the line for a long moment. Then he saw him send for somebody, and a clerk came with a tablet, and the clerk read something, and the general turned his head and looked at the grey centurion.

Word came along the ranks before the end of the morning, from man to man, the way word does. The man whose name had been given for the gap had not been at Placentia at all when the mutiny began. He had been at Cremona with a convoy of mules, on a pass signed by his own tribune, for the whole of that month; and the centurion who had given his name had known it, or should have known it, and had given it anyway, to make up his number, or for some reason of his own, because he did not like the man or because he owed him money or for no reason. And the general had found it out, in front of the army, with a clerk and a tablet, in the time it takes to read a line.

The centurion took the absent man’s place. He did not have to put his hand in the helmet. There was no need.

Twelve of them died that afternoon, by the river, in front of the Ninth. Laberius’s cohort was not near enough to see it done, and he was glad of that, and did not say so.

---

That night nobody sat outside the tents with a jar.

Laberius lay under his cloak with his boots off and looked at the dark. He could hear the river, and somewhere a long way off a dog, and from the Ninth’s camp, nothing. Gaius was asleep. Gaius could sleep through anything; it was the thing Laberius envied him most. Vibius was not asleep. Laberius could tell by his breathing.

“He’ll pay the Ninth,” Vibius said, after a long time, into the dark. “In the end. You’ll see. He’ll pay them the same as us. To the denarius.”

“I know.”

“He’ll take them to Greece first.”

“I know.”

Vibius was quiet. Then he said: “That was a centurion.”

Laberius did not answer. He lay there and thought about the names he would have to give, if anybody ever asked him for names, and about the eight or nine men round the jar on the evenings outside the tent, and which of them had been at Cremona with the mules, and which had not; and he found that he did not know. He had never needed to know. He had never kept a list of where his men were, beyond the morning count. He had never thought it was something a centurion would have to be sure of.

In the morning he told Vibius to get a tablet. Vibius looked at him and got one. They sat outside the tent in the early light, before the heat came up, and Vibius wrote down the names of the century, all eighty of them, and next to each name, as Laberius said it, where the man had been on every day of the month, as far as he could remember; and where he could not remember, he went and asked.
