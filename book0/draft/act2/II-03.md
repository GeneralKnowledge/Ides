# The Sicoris

POV: Laberius · Ilerda, Caesar’s camp between the rivers · June 49

---

Laberius cut a notch in the stake at first light and another at noon, and by noon the first notch was under water.

He had driven the stake himself, the day after the bridges went, at the edge of the flood where the camp’s lower ditch ran out into what had been a meadow and was now a lake. It was a tent-peg, an old one, split at the top, and he had hammered it in with the flat of a mattock and cut a notch at the waterline with his knife, and since then he had come down to it three times a day and cut another, and looked at the distance between them. Nobody had told him to. He did it because he wanted to know, and because standing at the edge of the water looking at a stake was something a centurion could do that looked like work.

“Up again,” said Gaius, coming down behind him. He had his cloak over his head against the rain, which had not stopped for three days, and he squatted down and looked at the notches. “That’s a hand since morning.”

“Half a hand.”

“It’s a hand if you’re short.” Gaius looked out over the water. It was brown, and fast in the middle, and things went by on it: branches, a dead sheep with its legs in the air, once a whole cart without its wheels, turning slowly. On the far side, a long way off now, the ground came up out of the water and the road went up from it towards the hills where the snow was, and on the road nothing moved. “There’s a convoy on that road somewhere,” Gaius said. “From Gaul. Corn and archers and the Gaulish horse and a lot of boys from good families coming out to see the war. They were three days off when the bridges went. They’re still three days off. They’ll be three days off till the water goes down.”

“Then we’ll eat when the water goes down.”

“We’ll eat what?”

Laberius did not answer. He looked at the stake.

---

There were two rivers. That was the trouble, as far as he understood it, and he did not try to understand it further than that. The camp was on a strip of high ground between them, the Sicoris in front and the Cinga behind, thirty miles apart where they came down out of the mountains and closer together here, and the army had come down into the strip in the spring sunshine, by the sun’s reckoning, with both rivers low and two good bridges, and had built a camp, and had faced Afranius’s camp on its hill across the Sicoris, and the two armies had dug at each other for a fortnight. Then it had rained. Then the snow on the mountains had come down all at once into both rivers, and the river had got up over the meadows in a night and taken both bridges as if they were made of straw, and in the morning the army was on an island.

Afranius was not. Afranius had his town and his bridge, a stone one, and his granaries, and the whole of the country on his side of the river to forage in; and every morning his light troops came down to the water’s edge opposite the camp, the Lusitanians and the hill men, and blew up their skins.

Laberius watched them do it again that afternoon, from the ditch. They had goatskins, sewn and greased, that they carried folded on their backs like cloaks, and they stood at the edge of the flood and put the necks to their mouths and blew, red in the face, until the skins were round and tight as drums. Then they tied them off and walked into the water with the skins under their chests and their spears across them and pushed off, kicking, and the current took them and they let it, and they came across a quarter of a mile downstream in the time it would take to walk it, and came up out of the shallows on Caesar’s side shaking themselves like dogs. They did it to cut off the foragers. Any party that went out from the camp to find corn had to go down the strip between the rivers, and the Lusitanians were waiting, and the foragers came back with less corn than they went out with, or did not come back.

“Look at that,” said a man from the second rank, beside him. “Like ducks.”

“You ever done that?” said another.

“Done what, swum? Course I’ve swum. Everybody’s swum.”

Laberius said nothing. He had never swum. He had grown up on a hillside where the stream at the bottom came up to a child’s knee in summer and you could step across it in winter on the stones, and he had gone for a soldier at twenty-two, and in fifteen years of rivers in Gaul he had crossed every one of them on a bridge, or a ford, or a boat, or with his hand on a rope, and he had never once said so to anybody. It did not come up. A centurion was not asked whether he could swim. He was asked whether his men could, and he said yes, and they got across somehow, and so did he.

He watched the Lusitanians come up the bank downstream, shaking the water out of their hair. One of them saw the men in the ditch watching and lifted his spear and shouted something in his own language, and laughed, and they went off into the willows.

---

The corn ran out on the fourth day, or what the quartermasters called corn; there had been a little barley in the bottom of the last sacks, and some beans, and that was gone. After that there was what a man had kept back, and what he could buy.

You could buy corn. There were sutlers in the camp, as there always were, Spaniards and Greeks and a family of Syrians from Tarraco, and some of them had sacks, from somewhere. Laberius went down to the sutlers’ lines with Vibius to see. A Greek with a squint and a very clean apron had a sack open on a board and a measure beside it, and a crowd of soldiers in front of the board looking at the measure, and nobody buying.

“How much?” Vibius said.

The Greek told him. It was fifty denarii for the modius.

Vibius did not say anything for a moment. Then he turned round and walked away from the board, and Laberius went after him, and when they were out of the crowd Vibius stopped by a tent rope and said it out loud, slowly, the way he did sums when he wanted Laberius to hear them.

“That’s more than two months’ pay for a modius. When he gets his pay. And a modius is what, four days’ corn for one man, if he doesn’t work? Five? So a man can eat for four days if he works for two months and doesn’t spend a copper on anything else and doesn’t eat while he’s working.” He looked at Laberius. “It’s a good price. If you’re the Greek.”

“Where’s he getting it?”

“Off Afranius’s foragers, I should think. They come down to the water at night and the Greek’s people go across on a raft.” Vibius shrugged. “It’s what I’d do.”

Laberius looked back at the board. A soldier had come up to it, a young man from one of the new cohorts, the levies from Corfinium, and was counting coins out of his belt onto the board, very slowly, one at a time, while the Greek watched him. Laberius knew him. He was the young man from Firmum, with the cut on his chin, healed now. He had his whole belt out on the board. When he had finished counting the Greek looked at the coins and shook his head, and the young man stood there for a while, and then he started putting them back.

“Leave it,” Vibius said.

Laberius left it.

---

The century ate that night because Gaius had a sack. Laberius did not ask where it had come from. It was a small sack and it had millet in it, which Gaius said was what the Gauls fed their horses on and what the Gaulish horse had been feeding the Gaulish horses on before they were cut off on the other side of the water, and he had got it off a groom. Vibius divided it. There was enough for a handful each, boiled up with water in two helmets, and some of it burned, and they ate it standing round the fire in the rain with their fingers.

Laberius took his handful and ate half of it and put the rest in his pouch, when nobody was looking. He would want it in the morning. He had been hungry before, in Gaul, the year of the long winter, and he knew how it went: the first day you were angry, and the second day you were weak, and the third day you stopped being either and started to be stupid. He did not want to be stupid on the third day with the river rising and the Lusitanians across it.

In the morning he went down to the stake.

The water had dropped. Not much; a finger, perhaps, below the last notch. He looked at it for a long time to be sure. Then he cut a new notch, at the new line, and stood up, and saw that the whole lower part of the camp was full of carts.

There were twenty of them, perhaps more, pulled up along the inner side of the ditch where they would not be seen from across the river. On each cart there was a boat. They were not proper boats. They were frames of light wood, a keel and ribs, like the skeleton of a fish, with wicker woven between the ribs and hides stretched over the wicker and sewn, and they were turned upside down on the carts like great black beetles. Men were still working on some of them, stitching hides, and the smell came up the slope, raw leather and tallow. An engineer with a tablet was going from cart to cart.

“What are those?” said Gaius, coming down.

“Boats.”

“Boats.” Gaius looked at them. “The engineer says they’re British. He says the general saw them in Britain and remembered.”

“He remembers everything.”

“He remembers what’s useful.” Gaius looked at Laberius. “They’re going to take them up the river on the carts at night, where Afranius isn’t watching, and go across in them, and build a bridge from the other side. That’s what the engineer says. Twenty miles up.” He paused. “You’ll want to be in the first boat, I suppose. You and your stake.”

“I’ll want to be on the bank,” Laberius said. “Holding the rope.”

Gaius looked at him a moment longer, and then he laughed, and said that was a centurion’s answer if ever he’d heard one, and went off to find out which cohort was going. Laberius went up the slope to the carts. The engineer was having trouble with one of the hides, which had been sewn too tight and was pulling the wicker out of shape, and Laberius stood and watched the man unpick it, and then, because he knew how to sew leather and the engineer’s men plainly did not, he took the awl and showed them.
