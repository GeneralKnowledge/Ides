# The Arbitrators

POV: Hordeonius · Rome, the Forum, by the Janus · December 49

---

The law was posted on a whitened board on the Rostra, and Hordeonius read it three times standing in the crowd, and then went back to his board and sat down and asked Sosia to fetch him a copy so that he could read it again sitting.

“It’s short,” Sosia said, when he came back with it. He had paid a copyist on the Argiletum two sesterces for it and the copyist had done it on a scrap of old tablet in a hurry, but the words were all there. “That’s the first thing. I’ve never seen a law that short.”

“Caesar wrote it.”

“How do you know?”

“Because it’s short.” Hordeonius held the tablet at arm’s length. His eyes had begun to go that year, for small hands, and he did not like it. “Read it to me.”

Sosia read it. It was short. It said that where a debtor could not pay in coin, because coin was not to be had, he might pay his creditor in property instead, in land or houses or goods; and that the property was to be valued not at what it would fetch now, in the present troubles, when nobody would buy, but at what it had been worth before the war began; and that the valuing was to be done by arbitrators, appointed by the praetor, sworn. That was all. It did not cancel any debt. It did not reduce any rate. It did not say a single word against the creditor. It simply said that a house that had been worth three hundred thousand last year was worth three hundred thousand this year, by law, whatever any man in Rome would pay for it, and that a creditor must take it at that.

“Well,” said Sosia, when he had finished. “That’s us done.”

Hordeonius did not answer. He took the tablet back and read it again himself, slowly, holding it far off.

It was beautifully drafted. That was what he kept coming back to. He had read a great many laws on debt in thirty years. He had read the laws of the tribunes who wanted to cancel debts altogether and the laws of the consuls who wanted to stop the tribunes, and they had all been long, and angry, and full of words like *wicked* and *usurer*, and they had all been either thrown out or got round within a month, because a law that is angry is a law that has made somebody angrier, and that somebody has money. This was not angry. It was not anything. It did not punish the creditor and it did not reward the debtor; it simply took the war out of the price. It said, in effect, that the war was not anybody’s fault, or at least not the fault of anybody who owed money, and that nobody should be ruined by it who would not have been ruined anyway. Debtors would be relieved. Creditors would lose, but they would lose quietly, and slowly, one house at a time, and they would not lose all of it, and so they would not go to Greece. It was the kind of law a man writes who wants everybody in the city to be a little unhappy and nobody to be desperate.

“He’s not cancelled anything,” he said.

“He’s cancelled about a third of everything,” said Sosia. “Without saying so.”

“Yes.” Hordeonius put the tablet down. “That is the art of it.”

---

The first of them came on the third day after the posting, a little after the second hour.

He was a dealer in hides from the Velabrum, a big, red, cheerful man called Opimius whom Hordeonius had lent to two years ago for a share in a contract to supply the army in Gaul with leather. The contract had been good. Opimius had done well out of it, and had paid well, until the war, when the contract had passed to somebody else, somebody Caesar knew, and Opimius had found himself with a warehouse of hides by the river and nobody to buy them, and had stopped paying. He was not cheerful this morning. He was embarrassed, and trying to look cheerful, which was worse. With him was a thin grey man in a toga with a wax tablet under his arm and a seal ring on his finger, who stood a little behind him and looked at the board and the money box and Sosia with an expression of complete impartiality.

“This is the praetor’s man,” Opimius said. “An arbitrator. He’s sworn.”

“I know who he is.” Hordeonius stood up. He had known Gnaeus for twenty years; they had sat on juries together. Gnaeus was honest, slow, and impossible to hurry, and the praetor could not have chosen better if he had wanted to annoy every banker in the Forum. “Good morning, Gnaeus.”

“Good morning, Lucius.” Gnaeus did not smile. He was not a smiling man. “Opimius here has a house in the Velabrum, by the Tuscan street. Four floors. Shops on the ground floor, let; rooms above, let. He proposes to make it over to you in settlement of the principal and interest owing, under the law. I have been to see it.” He opened his tablet and turned it round on the board so that Hordeonius could read it. “That is my valuation. As of the year before the war. I have the leases and the rents to support it, and the prices of the two houses on either side, which sold in that year. I am satisfied.”

Hordeonius looked at the figure.

It was a fair figure. That was the worst of it. He knew the house; he knew the street; he knew, as every banker in the Forum knew, what a four-floor house with shops by the Tuscan street had been worth in the year before the war, and Gnaeus’s figure was exactly that, to within a few thousand, which was as near as anyone could get. It was the figure he would have put on it himself, then. It was about a third more than any man in Rome would give for it now, in coin, this winter, if any man in Rome could be found who had the coin.

“It’s a good house,” Opimius said. “The roof’s new.”

“I know it’s a good house.”

“The rents come in. Mostly. The cobbler on the ground floor’s slow, but he pays.”

“I know about the cobbler.” Hordeonius did not look up from the figure. He could feel Opimius beside the board, waiting, and could feel what Opimius was feeling, which was a kind of relief so large and so shameful that the man did not know where to put his hands; he kept moving them, from his belt to the edge of the board and back. Hordeonius had seen that relief before, on the faces of men who had been let off things, at the end of trials, when the verdict came in. He had never seen it at his own board. Men did not get let off at his board. They paid, or they did not, and if they did not, he waited, and then he came for them, slowly and courteously and in the end completely. That was the business.

He looked up at Gnaeus. “And if I decline?”

“Then I write that you declined,” Gnaeus said. “And the debt stands, at the valuation. And Opimius is not in default, from today, because he has offered what the law allows. And you may sue him, and the praetor will send it to me, and I will value the house again, and I will come to the same figure.” He paused. “I would rather not do it twice, Lucius. My knees.”

Sosia made a small noise behind him that might have been a laugh.

Hordeonius took the stylus. He wrote his acceptance on the arbitrator’s tablet, under the figure, in his own hand, and pressed his ring into the wax beside the arbitrator’s seal. Gnaeus looked at the two seals side by side for a moment with what was almost satisfaction, and closed the tablet.

Opimius began to thank him. Hordeonius let him. It went on for some time. Opimius said that he would never forget it, and that Hordeonius had been more than fair, and that the cobbler was really a very good cobbler, and that if there was ever anything; and Hordeonius said that he was glad it had been settled, and that he hoped the hides would sell, and that he was sure they would, when the war was over. Then Opimius went away across the Forum with his hands at last in the right place, behind his back, walking like a man who had put down something heavy. The arbitrator went with him, slowly, favouring one knee.

---

“Well,” said Sosia, when they had gone. “How do I write it?”

Hordeonius sat down. He looked at the place on the board where the arbitrator’s tablet had been. It occurred to him that he now owned a house in the Velabrum with a slow cobbler in it, and that he had never in thirty years owned a house he did not live in, and that he would have to find somebody to collect the rents, and that this somebody would probably have to be Sosia.

“Write *settled*,” he said. “Principal and interest. The date. The house, and the street. And beside it, the figure.”

“Which figure?”

“The arbitrator’s.”

Sosia looked at him. “That’s not what it’s worth.”

“It is what it is worth,” Hordeonius said. “By law. You heard him.” He reached for the cloth and wiped his fingers. “Write *valued*. Not *sold*. Valued. The house is valued at the figure, and the debt is settled at the figure, and there is no loss on the tablet, because there was no sale. When there is a sale, if there is ever a sale, we will see. Until then the house is worth what Gnaeus says. The law says so, and so do I.”

Sosia wrote it. He wrote it carefully, the way Hordeonius liked, with the figure in the right-hand column where it could be added. Then he sat back and looked at it.

“It looks well,” he said.

“It looks very well.”

“And how many more are coming? With arbitrators?”

Hordeonius did not answer that. He had done the sum in the night, lying awake: the names on the tablet who had houses, and the names who had farms, and the names who had nothing but warehouses full of things nobody would buy. He had arrived at a figure. He had not liked the figure, and he had got up and walked about the house in the dark, and the boy who slept across his door had asked him what the matter was, and he had said it was his stomach.

On the Kalends, two days later, Visellius came to the board at the second hour and counted his interest out in stacks of ten, squaring each stack with the side of his thumb. He did not have an arbitrator with him. Hordeonius watched him do it, and did not say anything about the law, and nor did Visellius.
