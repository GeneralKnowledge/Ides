# The Bagradas

POV: Antony · Rome, Curio’s house on the Palatine · September 49

---

The letter said that Curio’s head had gone to the king, and Antony read that line three times and then put the letter down on the table and went out and was sick in the courtyard behind the stables, where nobody would see.

He had been at Tibur when it came, at a villa he had borrowed, with Cytheris and a party of her friends from the theatre and a great deal of wine, and the courier had found him at the third hour of the afternoon lying in the shade by the fishpond with his head in her lap and a garland on that had come down over one eye. He had taken the letter and broken the seal without sitting up. Cytheris had read it over his shoulder. She read better than he did, and faster, and he had felt her go still before he had got to the end of the first sentence.

It was from the man commanding at Lilybaeum, who had it from the ships that had got away. Curio had landed in Africa with two legions and beaten Varus outside Utica, and had been told that King Juba was coming with an army, and then been told that he was not, that it was only a detachment under one of the king’s generals; and he had gone out against the detachment along the river, the Bagradas, by night, with everything he had, to catch it before the king came. And the king had been there. The king had been there with everything he had, too, and the elephants, and the Numidian horse; and Curio’s men had marched all night and had nothing left, and the horse had come round them on the open ground by the river, and that had been that. Curio’s officers had begged him to ride for the camp. He had said that he would not go back to Caesar having lost Caesar’s army, and he had got down off his horse and gone in on foot. They had found the body afterwards. The king had taken the head.

His brother Gaius had surrendered on an island in the Adriatic the same month, with fifteen cohorts, because his fleet had been beaten and there was nothing to eat on the island. That was in the same letter. It came at the bottom, after Curio, as if it were the smaller news, and it was.

He came back from the stables and wiped his mouth and found Cytheris sitting where he had left her, with the letter in her lap, and the party gone quiet all round her.

“I have to go to Rome,” he said.

“I know.”

“Will you come?”

“I’ll go back with the others,” she said. “Tomorrow. Go.”

---

He rode all night and came into Rome at first light by the Tiburtine gate, filthy, with the horse blown, and went straight to the Palatine without going home.

The house had cypress branches on the door. That was all. There was no crowd. There was the porter on his bench, and the creditors’ boys were not at the gate, for once, which told Antony that the news was all over Rome and had been for a day. He went in. The atrium was swept and empty and there was a smell of incense from somewhere, and the steward came out to meet him and took his cloak, and said that the mistress was in the office, and would he wait, or would he go through.

He went through.

She was at the table in the steward’s office, where the household’s accounts were kept, a small square room with shelves of tablets and a window high up in one wall. She had a tablet open in front of her and a stylus in her hand and she was writing. She was dressed in black. Her hair was down, unbound on her shoulders as women wear it for a death, and it made her face look older and also, in a way he could not have described, younger, like a girl’s. She looked up when he came in.

“Marcus,” she said. “You’ve ridden.”

“From Tibur.”

“I can smell it.” She put the stylus down. “Sit down. There’s a stool. The steward will bring you something.”

He sat on the stool. He did not know what to say to her. He had prepared nothing on the road; he had thought about Curio the whole way, about the two of them at nineteen, and the debt, and the father, and the porter, and a night when they had climbed over the wall of a house on the Quirinal for a bet and Curio had torn his toga on the spikes, and he had not thought at all about what he would say when he got here. Now he was here, and she was looking at him, and waiting, and he found he had nothing at all.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“Yes.” She looked at him a moment longer. “So am I.” Then she picked up the stylus again. “You don’t mind if I go on? I’ve got the steward’s people coming at the second hour, and the bankers at the fourth, and I want to know what I’m going to say to them before they say it to me.”

“Go on.”

She went on. He sat on the stool and watched her. She was going through the accounts of the household, tablet by tablet, the ones on the shelves behind her; she would take one down and open it and run the stylus down the columns, quite fast, and make a mark on the tablet in front of her, and close the first and put it back and take down the next. Her hands were very steady. He watched them. They were small hands, with short nails, and there was ink on the side of the right one, along the edge of the little finger, where it had lain on the wax.

The steward came in with wine and water and bread on a tray, and put it down by Antony, and went out again without looking at the mistress. Antony drank the wine. It was the good wine. It had been unpacked.

“Who’s he owe?” he said, after a while.

“Who did he owe.” She did not look up. “Everybody. Again. He’d started again as soon as Caesar paid him off. It’s not much, by his standards. A hundred and twenty thousand, perhaps, here; another sum at Baiae for the house there; some in Sicily I don’t know about yet. The bankers will tell me.” She made a mark. “And then there’s the house. And the boy.”

“The boy?”

“His son. In the nursery. A year old.” She closed the tablet she was working on. “The house was his father’s. It goes to the boy. I’m the boy’s mother and I’m his father’s widow and I have Clodius’s children as well, and his property, what’s left of it after the lawyers had theirs. So the bankers will want to know which pocket they’re to be paid out of, and I’m going to tell them.” She looked up at him. “Is there anything else you’d like to know?”

He had said the wrong thing. He knew it. He had asked about the money because it was the thing he understood, because he had sat on the stool for half an hour watching her hands and had not been able to bear the quiet any longer, and because there was a part of him, quick and practical, that had been adding up Curio’s debts since Tibur without being asked to and wanted to know the figure. He could feel his face go hot.

“I’m sorry,” he said again.

“You keep saying that.” But her voice was different. She put the stylus down and sat back and looked at him, properly, for the first time since he came in. “He’d have asked the same thing,” she said. “If it had been you. He’d have come in and sat on that stool and asked who you owed. And then he’d have offered to stand surety.” She was quiet a moment. “He always did that, you know. For everybody. It was the thing he was best at. Better than speaking.”

“I know. He did it for me.”

“I know he did. I’ve seen the figure.” Something moved at the corner of her mouth. “It’s still in his father’s accounts. Over there. On the third shelf. I could show you.”

Antony laughed. It came out of him the way it had on the steps of Saturn, without asking, and he put his hand over his mouth, and she looked at him with her hair down and her ink-stained hand, and did not laugh, but did not look away either.

---

He stayed until the bankers came.

He had not meant to. He had meant to stay a little, out of decency, and then go home and wash and sleep, and come back in the evening, perhaps, when there would be other people. But when the steward came at the second hour with the steward’s people, the household freedmen with their own tablets and their own questions, she did not tell him to go; and he did not go. He sat on his stool in the corner by the shelves and drank the good wine and listened. She dealt with them one by one. She was not unkind. She was not kind either; she was exact. She knew what each of them had in their tablets before they opened their mouths, and when one of them tried to tell her a sum was larger than it was, she reached behind her without looking and took down the right tablet from the shelf and opened it at the right page and held it out to him.

When the bankers came at the fourth hour, two of them, with their slaves, she stood up to receive them, and Antony stood up too. The bankers looked at him. They knew who he was; everybody did. He was the man who held Italy. He saw them see him there in the corner of a widow’s office, unwashed, with a cup in his hand, and work out what it meant, or what it might mean, and decide to be careful.

Fulvia saw it too. She did not look at him. But she let them see him a moment longer than she needed to, before she sat down and asked them to sit, and began.

He went home at noon. His wife was in the garden reading, under the vine, and she looked up when he came in and saw his face and his clothes and put the book down.

“I heard,” she said. “I’m so sorry. Where have you been?”

“At the house,” he said. “Seeing to things.”

She nodded, and picked up the book again, and he went in to wash.
