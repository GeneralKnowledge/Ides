# Curio’s House

POV: Antony · Rome, Curio’s house on the Palatine · May 49

---

The creditors’ boys were at the gate before Antony was, three of them, sitting on the kerb with their tablets on their knees, and they stood up when they saw him and then sat down again when they saw he was not Curio.

He knew one of them. He knew most of the creditors’ boys in Rome, in the way a man knows the dogs in his own street. This one was a skinny Syrian with a squint who belonged to a banker on the Argiletum, and who had sat on Antony’s own step for a week the winter before last, every morning, politely, until Antony had paid him to go away with money he had borrowed from somebody else. The boy recognised him and gave him a small, professional nod, as one man of business to another. Antony nodded back.

“He’s going today,” the boy said. “Sicily.”

“I know. That’s why I’m here.”

“They say he’ll be back with the grain.” The boy looked down at his tablet. “Before the Kalends?”

“Ask him,” Antony said, and went in.

---

Curio’s house was full of people packing. It was a big house, too big for him, one of the old houses on the slope of the Palatine above the Forum, and it had been his father’s, and his father had hated Antony and had once had him thrown out of it by the porter in front of the whole street. Antony thought about that every time he came in at the door. He thought about it now, with a sort of affection. The old man had been right, as it turned out. Antony had been eighteen and Curio nineteen and between them they had run up a debt that had made the old man take to his bed, and Curio had stood surety for Antony’s share of it, which was the larger share, and the old man had found out. Six million. Antony could still say the figure without thinking. He was not sure he had ever paid Curio back. He had a feeling that in the end somebody else had paid it, and that it might have been Caesar, by way of Curio, by way of the debts Caesar had paid for Curio last year, all of them, every one, so that Curio had stood up in the Senate as tribune a free man and voted for whatever Caesar wanted. Everybody in Rome knew how much that had been, to the sesterce. The creditors’ boys had known it before Curio did.

So in a sense, Antony thought, picking his way through the atrium between chests and rolled carpets and a slave carrying a bronze lampstand like a spear, he had paid himself. That was a comfortable thought, and he kept it.

Curio was not in the atrium. A woman was.

She was standing in the middle of the packing with a tablet in one hand and a ring of keys in the other, saying where things were to go. Not loudly. She did not need to. She said *the blue chest to the cart, not the other one, he’ll want the other one in Sicily*, and the slave with the blue chest changed direction without a word; she said *those are mine, put them back*, and two boys carrying a basket of silver cups stopped and put them back; she said to the steward, who was standing beside her looking unhappy, *the Syrian at the gate is to be told the Kalends, and the other two are to be told the Ides, and if they argue, send them to me*. Then she turned round, because she had seen Antony come in, and looked at him.

He had met Fulvia before. Of course he had; everybody had. She had been Clodius’s wife, and everybody in Rome had seen her the night Clodius was carried home from the Appian Way with his wounds in him, when she had stood over the body in the atrium of his house and shown it to the crowd, the wounds one by one, until the crowd went out and burned down the Senate house. Antony had not been there. He had been in Gaul. He had heard it told so many times that he thought he could see it, the woman and the body and the torches. He had met her twice since she married Curio, at dinner, where she had lain on the couch beside her husband and said very little and watched who drank too much. He had been one of them, both times.

“Marcus Antonius,” she said. “He’s in the garden. He’s saying goodbye to the dog.”

“The dog’s not going?”

“The dog is sick on ships.” She held the tablet against her chest. “You’ll be wanting wine. There isn’t any. It’s packed. It was packed this morning, because if I had left it until this afternoon it would have been drunk.” She said this without any expression at all, looking straight at him. “There’s water.”

“Water’s fine.”

She looked at him a moment longer, and then she turned and said something to a girl standing by the door to the garden, and the girl went away, and Fulvia went back to the tablet. Antony stood there. He did not know why he did not go on into the garden. He watched her. She had a way of holding the keys, he noticed, not like a housekeeper, who holds them so they will not jingle, but loosely, letting them hang, so that every time she moved her hand they made a small sound, and every slave in the room heard it and knew where she was.

A small girl came out from behind a chest, perhaps seven years old, dark, with her mother’s eyes, and stood looking up at Antony with great seriousness.

“Claudia,” Fulvia said, without looking round. “Go to Nurse.”

The girl went. Antony watched her go.

“Clodius’s,” Fulvia said, still without looking up. “In case you were counting.”

He laughed. He had not expected to. She glanced at him then, quickly, and something at the corner of her mouth moved and then did not, and the girl came back with the water.

---

Curio was in the garden with the dog’s head in his lap, sitting on the edge of the fishpond, in his travelling cloak already, with his boots on.

“Marcus.” He did not get up. He was a thin, quick, untidy man with a face like a clever boy’s, and he had been the best speaker of their year by a long way and had never once let Antony forget it. “You’ve come to see me off. Or to borrow money.”

“You haven’t any.”

“I’ve four legions and a fleet. That’s the same as money, nearly.” He pulled the dog’s ears. The dog sighed. “Sicily. Cato’s in Sicily. He’s been building ships. I’m told he’s going to hold it to the last man, and make a speech about it.”

“He won’t.”

“No. He’ll make the speech and leave. I’ll be there in a fortnight and he’ll be in Greece, and I’ll have Sicily without a stone thrown, and then Caesar wants Africa.” Curio looked up. “Africa’s harder. There’s Varus in Africa, with two legions, and there’s that king.”

“Juba.”

“Juba. Who hates me.” He said it with a kind of pleasure. “I once proposed a law to take his kingdom away. As tribune. I’ve forgotten why. I expect I was being paid.” He stood up, and the dog stood up with him, leaning against his legs. “Will you look in on the house?”

“Of course.”

“Not on the house,” Curio said. “On Fulvia. She doesn’t need it. But I’d like to think someone was. And she likes you.”

“She told me there was no wine.”

“That’s how you know.” Curio grinned. He took Antony’s arm and they walked back towards the house, with the dog between them, slowly. “Italy,” he said. “You’ve got Italy. You and Lepidus. What are you going to do with it?”

“Hold it.”

“Is that all?”

“It’s what I was told.”

Curio looked at him sideways, the way he had looked at him across a dining couch at nineteen, when Antony had suggested something and Curio had already worked out what it would cost. “Then hold it,” he said. “Don’t spend it.”

---

They went out to the street together, and the cart was loaded and the mules were in, and Fulvia was at the door with the keys and the tablet, and the girl beside her holding her skirt. Curio kissed his wife, quickly, and the child, and the dog, and got up on his horse. The creditors’ boys stood up on the kerb.

“The Kalends,” Curio said to the Syrian, from the saddle, cheerfully. “Or the Ides. Ask my wife.” And he rode off down the hill towards the Forum with his escort behind him, without looking back, because he never did.

Antony stood on the step. He found that he did not want to leave. It was a fine house. It was the kind of house a man came home to, with a woman in it who knew where everything was and who could send a creditor away to the Ides with one sentence and make him go. He did not have a house like that. He had a house under the Esquiline that was not big enough and that he had borrowed against twice, and a wife who was his cousin and who had been a sensible match, and who read in the afternoons, and who would be wondering where he was.

He thought: Curio had all this, and it had cost Caesar sixty million, and it had been cheap.

“You’ll want to go,” Fulvia said behind him.

He turned. She was looking at him, with the keys hanging from her hand.

“I’ll look in,” he said. “He asked me.”

“I know he did. I heard him.” She considered him. “Come on a day when the wine’s unpacked,” she said, and went in, and the porter shut the door.
