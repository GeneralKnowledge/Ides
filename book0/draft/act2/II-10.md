# Eleven Days

POV: Nysa · Rome, the chief priest’s house on the Sacred Way · December 49

---

The steward said to double the lamps, and so for eleven days Nysa did nothing else.

She had never trimmed so many. The house had lamps in every room, of course, but in the master’s absence most of them had stood on their shelves with dry wicks for a year, because the mistress did not use the big rooms and oil was oil. Now they all came down. They came down from the dining room and the atrium and the reception rooms on either side of the atrium, and from the long room at the back where the master worked when he was at home, which had more lamps in it than any room Nysa had ever seen, standing lamps and hanging lamps and lamps on brackets on the walls, because the master worked at night. They came down to the bench by the side door in baskets, and she cleaned them and trimmed them and filled them, and they went back up, and in the evening the whole house was lit as if for a wedding, and in the morning they came down again.

“Every night?” Chreste said, on the first day.

“Every night. The steward said.”

“What does he need them all for?”

“He reads,” said Phileros, coming past with the keys. He did not stop. “He reads and he writes and he has people in, and he does it till the second watch, and he can’t see in the dark any better than you can. Fill them.”

The oil came from the storeroom. Nysa had known it would. She had counted the jars there since the summer, the rows of them in the passage with straw between, and she had wondered what all that oil was for, and now she knew. She did the sum in her head the first evening, the number of lamps by the number of nights by what each lamp took. It came to more than she would have thought. It came to three of the big jars, and a little of a fourth.

---

He had come in on the evening of the first day, in a carriage, with horsemen, and she had not seen him. She had been in the storeroom. She had heard the noise, the porter’s shout and the gate and the horses on the stones and a great many voices at once in the atrium, and then the voices going away into the house, and then the steward’s step going fast along the passage outside the storeroom, and his voice saying *the lamps, the lamps, where are the lamps*, and she had come out with a basket.

She saw him on the third day.

She was carrying water. The mistress had asked for it, hot, in the small room off the garden, and Nysa was carrying it there along the colonnade in the big bronze jug with a cloth round the handle, walking carefully because it was full; and the door of the long room at the back was open, and as she came past it a man came out of it, quickly, reading, and nearly walked into her.

He stopped. She stopped. The water rocked in the jug and some of it went over the lip and onto the stone between them, steaming.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

She looked at his feet first. She always did. He had on house sandals, plain ones, old, with a strap that had been mended at the heel by somebody who knew how. His feet were thin and brown and the toes were long. Then she looked up, because he had not moved, and he was looking at her.

He was older than she had thought. She did not know what she had thought. She had heard him described a hundred times, by the porter and the cook and the men who came to the side door, and he was not like any of it; he was thinner, and older, and his hair was going at the front, and he had a way of looking that was very quick and complete, as if he had read her the way he had been reading the tablet in his hand.

“What’s your name?” he said.

“Nysa.”

“Nysa.” He looked at the jug. “Is that for my wife?”

“Yes, master.”

“Then you’d better take it to her before it’s cold.” He stood aside, and then, as she went past him, he said, “Nysa. Where are you from?”

She stopped again. She did not know what to say. Nobody had asked her that since she came into the house.

“From here, master,” she said.

He looked at her for a moment as if that were an interesting answer. Then somebody called him from inside the long room, a man’s voice, about a letter, and he said “Coming,” and went back in, and the door closed.

She took the water to the mistress. The mistress was sitting by the window with her hands in her lap, doing nothing, and she looked at the jug and at Nysa and said, “You’ve spilled some,” and Nysa said yes, lady, in the colonnade, and the mistress said, “Did you see him?” and Nysa said yes, lady. The mistress did not ask anything else. But she looked at Nysa for a moment longer than she needed to, and something in her face that had been held very still all morning came loose a little, and she said, “Go on, then,” quite gently.

---

On the fifth day he passed her again, in the atrium, with three men in togas who were all talking at once, and he looked at her and said, “Good morning,” and then, as if remembering, “Your name?”

“Nysa, master.”

“Nysa,” he said, and went on.

She stood in the atrium with her basket of lamps and felt her face go hot. She did not know why. He had asked her name. He had asked it twice. He was the master of the house and the consul for next year and the dictator of Rome, the steward said, and the greatest general in the world, and he had asked her name twice, in two days, and that meant he had remembered her, and that he had wanted to know it again. She carried the basket to the bench by the side door and sat down with it on her knees and did not start on the wicks for some time.

She did not tell Chreste. She thought about telling her. But Chreste would laugh, and would want to know what he had said, exactly, and how he had looked, and would tell the cook, and the cook would tell the girls in the wool room, and by the evening it would be all over the house that Nysa had been noticed. And she did not want it to be all over the house. She wanted to keep it.

On the seventh day, in the colonnade, he passed her and did not look at her at all. He was reading.

---

On the ninth day the family came to dinner.

It was not a large dinner, the steward said; only family. But every lamp in the dining room was lit and the good silver was out, and the cook had been up since before dawn, and the porter had a new tunic. Nysa was not in the dining room. She was in the passage outside it, by the serving hatch, with the other girls, handing in the lamps when they needed filling and taking out the ones that were smoking, and from there she could see the couches through the hatch, if she bent, and the people on them.

She did not know most of them. There was the master, on the middle couch, and the mistress beside him. There was the mistress’s father, whom she knew by sight, an old man with a heavy face who laughed a great deal. There were two or three other old men and their wives. There was a handsome woman in a dark blue dress with a great deal of gold at her throat, who talked to the master across the table in a low voice and made him laugh, and beside her a stout pleasant man who was her husband and who did not talk much at all. The cook’s girl said she was the master’s niece, the daughter of his sister who had died. Her husband was a consular. They were very rich.

And on the end of the lowest couch, by himself, there was a boy.

He was thin, and about fourteen, and he sat up very straight with his hands in his lap and did not eat much. Nobody talked to him. Once his mother leaned across and said something to him, and he answered, and she turned back to the master. Once the old man with the heavy face looked at him and said something to the table, and they all laughed, and the boy smiled politely, a beat late, as if he had had to work out what the joke was first. Nysa watched him for a while because he was the only person at the table who was not talking. When the dinner was over and the guests stood up to go, and the slaves went in with their shoes, she saw that his were outdoor shoes, good ones, but too thin for the season, with the soles worn almost through at the ball of the foot. She thought somebody ought to tell his mother. Then she thought that his mother had a great deal of gold at her throat and must know.

The family went out into the atrium with the lamps going ahead of them, and the boy went last, behind his mother, and nobody looked back at him, and Nysa did not either. She was counting the lamps that had come out of the dining room, to see that none were missing.

---

On the eleventh day the master went away again, before light, to Brundisium and the sea. The steward said he was going to cross to Greece in the winter, which nobody did, and that the army was waiting for him at the harbour.

She heard him go from her pallet: the horses in the street, and the voices, and the gate. Then the house was quiet. In the morning the steward said that the lamps could go back to how they were. So she spent the day taking them down from the big rooms, the dining room and the atrium and the long room at the back, and cleaning them, and putting them away on their shelves with dry wicks.

The long room was very untidy. There were tablets everywhere, on the tables and the floor and the couches, and a cloak someone had forgotten, and an empty jug, and on the floor by the window a pair of old house sandals, with a strap mended at the heel. She picked them up. She did not know what to do with them. In the end she gave them to the steward, who looked at them, and at her, and put them in a chest.

That evening she did the sum again, the oil. It had come to three and a half of the big jars, in eleven days. She had been a little out.
