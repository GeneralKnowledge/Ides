## The book in one paragraph

Hannibal brings an army over the Alps and destroys three Roman armies in twenty months, the last at Cannae, the worst day in Rome's history. Then nothing he expected happens. Rome does not ask for terms, most of its allies do not leave, Carthage does not send what he needs, and the war turns into eleven years of sieges, defections and slow attrition. The book is about the gap between winning and using a win, seen from every camp that has to live in that gap. It ends when Hannibal is shown his brother's head and understands that the war in Italy is lost.

## Engine and shape

- **The question the book turns on:** why did Hannibal not march on Rome after Cannae? Maharbal's line (Livy 22.51) is the hinge. Every POV has its own answer, and the book never settles it with a narrator's verdict.
- **Central event:** Cannae, at about 30% of the book (about 28,000–32,000 words in). Ides sat at about 31% of Book 1.
- **Opening:** the descent from the Alps into Italy, late 218. The siege of Saguntum and the embassy to Carthage are memory, not scenes.
- **Ending image:** Hasdrubal's head thrown before Hannibal's outposts after the Metaurus (207). The last line belongs to whoever sees it, not to Hannibal's thoughts.
- **Mirror with Book 1:** Ides was a killing that worked and an aftermath that failed. Cannae is a victory that worked and an aftermath that failed. This is never said on the page.

## POV roster (eight, all real but one)

| POV | Side | Arc in one line |
|---|---|---|
| C. Terentius Varro | Rome | The man blamed for Cannae who is thanked for not despairing. |
| Q. Fabius Maximus | Rome | The delayer: mocked, overruled, then proved right, and not made kinder by it. |
| P. Cornelius Scipio (the young Scipio) | Rome | From a boy at the Ticinus to the commander Spain hails as king. |
| Maharbal | Hannibal's army | The cavalryman who knows how to use a win and is not listened to. |
| Hanno the Great | Carthage | The peace party at home; right about the cost, wrong about Rome. |
| Silenus of Caleacte | Hannibal's camp | A Greek historian writing the war while it happens, from beside the general. |
| Pacuvius Calavius | Capua | Takes his city over to Hannibal, and has to live with the choice. |
| One soldier of the Cannae legions (invented) | Rome, then Sicily | Survives Cannae, is exiled to Sicily in disgrace, and fights at Syracuse. |

No more than eight. Hannibal has no POV. The record goes silent on Maharbal and Pacuvius Calavius after 216, so their later scenes are invention and must stay small (see the character sheets).

## Rules carried over from Book 1

- Close third, past tense, no epigraphs, no "little did he know".
- No narrator explainer sentences ("X was the …"). Reminders in dialogue are allowed where the listener does not know, or the fact is leverage; briefings are banned (Book 1 `writing/STYLE_GLOSSARY.md`, exposition rule as relaxed).
- Real people first. Invent a character only where the record is empty, and give each invented character a job no real person could do. Book 2 starts with one invented POV.
- UK spelling; ’s possessives (curly apostrophe); no em dashes in scenic prose; no modern weekdays.
- Book 1 habits A–I (`writing/HUMAN_VOICE_RULES.md` §2) are avoided while drafting, not fixed afterwards. Habit I (caption fragments) and the "filed" habit are the ones that cost most.
- One DOCX per stamped copy. No EPUB, audiobook or print until freeze.

## Rules not carried over

No locks or frozen chapters, no POV percentage quotas, no length floors, no overhear requirement, no per-chapter pass logs. A rule stays only if a script or a reader can check it.

## Protected beats

Named beats, not chapters. They change only for a defect shown under the Book 1 evidence rule.

1. Cannae is fought, and lost, on the day Varro commands.
2. Maharbal's line after Cannae, and Hannibal not marching on Rome. Why stays disputed.
3. The Senate refuses to ransom the Cannae prisoners and thanks Varro.
4. Capua goes over to Hannibal.
5. Hasdrubal's head thrown before Hannibal's outposts.

## Tone in three lines

1. Hard, plain, physical: weather, mud, mules, hunger, wounds and money.
2. Every camp is intelligent and every camp is partly wrong.
3. Humour comes from people who do not know they are funny; none at Cannae or the head.
