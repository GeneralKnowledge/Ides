# Placentia

**I-04b** · POV: Scipio · Placentia, the night after the Trebia, midwinter 218 BC
**Sources:** the wounded consul at Placentia and the survivors of the Trebia coming in (Pol. 3.66, 3.74): to check. The Gauls of the plain going over to Hannibal: to check. Sempronius’s letter calling it a victory spoiled by weather (Pol. 3.75): to check. Everything else said in the room is invention inside the record.

---

They began to come in a little after dark.

Scipio was on the wall above the gate when the first of them arrived, because his father had sent him there, and because there was nothing for him to do in the house where his father lay but sit by the bed and watch him not sleep. The snow had stopped at dusk. The road from the west ran pale between the black fields, and down it, out of the dark, came men walking.

Not running. He would remember that. He had expected, from the stories, that a beaten army would come in running, with the enemy at its heels. These came walking in a column, close together, with their shields still on their arms, and their centurions walking beside them, and when they reached the gate a centurion with blood in his beard called up to the wall to say who they were, in a hoarse flat voice, as if he were reporting the state of the latrines.

Somebody beside Scipio called down to ask where the rest of the army was. The centurion did not answer.

They kept coming most of the night. The column first, thousands of them, the ones who had broken through the middle of Hannibal’s line and found nobody to stop them on the far side. Then, later, others in ones and twos and little knots, from the river, soaked through and shaking, some of them without their weapons, some carried by their friends. Their story was different and it came in pieces. The elephants. Men behind them out of nowhere, out of a ditch. The river at their backs. Men drowning in it who had got that far. Scipio went down from the wall and helped at the gate, holding a torch while the town’s magistrates and an officer of his father’s tried to count them, and he listened to the pieces and tried to put them together and could not.

Near midnight a slave came to fetch him. His father was asking for him.

The consul lay in the best room of the house of a man who sold wine to the garrison, with a brazier at the foot of the bed and his wound tied up under the blanket. He had been grey since the fight by the Ticinus and he was grey now, but his eyes were quite clear, and he had made them prop him up against the wall with cushions. The other consul had been with him. Scipio saw him leaving as he came in, a broad, heavy man with his helmet under his arm and his cloak wet to the waist, and the two of them stood aside for each other in the doorway, awkwardly, and Sempronius went out without saying anything.

“Sit down,” said his father.

He sat on the stool by the bed.

“You heard?”

“Some of it.”

“Tell me what you heard.”

So he told it, in pieces, the way it had come to him at the gate. The Numidians at the camp at dawn. The cavalry going after them, and the light troops, and then the whole army over the river without eating. The snow. The elephants on the wings. The men in the ditch. The centre breaking through and coming here, because it could not go back. His father listened with his eyes closed. When Scipio stopped, he was quiet for a long time.

“I told him,” the consul said at last. “Yesterday, in this room. I told him to wait. The Gauls are with Hannibal now, after the Ticinus, every village between here and the mountains, but they are Gauls. Keep him sitting in one place all winter and feed him nothing, and by spring they would have been eating his mules and stealing his horses and looking round for someone else to be friends with. I told him that.”

“Why didn’t he wait?”

His father opened his eyes and looked at him with something like amusement.

“His year is nearly over,” he said. “There will be new consuls at the Ides of March, and one of them will come here and fight Hannibal in the spring, and if he wins it will be his triumph and not Sempronius’s. And I am lying here with a hole in me, and if Sempronius waits until I am well, half of whatever he wins will be mine.” He shifted on the cushions and his face changed and he waited for it to pass. “He is not a fool. He is a man with a year that is running out. Remember that, when you have one.”

Scipio sat with that.

“Hannibal knew,” he said slowly. “That he would come out. That’s why the Numidians. He knew he would come out because he wanted to.”

“Yes.”

“How could he know that?”

“Because he asks,” his father said. “Every deserter, every Gaul who has sold eggs in our camp, every prisoner. He knew Sempronius’s temper before Sempronius had unpacked. We go into a fight knowing his numbers. He goes into a fight knowing our consuls.”

Outside, more men were coming in at the gate; Scipio could hear the voices, the challenges, the gate creaking. The brazier ticked. His father’s eyes had closed again.

“They are saying in the streets it was a victory,” the consul said, without opening them. “Did you hear that? The weather was against us, but our men broke through the enemy line. Sempronius has written that to the Senate tonight. I watched him write it.”

“It isn’t true.”

“Every word of it is true.” His father smiled, very faintly. “That is what is wrong with it.”

Scipio stayed until his father slept, which was not until the grey began at the window. Then he went out and walked through the town in the first light. The streets were full of men asleep, in doorways, in the porticoes, in the open on the cobbles under their cloaks. He stepped over them. At the end of one street he found a young soldier sitting against a wall with a piece of bread in his hand, laughing at something by himself, and a big man beside him asking what was funny. He walked on before he could hear the answer.
