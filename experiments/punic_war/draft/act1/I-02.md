# The Troop

**I-02** · POV: Scipio · near the Po, by the Ticinus, November 218 BC
**Sources:** Pol. 10.3.2–6 (the son, aged seventeen, given a picked troop; charges alone when they hang back; his father hails him as saviour; Laelius the source): text. Liv. 21.46 (Coelius's version, a Ligurian slave): text. The cavalry fight's numbers and order: to check.

---

His father had given him the troop the night before, in the tent, in front of the other officers, so that it would look like an honour and not like a nursemaid.

“Thirty of the best,” the consul said. “You stay with me. Where I go, you go, and they go with you.” He said it lightly, with the cup in his hand, and the officers smiled, because they had all been seventeen once and had all had fathers. Scipio smiled too. He understood perfectly what had been done. Thirty picked horsemen, veterans, men who had served in Sicily and Liguria and would not let a consul’s son get himself killed in his first fight, whatever the consul’s son thought he was doing. He thanked his father and drank, and went out into the dark, and stood among the horse lines with his face hot.

Laelius found him there. Gaius Laelius was a few years older and had been his friend since they were small enough to steal figs together from the same tree, and he had a way of arriving when he was not wanted that was very nearly a gift.

“Thirty,” said Laelius. “I heard. That’s a lot of men to stop you doing anything stupid.”

“It’s an honour.”

“Of course it is.”

They rode out at first light, across the bridge the army had built over the Ticinus, the consul and his cavalry and the light infantry running alongside, to see what Hannibal was doing. That was all it was meant to be: a look. Everybody said so. Scipio rode at his father’s left with his thirty behind him and Laelius among them, and the country was flat and wet and grey, with mist lying on the fields and the poplars standing out of it in rows, and he kept looking for the enemy in the mist and seeing trees.

When the enemy came it was not out of the mist but out of the light, a long line of horsemen on the rising ground ahead with the low sun behind them, so that they were black and he could not count them. Then there was dust where there should not have been dust on wet ground, and he understood that it was a great many horses, moving.

His father gave his orders without any change in his voice. The javelin men in front. The cavalry behind them. Scipio heard each order go out and thought, quite clearly, that this was how it was done, and that he would remember it, and then the javelin men threw once and turned and ran back through the gaps in the cavalry, all of them, without waiting, and the two lines of horse were in among each other, and he could not see his father.

He had imagined a battle as a field with order on it. This was a crowd of horses going round and round in their own dust. Men were fighting on foot among the hooves, men who had been knocked off or had got down to fight, and others were still mounted, cutting down at them. He saw a man’s face open like a door. He had his sword out and did not remember drawing it, and his horse was turning and he was letting it turn, and the thirty were round him in a ring, exactly as promised, shoving away anyone who came near, and he could not see his father anywhere.

Then the ring opened for a moment and he could.

His father was on the far side of the press, perhaps a hundred paces off, with only two or three men left around him. He was still on his horse, but bent over its neck in a way that was wrong, and there was blood on the horse’s shoulder that had come from somewhere above it. And around the far side, where the Roman line should have been, came horsemen who rode without bridles, small men on small horses, sitting them as if they had grown out of their backs. They were in behind everything, and they were heading for the place where the consul was.

Scipio shouted. He shouted to the thirty to go, to go now, and pointed with the sword, and the thirty looked where he pointed and looked at each other and did not go. He saw the decurion’s face. It was not cowardice. They had been told what to do, and what they had been told was to keep the boy alive, and the boy was asking them to ride him into the middle of the Numidians.

So he went by himself.

He did not decide to. He found that his heels were in his horse’s sides and the horse was going, and the noise was going past him, and the thirty were behind him somewhere, shouting. Then they were not behind him but beside him, because a troop of picked men cannot let a seventeen-year-old ride alone at the enemy in front of half the army and live with it afterwards. He felt them come up on either side of him like a wave lifting a boat. The Numidians saw them coming and swerved, as he would learn later Numidians always did when something heavy came at them. They were there to kill and not to die, and there were easier men to kill.

He reached his father. He did not remember the last part. He remembered his father’s face turned towards him, grey, with a look on it that he had never seen there and did not understand until much later, when he had sons of his own. Then there were Roman horsemen all round them, and they were going back, the whole lot of them, back towards the bridge, with his father held on his horse between two troopers and Scipio riding at his knee.

That night in the camp his father called the officers together, lying on his bed with his wound bound up and his face still grey. He said in front of all of them that his son had saved his life, and he called him by a name that meant something like the one who brings safety, before them all. Scipio stood and heard it and felt the officers looking at him. He could not tell whether the heat in his face was pride or something else.

Afterwards, outside, there was talk round the fires. There always was. He walked past one fire where some of the Ligurian auxiliaries were sitting, and one of them, an older man with a broken nose, was telling it differently. In his version it was a slave who had done it, a Ligurian like himself, a groom who had gone in after the consul on foot and dragged him out, and the boy had come up afterwards on his fine horse and taken the credit. The men round the fire were laughing.

Scipio stopped in the dark beyond the firelight and listened to the whole story. Then he went on.

Laelius had been walking with him. “Do you want me to say something?”

“No,” said Scipio. “Let them have it. It’s a better story with a slave in it.”

He thought about that for a long time afterwards, lying awake in the tent beside his father’s: that there would be two stories now and that people would choose. He found that he did not mind as much as he would have expected. He knew which one was true. He was beginning to understand that this was not the same as which one people believed, and that the second could be worked on.
