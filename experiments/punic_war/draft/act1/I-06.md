# The Voting Pens

**I-06** · POV: Varro · Rome, the elections for 217 BC

Varro could tell how a vote would go by the smell of the crowd. That was not a thing he could have explained to a senator, and he never tried.

It was a matter of how long they had been standing, and what they had eaten, and whether the wind came off the river or the hills. A crowd that had stood all morning on the Field of Mars in a cold wind with nothing in its belly but sour wine smelled of wet wool and old onions and impatience. It wanted something to happen. It would vote for the man who looked most like something happening.

This crowd had stood all morning. It smelled of wet wool and old onions and fear.

He had been praetor that year, which meant he had been in Rome, in the city court, hearing lawsuits about boundary stones and runaway slaves while the war went on in the north. That was the joke against him in the Senate, if they bothered to make one: Varro had sat out the Trebia judging a quarrel over a dead pig. It was true, as it happened, except that the pig had been a cow. He had heard about the Trebia the same way the rest of the city had. First the consul’s letter, read out in the Senate, with its brave weather and its line that broke through; and then, a few days behind it, the men themselves. Wounded men sent home, and men on leave with their mouths full of what had happened, and men’s families going down to the gates to meet them and finding out who was not coming.

The letter had said one thing. The city had worked out another. Now the city was voting, and the Senate was frightened, and Varro was enjoying himself more than he would have admitted to anyone.

He stood with the other magistrates near the tribunal, where the voting pens had been roped out across the field. He was in his purple-bordered toga, keeping his face grave, and watched the centuries file through the gangways to drop their tablets. The first class had voted. The knights had voted. Now the long middle of the people was going through, the small farmers and the tradesmen, the men whose sons were in the legions, and he could see from their faces what they would write before they wrote it.

The name they wrote was Flaminius, over and over.

He had nothing to do with it. He wished he had. Gaius Flaminius had been consul six years ago and had beaten the Insubrian Gauls. As tribune, long before that, he had given the land in Picenum to the poor against the wishes of the whole Senate, and as censor he had built a road and a circus and had them named after him. The nobles hated him the way they hated a bad smell they could not find the source of. He was everything Varro would have liked to be and was not yet. And he was being elected consul a second time, by a frightened people, because he had once beaten an enemy and because he did not look as if he would sit in his camp and write letters about the weather.

“They’ll regret this,” said the man beside him.

It was a young Aemilius, or a Cornelius, or one of those; Varro could never keep them apart, and suspected they could not either. He was watching the voting with his lips pressed together.

“Who will?” Varro said.

“The people. Flaminius will march straight at Hannibal and get himself killed and the army with him. He has no patience. He never had.”

“The people have had patience,” said Varro. “They had patience in the autumn, when we were told the Ticinus was only a skirmish. They had patience in the winter, when we were told the Trebia was nearly a victory. Now they are out of patience and they are voting for a man who isn’t.” He kept his voice pleasant. “I would call that clear thinking, myself.”

The young noble looked at him. Varro saw him notice, as they always noticed sooner or later, the face that did not come from any family with masks in its hall, and the hands, which were a tradesman’s hands, broad and square, though he had not cut meat with them since he was fourteen.

“You would,” said the young man, and moved away.

Varro let him go. He was used to it. He had been used to it since the year he first stood for quaestor and an ex-consul had asked, quite loudly, in the Forum, whether the butcher’s boy meant to weigh out the treasury by the pound. He had got the quaestorship anyway. He had got the aedileship after it, and given games that people still talked about, and he had got the praetorship, all on the votes of the men filing through these pens now. Every time, the nobles had been surprised. That was the thing he had learned about them, more than anything: they were always surprised. They thought the city was a house they had inherited, and they kept being astonished to find other people living in it.

The result was read out in the middle of the afternoon. Flaminius and Servilius. The crowd made a noise that was not quite a cheer, more a kind of release, like men setting down a heavy load. Some of them were already going home.

Varro walked back into the city with a few of the men who managed votes for him in the tribes: a fuller, a retired centurion, a man who owned three bakeries on the Aventine and owed his licence to Varro’s court. They talked about the result in the comfortable way of men who had not been responsible for it. The baker thought Flaminius would end the war by midsummer. The centurion said nothing at all.

At the gate Varro stopped to let a cart through, and found himself looking at a woman sitting on the step of the shrine by the gate with her hands in her lap. She was not young. She had the look of a farmer’s wife up from the country, in a brown cloak, and she was watching the road from the north, and he understood after a moment that she had been sitting there for some time and expected to sit there a good deal longer.

He thought of saying something to her and could not think what. Then the cart was through, and the baker was talking about bread prices, and he walked on.

That night he wrote a list. It was the habit of years: the names of the men in each tribe who would vote the way he asked, and the names of the men who might, and what each of them wanted. He wrote it carefully, and then sat looking at it, and then, at the bottom, under the bakers and the fullers and the centurions, he wrote *Flaminius*, and a question mark, because one consul in a hurry was a beginning, but it was not the same as two.
