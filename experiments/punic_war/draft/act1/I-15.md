# Consul

**I-15** · POV: Varro · Rome, the elections for 216 BC
**Sources:** Varro and L. Aemilius Paullus consuls for 216 (Pol. 3.106; Liv. 22.35): to check. Varro elected first and alone, Paullus the next day as the nobles' man (Liv. 22.35): to check. Eight legions, about eighty thousand men, more than Rome had ever put in one army (Pol. 3.107, 3.113): to check. The scene is invention inside that record.

---

He was elected alone. That was the part he would remember.

The rules said two consuls, and the people voted for two, and the two who got a majority of the centuries were declared. But that year, when the votes were counted on the Field of Mars at the end of the first day, only one man had a majority. The nobles had split their votes between three candidates of their own, a Cornelius and a Manlius and an Atilius, and fought each other to a standstill, and the people had not split at all. So at sunset the presiding magistrate stood up on the tribunal and read out one name, and it was his, and for one night, until they voted again in the morning, Gaius Terentius Varro was the only consul-elect of the Roman people.

He walked home through the city in the dark with a crowd behind him. It was not a big crowd; most people had gone home to eat. It was the baker and the fuller and the old centurion and fifty or sixty others, men from the tribes, carrying torches, singing a song about him that someone had made up that afternoon. It was not a good song. It rhymed his name with something rude about the Senate. He laughed at it all the way up the Esquiline.

At the door of his house he turned and thanked them, and they cheered, and he went in.

His wife was waiting in the atrium. She had been sitting up with a lamp, and she got up when he came in and looked at him, and he saw from her face that she had heard.

“Consul,” she said.

“Consul.”

She had married him when he was a quaestor with a lawsuit hanging over him, and she had kept his house through the aedileship and the games, when they had borrowed more money than he ever told her about. She had never once asked him what he was for. He had sometimes wondered if she knew. She came across the atrium now and put her hands on his arms, and he thought she was going to say she was proud of him, and she said instead, “What happens now?”

He told her what happens now. Tomorrow the people would elect a colleague, and the nobles would put up the man they hated least and vote for him as one man, and he would get in. That would be Paullus, probably. Lucius Aemilius Paullus, who had been consul once before, against the Illyrians. Afterwards he had been prosecuted along with his colleague for keeping back plunder, and his colleague had been condemned, and Paullus had got off by the skin of his teeth and had hated the people ever since. Paullus would be the nobles’ consul and Varro would be the people’s. Then, in the spring, the two of them would go out together with the biggest army Rome had ever sent into the field and find Hannibal and fight him.

“Together,” she said.

“Together.”

“And if he doesn’t want to fight? Paullus?”

Varro was quiet for a moment. He had thought about this. He had thought about little else for a month.

“Then he will have to explain to the people why,” he said. “As Fabius did.”

She looked at him a while longer. Then she said she would have the cook heat something for him, and went away, and he stood alone in his own atrium, under the empty space on the wall where a noble family would have kept the masks of its consuls.

There had never been one in his family. There would be one now. He thought about that, about his own face in wax looking down from that wall at his grandchildren, at their grandchildren, and found that his eyes had gone hot and that he had to stand very still for a moment until it passed.

In the morning the nobles did exactly what he had said they would. Paullus was elected before noon, with every one of the first-class centuries behind him, and came over to Varro on the tribunal afterwards to take his hand in front of the crowd, as custom required. He was a tall, spare man with a long face and grey in his hair, and his hand was dry and cold.

“Colleague,” said Paullus.

“Colleague.”

They stood side by side while the crowd cheered, two consuls of Rome, looking out over the Field. Varro was aware of Paullus beside him the way a man is aware of a wall at his back.

Paullus spoke without turning his head. “They tell me you mean to fight him the moment you see him.”

“They tell me you mean not to.”

“I mean to fight him when it is right to fight him.”

“So did Fabius,” said Varro. “It was never right.”

Paullus did not answer that. The cheering went on. Somewhere in the crowd the song started up again, the one that rhymed with the Senate, and Varro saw Paullus hear it and saw the line of his jaw change.

“Eight legions,” said Varro. He said it quietly, so that only Paullus could hear. “Eight. Twice what any consul has ever had. The whole city has emptied itself to give us that, and every man in it knows why. If we take eight legions out and walk up and down Apulia with them watching Hannibal burn farms, they will tear down the Senate house with their hands, and they will be right to.”

Paullus turned his head then and looked at him. It was a long look and Varro could not read it.

“Yes,” Paullus said. “I expect they will.” And he turned away to speak to someone else.

That evening Varro wrote no lists. He sat in his room and drank a cup of wine and did not light the lamp, and thought about eight legions, about eighty thousand men. He had a head for numbers, and he tried to see them, and found that he could not. He could see a crowd in the Forum. He could see the voting pens on the Field of Mars, full. He could not see eighty thousand men. He sat in the dark trying, until the wine was gone.
