# The Oxen

**I-11** · POV: Fabius · the ager Falernus and the pass above Casilinum, autumn 217 BC

For three days Fabius had Hannibal in a box, and for three days nobody in the Roman camp believed it.

He believed it. He sat on the hill above the pass in the evenings with his cloak round him and looked down at the plain, which was the richest plain in Italy, the Falernian land, where the vines grew up the elms in long green rows and the wine was so good the Greeks wrote poems about it, and he watched it burn. Hannibal had come down into it a month ago from the mountains, and had been burning it ever since. Farm by farm, village by village, the smoke went up in long columns that leaned all one way in the wind off the sea, and at night the fires made the whole floor of the plain glow as if the earth itself were alight.

And every evening Minucius came and stood beside him and said nothing, which was worse than anything he might have said.

Fabius knew what the camp thought. He had heard it, the way a commander always hears what he is not meant to. They called him the old woman. They called him Hannibal’s tutor, because he followed Hannibal everywhere and taught him nothing. They said the dictator wanted the war to last for ever so that he could stay dictator, and they said he was afraid. And here they sat on the hills, forty thousand Romans, watching the Falernian land burn below them, and the dictator would not let them go down.

But Hannibal had made a mistake. He had come into the plain by one pass and he had meant to go out by another, and Fabius had got there first. He had a garrison in Casilinum, on the river, which shut the road south. He had four thousand men in the pass itself, the narrow way over the hill on the road east, and his own army camped on the heights above it. The plain was a cauldron with the lid on. Hannibal had plunder to carry, cattle and grain and captives, more than his army could eat, and in a month the plain would be bare, and there would be no way out of it that did not go up a narrow road under four thousand Roman spears.

“He will try the pass,” Minucius said on the third evening. It was the first thing he had said in two days.

“Yes.”

“And when he does, we fight.”

“When he does,” said Fabius, “the men in the pass hold it, and we wait. We do not go down. We let him beat himself to death against the pass.”

Minucius made a noise in his throat and went away.

It began after midnight. Fabius was asleep in his tent; he slept well, as old men often do who have stopped expecting much from the night. His freedman woke him with a lamp and said there was something on the hill.

He went out and looked. Across the valley, on the long slope of the hill that ran up beside the pass, there were lights moving. A great many lights, hundreds of them, like torches carried by men, moving up the hillside in the dark all together, in a wide loose crowd, away from the road and up towards the ridge. From his hill he could see that they were not following any path. They were going straight up the slope through the scrub, bobbing, swaying, spreading out and drawing together again, and as he watched, more of them came up from the plain below and joined them, until the whole hillside was crawling with fire.

“They are going over the top,” said someone behind him. “They are going round the pass.”

Fabius stood and watched the lights. He did not know what they were. He knew only that he did not know, and that it was dark, and that Hannibal had done something at night three times already in this war and every time it had been something nobody expected. He gave the order the camp should stand to arms and stay where it was. It was the only order he was sure of.

In the pass, below him, four thousand men had seen the same lights from much closer. And they did not stay where they were.

He watched it happen and could not stop it. He saw the torches of the guard in the pass begin to move, and stream out of the pass and up the hillside after the other lights, to cut them off before they reached the ridge, to catch Hannibal’s army going round them in the dark. The pass emptied. He sent a rider down to call them back and knew as he did it that the rider would be too late. Then, under the hill, in the dark, on the road where the four thousand had been, he heard the sound of an army marching. Not torches. Not shouting. Just a great many feet on a road, and horses, and the creak of carts, very fast, going up through the pass and over it and away.

At dawn he rode down to see.

The lights had been oxen. He found them on the hillside where the guard from the pass had caught up with them, two thousand head of cattle that Hannibal had taken in the plain, with bundles of dry brushwood tied to their horns. In the night the bundles had been lit and the beasts driven up the slope, and they had gone mad with the fire on their heads and run every way across the hill, which was exactly what Hannibal had wanted them to do. Some were still alive, standing in the scrub with their horns charred black and their eyes rolled white, bellowing. A great many were dead. Fabius’s men were going about among them with knives, putting an end to them, and the smell of burned hair lay over the hillside so thick that he put his cloak over his mouth.

Minucius rode up while he was there. He did not say anything at all. He sat his horse and looked at the oxen, and then at the empty pass, and then at Fabius, and then he turned his horse and rode back up the hill.

The news reached Rome, as news did, faster than the dispatches. When Fabius’s letter arrived explaining it, the city had already made up its mind. That autumn the jokes in the Forum were about cattle, and somebody wrote on the wall of the Senate house that the dictator had been outwitted by a herd of cows. Then a second story came in from the camp, and it was worse. Hannibal, they said, burning every farm in the plain, had sent orders that one farm was to be left untouched. It was a farm that belonged to Quintus Fabius, so that all Rome would wonder why.

Fabius heard that one from his son, who wrote to him from Rome to ask if it was true.

He wrote back that it was. He did not explain it, because there was nothing to explain; Hannibal had done it to make the city doubt him, and the city doubted him, so it had worked. He did one thing instead. Earlier that summer he had agreed an exchange of prisoners with Hannibal, man for man, with silver to be paid for the Romans left over, and the Senate had refused to send the silver. He sent his son to sell the spared farm and pay it from that, so that the debt was Rome’s honour settled with Fabius’s land, and there would be no more talk about why Hannibal had spared it. Then he went back to following Hannibal across Italy, at a distance, on the high ground, the way he had done all summer, and the camp went on calling him the old woman. He let it.
