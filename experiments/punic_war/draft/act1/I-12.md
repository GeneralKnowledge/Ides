# Equal Command

**I-12** · POV: Varro · Rome, the Assembly, autumn 217 BC
**Sources:** Liv. 22.27 (the people give Minucius command equal to the dictator's): text. Liv. 22.25 (Varro, praetor the year before; his father said to have been a butcher): text. Minucius's small success while Fabius was in Rome, the tribune Metilius's bill, Fabius leaving the city before the vote, and Varro the only man of rank to speak for it (Liv. 22.24–26): to check.

---

Varro had been waiting all year for someone to give him a bill like this, and when it came it came from a tribune nobody had heard of, about a skirmish nobody had seen.

The skirmish was the thing. While the dictator was in Rome for the sacrifices, sitting in the Senate talking about vows, his master of horse had been left in charge of the army in Apulia under strict orders not to fight. And Minucius had fought. Some foragers of Hannibal’s had been caught out in the open near a place called Gerunium, and Minucius had gone at them with the cavalry and the light troops and killed a good many, and Hannibal had pulled his army back a mile or two. That was all. By the time Minucius’s letter reached the city it was a battle; by the time it had been read out in the Forum it was a victory; and by the evening of the next day the whole city knew that the moment the old man’s back was turned, his master of horse had beaten Hannibal.

Metilius the tribune had put up his bill the next morning. Give Minucius the same command as the dictator. Divide the army. Let each man fight the war his own way, and let Rome see which way won.

It was illegal, or very nearly; there was no such thing as two dictators. It was insulting to the man who held the office. It was the stupidest military idea Varro had ever heard. He went down to the Assembly to speak for it with the happiest heart he had had in months.

The crowd was enormous. It was the end of the harvest and men had come in from the country to the market and stayed for the vote. They filled the Comitium and the Forum around it and stood on the steps of the temples to see. Varro had told his tribe men to put the word about that he would speak, and he could see them in the crowd now, the baker and the fuller and the old centurion, in their places, ready to shout at the right moments.

The nobles had spoken against the bill already, one after another, the ex-consuls in a row. They had talked about the dignity of the dictatorship, and the dangers of a divided command, and their own fathers, and what had been done in the time of their fathers. The crowd had heard them out in a silence Varro could have cut with a knife and sold by the pound. Fabius had not spoken. Fabius had left the city the night before to go back to the army, without waiting for the vote, and that had gone round the Forum faster than the bill had, and every man in the crowd had taken it as an answer.

When the tribune called on Varro there was a stir. Men who knew him nudged men who did not. He went up onto the Rostra and stood looking out over them, and waited until the stir had finished, because a man who starts before they are ready has to start twice.

He did not talk about the dignity of the dictatorship. He talked about money.

He asked them how many of them had farms in Campania. He asked them how many of them had farms in Apulia. He asked them how many of them had heard, this summer, from a cousin or a neighbour, that the farm was burned, and the vines cut, and the oxen driven off to Hannibal’s camp. He saw the hands go up, a few, then more, then a forest of them. He said that he too had a cousin with a farm in Campania, which was true; it was a very small farm and his cousin was a very bad farmer, but it was true. He said that the dictator had sat on a hill above the Falernian land all summer and watched it burn, and had not gone down. He said that the dictator was an honest man and a pious man and a wise man, and that the city ought to be grateful for his wisdom, and that he, Varro, would be very grateful indeed for it if he were a rich man with all his land in Etruria, where Hannibal had not been.

They laughed at that. He let them laugh.

Then he said that Minucius had gone down. Minucius had seen Hannibal’s men burning a farm and had gone down and killed them, and Hannibal had run. That was what the dictator had been afraid of all summer, and it turned out to be possible. He said they should not take his word for it, or the dictator’s; they should give Minucius an army and let him show them.

He came down off the Rostra into a noise like surf.

The bill passed before dark. It passed by more tribes than Varro had dared to count on. He stood at the edge of the voting pens with his hands behind his back and watched the result being read out, and he felt the faces of the ex-consuls turn towards him one by one, and he did not look back at them. He did not need to. He knew what they were thinking. They were thinking that the butcher’s son had found out how to sell meat in the Forum.

Afterwards, walking home, the old centurion fell into step beside him. He had served under Fabius in the Ligurian war, years ago, and had not shouted once during the speech, Varro had noticed.

“That was well done,” the centurion said.

“Thank you.”

“It wasn’t a compliment.” The centurion walked on for a while. “Minucius will go out and look for a fight now. He has to. You’ve made him. He’ll go looking for Hannibal to prove you right, and Hannibal will know it, because Hannibal knows everything about us, and he will give him one.”

Varro said nothing for a few paces.

“Then Minucius will win it,” he said, “or he won’t, and either way the people will have seen what they wanted to see.”

“They’ll have seen men die.”

“They see that already,” Varro said. “In Campania. On their own farms. At least this way they get a vote on it.”

The centurion did not answer. At the corner of the street he said good night and turned off towards the Subura, and Varro went on up the hill alone, and found that the happy feeling had gone somewhere, and could not remember exactly when.
