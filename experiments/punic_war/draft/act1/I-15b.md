# Advice

**I-15b** · POV: Fabius · Rome, spring 216 BC
**Sources:** Fabius's warning to Paullus before he leaves for the army, and Paullus's answer that he would rather face the enemy's swords than the citizens' votes (Liv. 22.39–40): to check. Paullus's earlier trial after his first consulship (Liv. 22.35): to check. The scene follows Livy's sense; the words are the book's.

---

Paullus came to see him on the evening before he left, and Fabius had known he would.

He received him in the garden, because it was a warm evening and the first warm evening of the year, and because he did not want the freedmen in the house to listen. There was a stone bench under the fig tree that his father had planted, and Fabius sat on it and Paullus did not. Paullus stood, and walked about, and looked at the fig tree as if it had done him an injury.

“You know why I’ve come,” Paullus said.

“You have come to ask me how to fight Hannibal,” said Fabius. “And you are hoping I will say something you can repeat in the camp so that when you refuse to fight him, it will be my fault and not yours.”

Paullus stopped walking.

Then, unexpectedly, he laughed. It was a short sound, not very happy, but real.

“Something like that,” he said.

Fabius looked at him. He had known Lucius Aemilius since he was a young man, a stiff, proud, clever young man, who had been a good consul against the Illyrians and had then very nearly been ruined by a trial over the plunder. The trial had been a disgrace. The man had been as honest as men are. But the people had wanted someone to blame for something, and had come within a few votes of blaming him, and Paullus had never forgotten it or forgiven it. Fabius had always thought that was a weakness in him. He thought now that it might be the most useful thing about him.

“Sit down,” Fabius said.

Paullus sat on the far end of the bench.

“I will tell you three things,” said Fabius. “Then I am going to bed; I am an old man and I have had a long day of being thanked by people who called me a coward last autumn.

“The first is about Hannibal. He is not stronger than you. You will have eight legions and the allies, near enough eighty thousand men; he has perhaps half that, and they are a long way from home, and nobody is sending them anything, and every month they have to find their bread in a country that hates them. Time is your ally. Every day you do not fight him is a day he grows weaker and you do not. That is the whole of my wisdom and you may have it free.

“The second is about the ground. He chooses it. Every battle so far, he has chosen it: the river, the lake, the hill at Gerunium. He looks at a place and sees what will happen there. We look at a place and see a place. If you must fight him, do it where you can see everything round you for a mile, with nothing he can hide in, and make him come to you.

“The third,” said Fabius, and paused.

“The third is about your colleague.”

Paullus said nothing.

“You will find, I think,” said Fabius, slowly, “that you have to fight two enemies, and that Hannibal is not the more dangerous. I do not say Varro is a bad man. I don’t believe he is. He is a clever man, and he loves the people, and the people love him, and he has promised them a battle. He will not be able to stop himself giving them one. If he does not, he is nothing; he is only another consul. Hannibal knows that. Hannibal knows the name of every consul we have sent against him and everything that was said in the Forum at their elections. You may be certain he knows about Varro’s promises.”

“What do you advise?”

“I advise you to be hated,” said Fabius. “As I was. To be called a coward in the camp and in the Forum and to let them call you it and not care, and keep the army out of his reach until he starves. It is easy to say. It is the hardest thing I have ever done.”

Paullus looked at the ground between his feet for some time.

“You were dictator,” he said at last. “You had no colleague. Even then they gave half your army away to Minucius.”

“Yes.”

“I will have a colleague who commands on alternate days.” He said it quite flatly. “I will keep the army safe one day and he will march it out the next. And when I come home, if I come home, the people will try me again, as they tried me before. For cowardice, this time, instead of theft.” He looked up. “I have been through one trial, Quintus Fabius. I know exactly how it feels to stand in the Forum and have the whole city look at you as if you were a thief. I will not do it again. If it comes to it, I would rather face Hannibal’s swords than the votes of my fellow citizens.”

Fabius did not answer at once.

“That,” he said finally, “is the answer I was afraid you would give.”

Paullus stood up. He held out his hand, and Fabius took it, and then Paullus went out through the house, and Fabius heard the door close behind him and his freedmen talking in the street.

He sat on under the fig tree until it was dark. He thought about Paullus’s hand, which had been cold, on a warm evening. He thought about the eight legions, and the alternate days, and about what a man means when he says he would rather die than be tried. He was a careful man and did not like to think in omens. But when his freedman came out with a lamp to bring him in, he found that he had been sitting with his hands pressed together in his lap, very hard, the way he had sat as a boy in the temple while the priest looked at the liver, waiting to be told.
