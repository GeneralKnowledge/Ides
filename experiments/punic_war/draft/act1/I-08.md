# Terms

**I-08** · POV: Maharbal · Lake Trasimene and a village beyond it, June 217 BC
**Sources:** Pol. 3.84 (the morning very misty; Flaminius killed by Celts; about six thousand who broke out surrender to Maharbal on condition their lives are spared): text. The mist was Hannibal’s luck, and everybody said so afterwards, as if he had ordered it. Maharbal did not believe in luck. He believed in ground, and the ground had been chosen days before there was any mist to choose.

The road ran along the north shore of the lake, between the water and the hills, and at the west end of the shore the hills came right down to the water and pinched the road almost shut. Beyond the pinch the shore opened out into a long flat strip, a mile or more, with the lake on one side and a wall of low wooded hills on the other, and at the far end of the strip the hills closed in again. It was a bottle. You walked in at the neck, and the bottom was a hill, and the sides were hills, and the only way out was the way you came in.

Hannibal had camped the Libyans and the Spaniards on the hill at the bottom of the bottle, in plain sight, where the Romans would see their fires and know where they were. He had put the slingers and the light troops along the wooded slopes on the left, among the trees, out of sight of the road. He had put the Gauls further back along the same slopes. And he had put the cavalry, Maharbal’s Numidians and the heavy horse, round behind the hills at the neck, so that when the last Roman was in the bottle they could ride down and put the cork in.

“He will come,” Hannibal had said. “He has been chasing us for a week. He wants to catch up.”

So they had waited in the dark behind the hills, without fires, the men lying beside their horses and holding their muzzles to stop them calling to each other. In the last hour of the night the mist came up off the lake.

It came up like milk boiling. Maharbal had never seen anything like it. By first light it filled the whole strip of shore from the water to the foot of the hills and lay over the road to the height of a man on horseback, white and thick and perfectly still. Above it the hilltops stood out clear in the early sun, with the trees on them and the slingers among the trees, and the hill at the bottom of the bottle with its standards. Below it there was nothing at all.

He heard the Romans before he saw anything. They were in the mist on the road, coming, and they were not trying to be quiet. He heard the tramp of feet on the road and the jingle and creak of harness and baggage, and voices, ordinary voices, men talking, somebody laughing, a centurion swearing at a mule. They were marching the way men march on a road in their own country with the enemy somewhere ahead. The column went past the neck below him for a long time. He lay on the ridge with his chin on his arms and listened to it go.

Then the trumpets went on the hill at the far end, and all along the slopes the army stood up.

He could not see what happened in the mist. Nobody could, not even Hannibal on his hill; the reports came in afterwards and did not agree. What Maharbal saw, from the ridge, was the slingers and the javelin men coming down out of the trees on the left into the top of the mist and vanishing into it, and then the Gauls after them, a long howling wave of them going down the slope at a run, and the mist taking them too. He heard the noise change below. The ordinary voices stopped. There was a moment, quite short, when there was almost no sound at all, as if the whole Roman column had drawn breath, and then it began, and it was the worst noise he had ever heard and he had been at the Trebia.

He took the cavalry down to the neck and closed it.

That was all the cavalry did that morning, for three hours. They sat across the road at the neck of the bottle and killed whatever came out of the mist towards them, and very little came. The Romans never formed a line. They had been caught in column of march with their baggage, strung out over a mile of road, fighting in little knots wherever they stood, with the enemy coming at them out of a fog from a direction they could not see. Men came running out of the mist towards the cavalry with their arms full of nothing, without shields, looking back over their shoulders, and saw the horses and stopped and stood there. Some of them turned and ran into the lake. He watched men wade out until the water was at their chins and stand there, with only their heads above the water, and the Numidians rode in after them and killed them where they stood.

The sun burned the mist off in the middle of the morning, and he saw the shore.

He did not afterwards describe it to anyone. He had a way of thinking about things he did not want to think about, which was to count them, and he counted horses that morning, the Roman horses lying dead on the road, because he could count horses without thinking about anything else. He got to two hundred and stopped.

A rider came to him at noon with orders. A body of Romans had broken out. They had been at the head of the column when the trap closed and they had simply kept going, straight ahead, through the Spaniards at the far end and out the other side, six thousand of them, and gone on up into the hills, and taken refuge in a village. The general wanted them. Maharbal was to take the Spaniards and the light troops and the Numidians and go and get them.

The village was on a hill some miles to the east. When he got there in the afternoon the Romans were behind its walls and the walls were not much, a rampart of earth and stones round the houses, but they had made a line along it with their shields and they were standing there looking at him.

He rode round the village twice with an interpreter, looking at it. He looked at the Romans, who were filthy and exhausted and hungry and had nothing to eat in that village, he could tell, because there was nobody in it but themselves. He looked at his own men, who had been fighting since dawn. It would take a day to take the place, perhaps two, and a great many dead, and at the end of it six thousand dead Romans who would be no use to anyone.

So he sent the interpreter up to the rampart under a branch, and the interpreter called up to them that if they laid down their arms they would be let go with their lives. That was the word. Their lives and nothing else.

They talked about it for some time behind the rampart. He could see them talking, an officer with his helmet off arguing with a group of others. Then one by one they came out and put their swords and their shields down in a pile in front of the gate. He had his men count them. It came to just under six thousand. He sent a rider to tell the general and turned for camp with the whole lot of them walking in front of his horses.

Hannibal met them at the edge of the camp in the evening, on foot, with his bandaged eye and his officers behind him. He walked down the line of Romans looking at them. Then he walked back to Maharbal.

“You gave them terms,” he said.

“Their lives.”

“You had no power to give them anything. You are my officer; you are not the general.” He did not say it angrily. He said it in the tone in which Maharbal had heard him tell a cook that the bread was underdone. “They will be prisoners. The Romans among them, at least. The allies can go home; I have no quarrel with the allies, they can go home and tell their towns I let them go.” He turned and raised his voice so that the interpreters could take it down the line, and began to say it again in Greek, for the Romans: that he had come to make war on Rome, and not on Italy.

Maharbal sat on his horse and listened. He had expected to be thanked. He found that he was not angry; it was too large for that. He sat there while the Latin and Etruscan prisoners were taken out of the line and the Roman citizens were left standing, and he thought about the interpreter calling up to the rampart under a branch, in a clear voice, with Maharbal’s name on the words. He thought about the officer who had argued, behind the wall, and who was standing now with the Romans, and who had looked at him once as he was led away, without any expression, as if to fix his face.

That night he did not sleep. He sat outside his tent with a jar of wine and did not drink it. Towards midnight Hiempsal came and sat beside him on the ground, as the Numidians did, without asking, and after a while said in his bad Punic, “He was right, you know. You can’t let the officers make the treaties.”

“I know he was right,” said Maharbal. “That isn’t the trouble.”

Hiempsal waited.

“The trouble is that they will remember it was me,” Maharbal said.
