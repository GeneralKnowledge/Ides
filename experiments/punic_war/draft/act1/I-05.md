# The Letter from Italy

**I-05** · POV: Hanno · Carthage, early spring 217 BC
**Sources:** Hanno's opposition to sending Hannibal to Spain (Liv. 21.3) and to the war over Saguntum (Liv. 21.10): text. How and when news of the Trebia reached Carthage is not recorded; the scene is invention inside the record.

---

The first ship of the season came in on a morning when the wind had dropped and the harbour lay flat as oil, and by the time Hanno had finished his breakfast the whole city knew there had been a battle in Italy and that Hannibal had won it.

He heard it from his steward, who had heard it from the boy who swept the steps, who had been down at the harbour wall when the ship tied up. The steward told it with his eyes shining. Hanno let him finish, and then asked which ship it had been, and whose, and where from. It had come from Spain, it turned out, not from Italy at all, and carried a letter from Hasdrubal in New Carthage, who had it from a rider who had come over the mountains before the snow closed them, and the rider had it from Hannibal’s own camp. So it was a story about a story about a letter, three months old, and the city was already lighting lamps.

“They say the Romans were driven into a river,” the steward said. “In the snow. Thousands of them.”

“Do they say how many of ours?”

The steward did not know. He looked at Hanno as if the question were in poor taste.

The senate met at noon. Hanno had himself carried there in the litter rather than walking, though it was less than a mile, because he was sixty-nine and his knees were bad in the cold and because he wished to arrive looking like a man who had not hurried. The streets were full. People had come up from the harbour quarter and down from the Byrsa to stand in the square outside the senate house and wait for something to happen, and they made way for his litter without much interest. Somebody shouted the name Barca at it, not unkindly, the way men shout the name of a horse at the races.

Inside, Himilco was reading the letter aloud.

He read it well. He was one of those men who had been made for reading out other people’s good news. The Romans had come out of their camp in midwinter, unfed, and crossed a river, and been destroyed between the river and the army, and the elephants, and an ambush laid by Mago. Their consul had written to Rome that the weather beat him. The Gauls of the whole country were coming over. Hannibal was wintering among them and would march south when the passes opened. And at the end there was a sentence that Himilco read twice, in a voice that shook with feeling, in which Hannibal sent his duty to the senate of Carthage and asked it to remember his men.

The house rose. Men were on their feet clapping and calling out, and the older men in the front rows had tears on their faces, some of them, and Himilco stood with the letter held up in both hands as if it were a sacrifice. Hanno stayed in his seat. He did not do it to be seen; his knees would not have thanked him. But he was seen, and the noise around him changed a little in a way he had heard before.

When it was quiet enough, a motion was made to send the thanks of the senate to Hannibal and his brothers and the army. It was carried by acclamation. Another was made to send a thank offering to the temple of Eshmun on the Byrsa. That was carried too. Then Himilco, still standing, turned in his place and looked across the floor at Hanno, as Hanno had known he would.

“The house has not heard from Hanno,” Himilco said. “He was very free with his opinions last year. Has he none today?”

Hanno let them laugh. Then he said, without getting up, “Only a question.”

“By all means.”

“The letter asks us to remember his men. I should like to know how many there are left to remember.” He said it mildly. “It doesn’t say. It says how many Romans died, which is very pleasant to hear, but it doesn’t say how many of ours went over the mountains and how many came down the other side, or how many died in the river, or how many are lying sick in a Gaulish village eating acorns. Hasdrubal must know, or he could find out. I would like to know that before I vote him men, because I imagine that is what the next letter will be about.”

There was a little silence.

“You would like a list of the dead,” said Himilco.

“I would like a price,” Hanno said. “I have never bought anything without one.”

Somebody at the back laughed, and was hushed. Himilco began to answer, and the answer was about courage, and the honour of the city, and the shame of counting coins over the graves of heroes. It went on for some time and Hanno did not listen to it. He sat with his hands folded on his stick and looked at the faces in the front rows, the merchants and the landowners and the men who had made their money in Spain under Hamilcar and his son-in-law and now his son, and counted, out of habit, the ones who had not stood up when the letter was read. There were not many. He knew all of them.

He had said this before. He had said it when they sent the boy out to Spain, seven or eight years ago, and been laughed at; and two years ago when the boy took Saguntum, and been laughed at again. Keep the young man at home under the laws, he had said, and teach him to obey before you teach him to command. They had sent him anyway. He had grown up in an army that belonged to his father, and then to his brother-in-law, and then to him, and an army like that belongs to nobody in Carthage, whatever the treaties say. Now he had taken it across the world, and every letter that came back would ask for more.

That was what nobody in the room wanted to hear. Not that the war was wrong. That it was expensive, and that the bills would come in for years after the lamps had gone out.

The meeting ended in the middle of the afternoon. Outside, the crowd had grown and somebody had brought wine. Hanno’s bearers carried him through it slowly. A boy ran beside the litter for a few paces and shouted up at him that he was a Roman lover. Hanno looked down at the boy and saw that he was perhaps twelve, and barefoot, and that he would be about the right age, in five or six years, for whatever the next letter asked for.

At home he ate his dinner and sent for his steward and asked him the price of grain in the harbour that morning, and the price of hiring a ship to Spain, and the price of a Balearic slinger for a season. The steward knew the first two and promised to find out the third. Hanno wrote all three down on a wax tablet and kept it by his bed. It seemed to him that somebody in the city ought to.
