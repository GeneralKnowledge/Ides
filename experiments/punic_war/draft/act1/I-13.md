# The Hill at Gerunium

**I-13** · POV: Vettius · near Gerunium, Apulia, autumn 217 BC
**Sources:** Pol. 3.103 (equal command), 3.104 (Hannibal lures Minucius to a hill), 3.105 (Fabius comes to the relief of his imperilled comrades): text. Liv. 22.28–30 (the ambush; Fabius's rescue; Minucius calls him father): text. The army split into two camps, and hidden men in the hollows round the hill: to check.

---

They had given half the army to Minucius, and Vettius was in the half that cheered.

He had cheered with the rest of them when the news came that the people had voted it. The whole of Minucius’s camp had cheered; men had banged their swords on their shields, and somebody had got hold of wine, and the centurions had looked the other way. For three months they had followed the old man up and down the hills of Samnium and Apulia, watching the smoke of burning farms, never fighting, and every man in the army knew a dozen jokes about it. Now they had a general of their own who wanted to fight, and the jokes were about the other camp.

Sabidius had been put in the other camp. The legions had been shared out between the two commanders, two each, like a dowry, and Sabidius’s maniple had gone with Fabius’s half and Vettius’s with Minucius’s. They had said goodbye in the morning with a good deal of noise about which of them would be dead first. Now Vettius could see Fabius’s camp from his own, a mile and a half off, on a rise of ground, and in the evenings he thought about Sabidius sitting over there complaining, and found he missed it.

Between the two Roman camps and Hannibal’s camp, which was on the far side of the plain, there was a hill.

It was not much of a hill, a long low swelling in the middle of the flat ground, bare on top, with a few hollows and gullies round its foot where the winter rains had cut into the earth. It was the only high ground for miles. The men talked about it. Whoever held that hill could look down into all three camps and could see anything that moved on the plain.

On the fourth morning after the army was divided, Hannibal sent some of his light troops to take it.

Vettius saw them go up from the rampart, a few hundred men going up the far slope in a loose crowd. Then Minucius sent his own light troops to knock them off, and Hannibal sent cavalry, and Minucius sent cavalry, and it went on like that all morning. Each side fed in a few more and a few more, the fight on the hill getting bigger, the noise of it coming across the plain, until around midday the trumpets went in the camp and Minucius led the legions out to finish it.

Vettius marched with his maniple. He had been in one battle and had thought he would be afraid, and found instead that he was mostly hungry, which he took as a good sign. The hill was ahead of them. On top of it the Roman light troops were holding, just, against a crowd of Hannibal’s men, and as the legions came up the slope behind them the enemy began to give way. They went back down the far side. The legions went up and over the top after them. Vettius came over the crest with his shield up, into the wind, and saw Hannibal’s army.

All of it. Drawn up on the plain beyond the hill in its long line, the Spaniards and the Libyans and the Gauls, the cavalry on the wings, standing quite still and waiting, exactly as they had stood on the far side of the river at the Trebia. The men they had been chasing ran down into the line and through it and disappeared.

The maniple stopped. Behind them more maniples were coming over the crest and stopping too.

“Oh,” said someone beside him, quite quietly.

Then from behind, from the hollows and gullies round the foot of the hill, the ones they had marched straight past on the way up without a glance, men came out of the ground.

He would remember that it was exactly the same. He thought about it for years afterwards, at night, in Sicily: that it was exactly the same as the Trebia, the hidden men, the noise behind, and that he had been there and seen it and that it had not helped at all. Knowing did not help. You could not turn round in a line of battle to look behind you; you could only hear it and feel the men at the back start to push forward, away from it, and the men in front start to push back, away from what was in front, and be squeezed between.

It went bad very fast. The light troops broke first, back over the hill, through the legions. The cavalry on the flanks was gone; he never saw where. The maniples on the right gave and the Numidians were round behind them, and the whole Roman line on the hill began to bend in on itself like a bow. Vettius was in the middle of it with his shield pressed against the back of the man in front. He could not see anything but helmets and sky, and was quite certain he was going to die there, on a nameless hill in Apulia, for Varro’s speech and Minucius’s pride and a joke about an old woman. He was not afraid. He was angry. He would remember that too.

Then there was a different noise from the left. It was trumpets, Roman trumpets, coming from the wrong direction, and a roar of voices, and the press round him eased a little, and then a little more.

Fabius had come.

He had seen it from his camp, the men said afterwards. He had been watching all morning from his rampart, and when the legions went over the hill he had known what was on the other side, and he had had his army out of the gate and formed and marching before the ambush was sprung. Now it came up onto the flank of the hill in good order, his two legions in their lines, the standards up, and the Numidians who had been round behind Minucius’s men saw it coming and pulled away, and the hidden men from the gullies pulled away, and Hannibal’s line on the plain, which had been coming forward, stopped. It stood there for a while. Then it went back to its camp.

Vettius walked down the hill afterwards on legs that did not seem to be his. At the bottom, among the men of Fabius’s army, he found Sabidius sitting on a rock with his helmet off, eating a piece of cheese.

“I saved your life,” said Sabidius.

“You did not.”

“The old woman did. I came with him. I’ll take my share.” He broke off half the cheese and held it out.

Vettius took it and sat down beside him on the rock.

He did not see what happened next, at the Fabian camp, but everyone heard about it by nightfall. Minucius had marched his legions straight to Fabius’s camp and drawn them up in front of the dictator’s tent, and gone in, and come out again with Fabius beside him. In front of both armies he had called the old man his father, and said that he owed him his life and the lives of his men, and that he had had enough of being a general. He handed his army back. The two camps were one again by the next day.

Vettius thought, lying awake that night, about the speech in Rome that had split the army, and about the man who had made it. He had never seen Varro. He did not know what he looked like. He found he could imagine him perfectly, a fat senator with clean hands, standing up on the Rostra telling a crowd that the men in Apulia wanted to fight. He thought that if he ever met him he would like to tell him what it had been like on the hill. Then he thought that Varro would probably not listen, and went to sleep.
