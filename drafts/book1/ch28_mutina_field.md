# SC-80: Mud

POV: F3 Laberius · Forum Gallorum / Mutina
Date / place: April 43 · ditch / standard line
Canon refs: A11 · Galba letter energy · B4 · D4
Info ENTER: Orders, mud, pay · EXIT: Tactical pressure on Antony; consuls falling
Overhear risk: High camp

---

Laberius’s world narrowed to mud and which eagle still stood.

In rooms with clean sandals this would be named success before the mud dried. In the ditch it was screaming. Martians refusing to be held. Someone’s javelin nearly killing him until a voice yelled his century’s joke-name. Antony’s men came out hard. Hard enough that Laberius smelled the old Spanish stubbornness again, the kind that did not care who Rome called consul. Hirtius hit harder when the day turned. Laberius didn’t see strategy. He saw a standard tilt. He saw a friend open at the neck. Blood mixed with meltwater and ran pink off the rim of his shield.

Vibius spat black grit. “Win,” he said. “Then count.”

“Count what?” said the man behind them. “Five hundred from the boy at Capua to stand in this.”

“Five hundred beats Antony’s hundred,” Vibius said. Laberius had taken the boy’s coin in the end, in the autumn, when the shadow with a seal learned to feed men. It had bought his boots for the young Caesar’s ranks and this ditch. It had not moved the twine.

Pay talk lived under the noise like a second drum. Who owed donatives. Which Caesar’s name still fed mouths. Whether Hirtius’s clean reputation meant Rome finally understood mud. Capua still sat in Laberius’s boots as an unpaid date. A boy from another century asked if they were fighting for the Senate or for bread. Laberius said, “Fight for the man on your left. Capua argues later, if later still has a road.”

Between assaults, when the yelling thinned and the cold found room, Vibius and two men from the contubernium talked dirt the way other men talked gods. “Capua was a date when the paymaster breathed,” Vibius said, voice flat. Mud did not buy jokes. “Now Capua is only what we stand in.” The second man wiped pink melt from his shield rim. “Still wants a road, if we live.” Laberius did not finish the thought aloud. Finishing would have made Capua a speech. He kept his century from becoming a gap. When a signal dipped wrong in the wet air, he held the line anyway. Signals lied as often as clerks.

Yell-rumours arrived before facts. Someone claimed a consul down. Someone claimed both. Someone claimed a victory large enough to make the ditch look like a lie. Laberius believed the ache in his forearms and nothing else. Until mid-afternoon, when a staff officer he recognised from Hirtius’s tent came past on a blown horse, cloak dark with more than rain, and said to a tribune three paces from Laberius’s ear: “The consul Hirtius is dead. I held his shoulder. Do not invent a better story.”

That was one fact Laberius could keep: Hirtius down, confirmed by a man who had touched him. Pansa’s name still swam in the yell-water, wounded, dying, fine, poisoned, blessed. “They carried him off at Forum Gallorum,” Vibius said. “A week back. Javelin in the side. I saw the litter. I didn’t see inside it.” Laberius didn’t promote any Pansa version. Vibius had a litter. Nobody had a shoulder.

Near dusk Antony’s near line pulled back toward the higher ground. Not a rout Laberius could swear to. Only a thinning of shields where shields had been thick. He saw that with his own eyes. A wounded Antonian left behind tried to crawl and was finished by someone Laberius did not know. Finishing was not glory either. Withdrawal or regroup: clerks would decide later. Laberius decided only that his century still had an eagle. And a gap where a friend had been.

Water ran into his boots. He had stopped noticing wet hours ago; noticing returned when the fighting thinned and the cold found room. Vibius pointed at Mutina’s wall, still closed. “Town’s still his,” Vibius said, meaning Decimus. Laberius grunted. Walls didn’t feed the ditch.

A mule train stuck in the same ditch as the dead. Drivers cursed gods and officers with equal accuracy. Laberius helped push because pushing was orders you could obey without understanding Senate talk. One mule went down and stayed down; they cut the traces and left it.

He stacked a shield that was not his. The owner would not need it. Somewhere behind, gallopers already carried “great success” toward Rome. Laberius carried a friend’s boots, left boot split at the heel, still warm somehow. He knew Hirtius was dead. He knew Antony’s near shields had thinned. What Rome would buy with those two facts, he left to the gallopers. He didn’t follow their dust.

# SC-81: Poison Talk

POV: F4 Hordeonius · rumour ledger / letter traffic
Date / place: Late April 43 · Hordeonius’s stall, Rome
Canon refs: AMB-2: not confirmed · M17 · FT11
Info ENTER: Pansa dying · EXIT: Poison rumour spreads, unconfirmed
Overhear risk: Extreme rumour

---

Hordeonius recorded three explanations for Pansa’s death before the man had finished dying:

1. Battlefield medicine
2. Antony’s smear
3. The boy’s friends prefer fewer consuls

He labelled all three *Rumour* in Greek abbreviation, because Latin made gossip look like evidence. A client wanted to buy the third as fact, wanted a price on conspiracy that would make his dinner clever. Sosia sat on the next stool with a cup; a runner from camp hovered with ink still wet.

Hordeonius refused the sale and charged for the refusal.

“Do not sell poison as fact,” he told the man, gentle as a father closing a child’s eyes. “I keep seals. Seals are not dinner cleverness. Pay for the refusal if you want a clean margin.”

The client bowed, grateful as a man receiving virtue. “Clean margins. Of course. Wisdom.”

Sosia tipped his cup toward Hordeonius without drinking. “Only you could make refusing a sale sound like a temple tax.”

Hordeonius did not smile. “Silence clears. Cleverness does not.”

Sosia muttered, after, that dead consuls moved land prices either way. A letter from camp arrived with three different inks and one shared panic. Hirtius down as fact in one hand. Pansa still a menu of causes in another. Hordeonius priced land by the panic without crowning a cause and entered *Pansa: cause open*.

A second client asked whether to short land near Mutina. Hordeonius took a fee for advising against it and wrote the fee under *prudence*. Sleep would argue with him later. For now the market argued louder.

# SC-81b: Three Verbs

POV: Cicero · his house on the Palatine
Date / place: Last days of April 43 · dinner
Canon refs: T12 · Cic. *Ad Brut.* 1.3.2 · *Fam.* 11.20–21
Info ENTER: Mutina relieved; both consuls dead; the Senate’s honours for Decimus · EXIT: The saying out of the room
Overhear risk: One guest

---

A week earlier the crowd had carried him up the Capitol.

Cicero kept coming back to it the way the tongue goes back to a sore tooth, except that this did not hurt. The news from Forum Gallorum had reached Rome wrong first, as news did. For a day the city believed Antony had won, and Cicero’s enemies had begun saying in the Forum that he meant to take the fasces for himself and should be stopped before he did. Then a second courier came in, and the truth with him, and by the afternoon a crowd Cicero had not summoned and could not have counted was at his door. They took him up the hill to Jupiter, and down again to the Rostra, and set him there, and shouted his name until he had to raise his hand to stop them. He had written it to Brutus that night and been careful to sound modest about it. He had not felt modest. He had felt, for the first time since the Ides, like a man whose sentences moved bodies.

Then Mutina, and the siege broken, and the price of it: Hirtius dead in the ditch, Pansa of his wound a day after. Two consuls gone in a week. The Senate had sat with their chairs empty and voted what it could. A triumph for Decimus, who had held the wall. The dead consuls’ legions to Decimus, to finish Antony. Thanksgivings. Statues. And for the boy, whose veterans had stood in the mud beside the consuls, an ovation and a vote of thanks, and a quiet agreement among the older benches that he had served his turn.

Cicero had argued for more. Not much more. Enough that the boy would not feel the slight.

“He’ll feel it anyway,” said Tiro, clearing tablets at the door of the dining room. Tiro said such things at doors, where they could be taken as housekeeping.

There were six at dinner. Two young senators who had found their courage since the news came; a cousin of Atticus; a magistrate’s brother whose name Cicero kept losing; and Segulius Labeo, who was nobody in particular and went everywhere, and who collected sayings the way other men collected cameos, to take out and show at other tables.

The fish was good. The wine was better. Somewhere past the second jug one of the young senators, flushed and pleased with himself, asked what was to be done with young Caesar now.

“Now that he’s won,” the young man said. “Now that we’ve won. He has legions. He has that name. He has no consul above him any more. What do we do with him?”

Everyone looked at Cicero. Cicero had been waiting for the question all evening without knowing it.

He knew a dozen true answers. That the boy was nineteen and grieving and owed money he could not pay. That the veterans loved the name more than the man and would leave him the day the name stopped feeding them. That he was clever, much cleverer than the older benches thought, and had written to Cicero every week since the autumn in a tone of respectful sonship that Cicero did not entirely believe and did not entirely disbelieve. That nobody knew what to do with him, least of all Cicero, who had brought him into the Senate as a sword and now had to decide where to hang it.

None of those was an answer for the second jug.

“*Laudandum adulescentem,*” Cicero said. “*Ornandum. Tollendum.*”

Praise the young man. Honour him. Raise him up.

And the last word turned over in the air, as he had meant it to, and showed its other face. *Tollere.* To lift. To lift away. To remove.

There was half a breath of silence, which was the sound of the table hearing the second meaning. Then the laugh came, and it was a good one, the kind of laugh Cicero had been buying with sentences for forty years. The young senator slapped the couch. Atticus’s cousin wiped his eyes. The magistrate’s brother said it again, *tollendum*, to taste it.

Labeo laughed last. He laughed with his mouth while his eyes did something else, a small movement like a man putting a coin in his purse. Cicero saw it and was flattered by it. A saying worth keeping. He had made it. It would go round the city by the end of the week, and men who had never heard him plead would know he was still the quickest tongue in Rome.

At the door Tiro had stopped clearing.

Later, when the guests had gone and the lamps were guttering, Tiro said, “Labeo dines with everyone.”

“That is why one says things in front of him.”

“He dines with the boy’s friends too.”

Cicero waved a hand. He was tired and warm and a week ago a crowd had carried him up the Capitol. “It was a joke, Tiro. A pun. The boy has had a Greek education. He knows what a pun is.”

“He knows what *tollendum* means,” said Tiro.

Cicero thought about rebuking him and found he could not be bothered. He went to bed. He slept well. He did not dream of anything at all, and in the morning he could remember only that the dinner had gone off well and that he had been, for one moment, very good.
