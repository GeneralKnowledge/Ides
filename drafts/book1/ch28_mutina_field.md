# SC-80 — Mud

POV: F3 Laberius · Forum Gallorum / Mutina  
Date / place: April 43 · ditch / standard line  
Canon refs: A11 · Galba letter energy · B4 · D4  
Info ENTER: Orders, mud, pay · EXIT: Tactical pressure on Antony; consuls falling  
Overhear risk: High camp

---

Laberius’ world narrowed to mud, shield rim, and which eagle still stood.

They said later it was a great success. In the ditch it was screaming and the Martians refusing to be held and someone’s javelin nearly killing him until a voice yelled his century’s joke-name. Antony’s men came out hard—hard enough that Laberius smelled the old Spanish stubbornness again, the kind that did not care who Rome called consul. Hirtius hit harder when the day turned. Laberius did not see strategy. He saw a standard tilt and a friend open at the neck. Blood mixed with meltwater until colour became argument.

Vibius spat black grit. “Win,” he said. “Then count.”

Pay talk lived under the noise like a second drum: who owed donatives, which Caesar’s name still fed mouths, whether Hirtius’ clean reputation meant Rome finally understood mud. A boy from another century asked if they were fighting for the Senate or for bread. Laberius said, “Fight for the man on your left. Bread argues later.”

He kept his century from becoming a gap. Gaps ate men. When a signal dipped wrong in the wet air, he held the line anyway—signals lied as often as clerks.

When rumour said a consul was down, Laberius tasted metal. Consuls were supposed to be furniture behind wars. Furniture was breaking. Someone claimed Hirtius; someone claimed Pansa; someone claimed both and a victory large enough to make the ditch look like a lie. Laberius believed the yell nearest his ear and the ache in his forearms. Fields lied the same way plains would later: through messengers who needed the story more than the mud did.

A mule train stuck in the same ditch as the dead. Drivers cursed gods and officers with equal accuracy. Laberius helped push because pushing was orders you could obey without understanding Senate weather.

He stacked a shield that was not his. The owner would not need it. That was the only strategy he trusted before dark. Somewhere behind, gallopers carried “great success” toward Rome. Laberius carried a friend’s boots and did not call it glory. Glory, he decided, was what clerks invented when they had clean sandals and a need to sleep.

# SC-81 — Poison Talk

POV: F4 Hordeonius · rumour ledger / letter weather  
Date / place: Rome / camp paper · M17 · FT11  
Canon refs: AMB-2 — not confirmed  
Info ENTER: Pansa dying · EXIT: Poison rumour spreads — unconfirmed  
Overhear risk: Extreme rumour

---

Hordeonius recorded three explanations for Pansa’s death before the man had finished dying:

1. Battlefield medicine  
2. Antony’s smear  
3. The boy’s friends prefer fewer consuls  

He labeled all three *Rumour* in Greek abbreviation, because Latin made gossip look like minutes. A client wanted to buy the third as fact—wanted a price on conspiracy that would make his dinner clever. Hordeonius refused the sale and charged for the refusal.

“Do not sell poison as fact,” he told the man. “Fact is expensive. Rumour is a tax on fools. I am a priest of seals, not of convenience. Seals survive opinions. Opinions do not survive seals.”

Sosia muttered that dead consuls moved land prices either way. A letter from camp arrived with three different inks and one shared panic. Hordeonius priced land as if rumour were weather—which it was—and entered *Pansa: cause open* without picking a god. AMB-2 held in wax: the poison talk lived; the ledger did not crown it.

A second client asked whether to short land near Mutina. Hordeonius said shorting battlefields was how men learned the gods had a sense of humour. He took a fee for the advice and entered it under *prudence*.

He almost felt virtuous. Virtue under civil war was a rate that cleared until sleep audited it. Sleep would argue later. For now the market argued louder, and Hordeonius listened like a man counting rain.
