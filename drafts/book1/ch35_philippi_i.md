# SC-89a: Abydos

POV: Cassius · Abydos, on the Hellespont
Date / place: Summer 42 · the camp before the crossing to Europe; Cassius’s tent at dawn
Canon refs: S2 · Plut. *Brut.* 36–37, *Caes.* 69 · Epicurus (via Cassius)
Info ENTER: The armies about to cross into Thrace · EXIT: Brutus’s night visitor, reported; Cassius explains it; “Philippi” left in the air
Overhear risk: None; Cassius sends the slave out

---

Brutus came to his tent before the trumpets, when the strait below the camp was still the colour of lead and the ships for the crossing lay along the beach in rows like cattle waiting to be loaded.

Cassius was awake. He was always awake at that hour now; it was the hour the accounts came out right, before anyone could add to them. He looked up from the grain tallies and saw Brutus in the doorway with his cloak on over his night tunic and his feet bare on the matting, and sent the slave out before either of them spoke.

“Sit,” he said.

Brutus sat. He did not seem to know what to do with his hands. He put them on his knees. Then he told it, in the plain voice he used for facts, without periods.

He had been working late. He always worked late; he slept a little after dinner and then sat up half the night with his papers, while the camp was quiet, and dozed for an hour before dawn. Last night he had been reading. The lamp was low. The camp was very still, the way a camp is in the third watch, when even the sentries stop talking. And he had become aware, without hearing anything, that there was someone in the tent.

He had looked up. There was a figure standing near the door, between him and the lamp. Very tall. Not a man he knew. Not, he thought, a man. He could not afterwards have said what it looked like, only that it was large and dark and strange and that it stood quite still and looked at him.

“I asked it what it was,” Brutus said. “Man or god. And what it wanted with me.”

Cassius waited.

“It said, *I am your evil spirit, Brutus. You will see me at Philippi.*” Brutus’s voice did not change. “And I said, *Then I will see you.*”

“And then?”

“And then it was gone. I called the slaves. They had seen nothing. Heard nothing. The guard at the door had been awake all night and seen nothing go in or out.” He looked at his hands. “I sat until the lamp went out. Then I came here.”

Cassius put down his stylus.

He did not believe in ghosts. He had stopped believing in the gods in any way that mattered somewhere in the desert after Carrhae, watching the Parthians do what they did, and had found in Epicurus, afterwards, a cool clean house to live in: atoms and the void, and the gods, if there were any, far away and indifferent, and death nothing to us, because where death is we are not. He had an answer for this. He had had it ready, in a sense, for twenty years.

So he gave it. He gave it gently, which surprised them both.

The senses deceive, he said. Not out of malice; out of looseness. The mind is a wax tablet that never quite smooths, and when the body is tired the old marks show through. Images come off things all the time, thin films, like the skins snakes leave, and float in the air and drift into the eyes and the mind when the mind is open and has no guard on it. A man who sleeps an hour a night and works the rest, and has been carrying the Ides and Porcia and Xanthus and Decimus and Cicero on his back for two years, will see things in a low lamp at the third watch. Of course he will. It would be stranger if he didn’t. There are no spirits, good or evil. There is no one waiting at Philippi but Antony and the boy, and they are quite bad enough.

“And the name?” Brutus said. “Why would a film off a tired mind know the name of a town in Macedonia?”

“Because you know it.” Cassius pulled the map across the table, the one he had been working from all week, and turned it round and laid his finger on the place. “We’ve talked of nothing else for a month. Where the road runs between the mountain and the marsh. Where we’ll stand if they come at us. You’ve had Philippi in your ears every day since Sardis. It’s in mine. If I saw a ghost tonight it would say Philippi too.” He took his finger off the map. “It would also, I expect, ask me about the grain.”

Brutus smiled at that, a little, which was what Cassius had wanted.

They sat for a while. The light outside was changing. Somewhere on the beach a centurion began shouting at the men loading the first ship, and the familiar noise of a camp waking came up the slope and into the tent and filled it, and the thing Brutus had brought in with him seemed to grow smaller in the daylight, the way such things do.

“You don’t believe it,” Brutus said at last.

“I don’t believe anything I can’t count.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No,” Cassius said. “It isn’t.”

Brutus stood up. He looked better. He thanked Cassius, formally, the way he thanked everyone, and went out barefoot over the matting into the morning to dress and see to his legions, and Cassius sat alone with the map.

He had told the truth. He did not believe in spirits. The senses deceived; the tired mind made shapes. He had said so, and it was sound doctrine, and he would have said it again.

He looked at the place on the map where his finger had been. The road between the mountain and the marsh. Two hills, one on each side of it. If it came to that, he would want the southern one, above the marsh, near the sea, where the ships could feed him.

He found he did not want to be the one to tell the men about Brutus’s visitor, and that he would not tell them, and that he did not want Brutus to tell them either. He did not examine why. He picked up the grain tallies again and went on adding. The columns came out right. They always did at this hour.

# SC-90: Dust

POV: Antony / F3 · Philippi plain · first engagement
Date / place: 3 Oct 42 · Philippi plain, marsh edge
Canon refs: A14 · M18 setup · fog pack, no fake OB · SIDE-1b OPEN-0 echo only
Info ENTER: Two camps · EXIT: Fog; Cassius’ sector collapses *in reports*
Overhear risk: Dust/noise

---

### Antony

Antony liked the plain’s honesty: kill or be rearranged. Marshes and hills broke sightlines; he liked that too, when it broke the other man’s map.

They had sat down on two hills astride the road west, Brutus on the northern one, Cassius on the southern above the marsh, with the sea behind them bringing grain every morning. Antony had the plain and bread for less than a month. So he had given his men spades. For ten nights they had cut a causeway through the reeds toward Cassius’s flank, as quiet as men can be up to the thigh in water. This morning Cassius had seen it, and Antony had stopped being quiet.

He rode the near wing until the dust made faces into guesses. “Weight left,” he told a tribune, hand chopping air. “Do not chase the pretty gap. Fill it. Timing, not poetry.” The tribune’s mouth opened on a question; Antony was already past him. Omens were for the tents. On the field he trusted weight and the sound of men who still obeyed.

Dust coated his teeth. He spat and pointed and the pointing became cohorts moving, not clean, not parade, but moving. Someone shouted Cassius’s name from a direction that might have been victory or panic. Antony didn’t pause to translate the shout. Another came down the line from the north, carried by a rider with no helmet: Brutus’s men were in the boy’s camp, cutting up the tents. The boy had a fever and Agrippa. Let them keep their own camp.

A standard dipped, rose, dipped again in the marsh glare. Antony cursed once, short, practical, and sent a second line into the dip before the dip became a story.

He did not think of Italian acres. For one breath a courier’s report from weeks ago surfaced anyway. Solvent men sleeping badly on neighbours’ soil. Auction quarrels nobody settled. Antony shoved it under the next order. Italy would complain. Italy always complained. The plain was louder.

### Laberius

Dust found every gap in Laberius’s teeth; the eagle staff was the only upright fact he trusted. The young Caesar’s men had been lent to the spades, so it was Antony’s marsh his century stood in this morning. Behind him, from the north, the shouting said the boy’s own camp was gone, tents, mules and Laberius’s spare tunic with it.

He saw a signal, or thought he did: flags in dust, an eagle dipping toward a ditch that might have been a marsh yesterday. Vibius yelled that Cassius’s side was broken. Someone else yelled the opposite with equal faith and a different god’s name attached. Laberius believed the yell nearest his ear and the ache in his forearms.

He kept his century’s shield-line from becoming a rumour of its own. A javelin stuck in dirt near his foot; he left it. Men who stopped for trophies got killed.

His own eagle still stood. He put his hand on the staff once: wood, sweat, the crack that had been there since Mutina’s ditch. He kept it there while the shouts about Cassius’s hill cancelled each other. He did not know Cassius’s hill. He knew thirst and the next order that might be wrong.

Pay would come later, if later existed: farms promised by dead men and living boys. For now the staff fit in a hand. That was enough for the dust.

# SC-91: Mistaken Loss

POV: Cassius · his hill/camp · M18 information kills
Date / place: 3 Oct 42 · Cassius’s hill, few witnesses
Canon refs: M18 · A14
Info ENTER: Incomplete report · EXIT: Suicide on false belief
Overhear risk: Few

---

The messenger’s mouth was wrong before the words were.

Dust made the man’s tongue thick; fear made it thicker. He had lost a sandal somewhere on the climb; one foot bled into the packed dirt of Cassius’s forward post. The map in Cassius’s head hungered for confirmation anyway. The first report said *lost* in a shape that matched Cassius’s worst sketch of the day. Standards gone. Wing broken. Brutus somewhere beyond the marsh and silent. Cassius’s hand found a tablet edge and gripped until the wax remembered his thumb.

“Say it again,” Cassius said. “Slower. Names. Directions. Which standard.”

The messenger tried. Names arrived missing: a century mark without a cognomen, a hill that might have been the left ditch. Directions softened into gestures at dust. A standard might have fallen or only dipped. Cassius made him point at the wax sketch of the sector. The finger landed between two lines and stayed there, trembling.

Cassius sent a runner down the reverse slope for a second count: cohort numbers, eagle sightings, anything with a body attached. He checked the water jars himself while he waited. Half full. He moved a shield that did not need moving. Do the next countable thing while the uncountable arrives.

The runner came back empty-mouthed. No second count. Only another man who had heard the first yell secondhand and wanted to be useful by repeating it louder. A friend, attendant, someone with too much loyalty and not enough eyes, tried to amend the first report and only added fog. Brutus’s success, if it existed beyond the lying air, did not fit in either throat.

“Brutus,” Cassius said. One clarifying question about the sector. The answer came as a shrug wearing words: *no word; marsh between; dust.*

Cassius, who had trusted numbers, trusted a broken number because broken numbers still looked like numbers if you needed them enough to hate delay. Incomplete had always been his enemy in other men’s mouths. Now it lived in his own ears. He looked once toward the dust where Brutus might still be winning and saw only glare, nothing he could count, nothing he could punish.

He almost waited. Waiting was the mathematician’s virtue. Waiting here meant listening to more mouths invent geometry. He felt the old contempt for delay rise and catch on something softer: hope, or the habit of correcting clerks before they ruined a sum. For three breaths he held both. The incomplete report as a sum that would not clear. And the possibility that Brutus was still rearranging the other half of the plain without him.

The softer thing lost.

He dismissed the messengers with a bow that meant *done*. With only two men left near him, whose faces already disagreed about everything except the steel, he called Pindarus over. He had kept the freedman at his elbow since Carrhae, since the desert where he had watched what the Parthians did with Crassus’s head, for this one errand. Pindarus held the blade where Cassius set it. Cassius made the end himself rather than wait for the plain to invent a worse one.

He didn’t speechify liberty. He didn’t correct the map. He treated the incomplete report as closed by belief. He closed the account the only way left.
