# SC-31: Hold the Consul

POV: Trebonius / Decimus · Portico of Pompey
Date / place: 13–14 March · colonnade shade
Canon refs: CHOREO-1 · M6 · spare decision
Info ENTER: Spare decision known to few · EXIT: Trebonius tasked to delay Antony
Overhear risk: Medium–Low

---

“Keep him talking,” Decimus said.

They stood where Pompey’s stone still watched the living arrange a hold. The Senate was to meet in the hall attached to Pompey’s theatre, because the old Curia was still being rebuilt, and Pompey’s statue stood there looking on, the dead rival presiding over the day his conqueror would die. Trebonius nodded like a man accepting a boring errand while his eyes did the opposite. His face had the mildness of someone who had been consul under Caesar and still knew how to make business smell like friendship. Caesar had rewarded him with the office and trusted him with Spain; Trebonius had accepted both and had gone on owing neither.

“Gaul talk,” Decimus went on. “Debt talk. The sort of talk that makes a consul lean in. Hold him in the portico until the room inside has finished being a Senate.”

The consul was Mark Antony, who held the highest office in the Republic alongside Caesar and was, by size and loyalty, the one man in Rome who might have turned the morning into a brawl. Antony was big, loud, and devoted to Caesar the way a large dog is devoted: by instinct and at volume. If he walked into the chamber, the knives would have to go through him first. So he must not walk in.

“And if he smells it?” Trebonius asked.

“Then you invent a longer hold,” Decimus said. “More debt. Another remount. Whatever keeps him facing you.”

“He likes horses.”

“Everyone knows he likes horses. Be shocked about the price of one.”

“I am always shocked about the price of one,” Trebonius said. “It is the only honest part of my character.”

Glaucia, Trebonius’s attendant, waited just inside polite earshot with the patience of men who misread shouts for sport, and who would later sell whatever soft version of this huddle paid best. Decimus did not look at him. Looking would have made the true errand a performance.

“Cover,” Trebonius murmured, for Glaucia’s benefit as much as Decimus’s. “I lingered for provincial papers. I lingered because Antony likes to be lingered with. I lingered because the morning was cold.”

“Any of those,” Decimus said. “None of the true one.”

“I will tell it in the order of increasing boredom. Nobody has ever accused a man of murder for being boring.”

“That is because boring men are never believed capable of it,” Decimus said. “Which is why you are here.”

They parted with the clasp of men confirming a hunt, festival-brother hands still warm for anyone watching. Ten paces on, Trebonius’s mildness thinned for Glaucia alone. “If anyone asks, I am a bore with Gaul on my tongue. If anyone listens well, I am already tired of being the polite half of other men’s courage.”

Glaucia’s mouth twitched. “Bores survive the Ides.”

“Bores,” Trebonius said, “also hold consuls while better men invent their knives.” He sipped nothing from an empty hand and went to practice a longer hold. The spare-Antony mercy now had a body attached: Trebonius’s voice as a leash.

# SC-32: Blind Calm

POV: Cicero · villa letter desk
Date / place: 13–14 March · afternoon
Canon refs: digest pattern · C7 · B3
Info ENTER: Elite rumour only · EXIT: Still not ops
Overhear risk: N/A

---

Cicero wrote to Atticus about games.

Atticus was his oldest friend and his banker of the mind, the rich, cautious man in Athens or Epirus or wherever he had decided that month to be safe, to whom Cicero sent his real opinions because nobody else would keep them. The letters were Cicero’s habit as other men keep a temper: daily, compulsively, with relief.

Not only games: also a cousin’s lawsuit, a bookish slight, the eternal question of whether Rome still deserved his lungs. Games sat in the ink like a man pretending the city was still a holiday problem. Elite rumour had brought him kingship talk, Sibylline trial balloons, Calpurnia’s household nerves as gossip with dinner. None of it arrived as a roster. None of it named a morning in Pompey’s Curia.

“The city is theatrical,” he wrote, then sharper: “Theatre is what remains when men of standing refuse to stage a Republic.”

He read it back and was pleased. That was the danger of Cicero: he was nearly always pleased by the sentence in front of him, and rarely asked what it was for.

He considered a sentence about Caesar’s departure east and the offices stacked like bricks ahead of lesser men. Caesar was to leave for Parthia within days, and had already arranged who would hold the consulships and magistracies for years in his absence, as if the Republic were a farm he was leasing out. He considered, briefly, whether Brutus looked tired at last dinner. Tired men were everywhere in March; that proved nothing.

Sphaerus, his secretary, had brought a note from Hirtius’s orbit about nothing that mattered. Hirtius was Caesar’s staff officer, a consul-designate, a gentleman and a gourmand, and Cicero’s most useful friend inside Caesar’s camp, the man he gave dinner lessons in oratory in return for gossip. Cicero answered with courtesy that congratulated itself. He didn’t know Trebonius was learning how to hold a consul with talk. He didn’t know Decimus had become the man who could still walk Caesar in. He believed, with the confidence that had always been half his talent, that if blades were coming they would first have to pass through a speech he had not yet written.

“You look pleased, master,” said Sphaerus.

“I am composing a sentence about the death of the Republic,” Cicero said. “It is going quite well.”

“Is the Republic dead?”

“Not until I have finished the sentence,” said Cicero.

He sealed the letter. Sealing felt like action. In the garden a thrush made a sound like a hinge. Cicero took it for spring.

# SC-33: Land Powder

POV: F3 Laberius · Italian winter quarter talk
Date / place: Mid-March-ish · colony rumour around a brazier
Canon refs: FS10 · B4
Info ENTER: Love of Caesar + unpaid expectation · EXIT: Soft powder for post-Ides rage
Overhear risk: High among soldiers

---

Sextus Laberius Cordus loved Caesar the paymaster more than Caesar the god.

He was a veteran, one of the legionaries who had marched through Gaul and Spain and Africa on Caesar’s word and Caesar’s pay, and who now sat in a camp or a settlement waiting to be turned from soldier into farmer. In Roman custom a legion that won was paid off with land, and land had been promised in huge quantities: colonies founded, plots surveyed, farms taken from the losing side and handed to the winners. The promise was good. The delivery was slow.

Around the brazier the talk was colonies and delays. Who had been promised land near Capua, the rich farming country south of Rome. Who had heard the promise thin out into “after Parthia.” Who still had a father’s farm seized in someone else’s victory. Spain’s mud still lived in Laberius’s boots as memory: mopping finished, donative not yet dated. The donative was the cash gift a general paid his men after a victory, and Caesar’s was late. Vibius, his optio, the sergeant who kept the centurion’s orders and the rest of the men’s tempers, spat and said philosophers in Rome were weighing crowns while veterans weighed empty hands. Gaius of the split sandal held his palms to the heat and asked whether Parthia paid in dirt or in postponement.

“He pays,” Laberius said. “When the eagle eats, we eat.”

The eagle was the legion’s standard, the gilded bird carried at the head of the column, and the men swore by it the way a household swore by its hearth.

“When the eagle flies east,” Vibius answered, “we count dirt.”

Gaius did not grin. “Capua was a date once. Now it’s after Parthia. After Parthia is not a place.”

“It is a very large place,” said Laberius. “It has a great many men who didn’t come back from it.”

“That is the joke, then.”

Vibius poked the brazier with a stick until ash rose. “Dirt without coin is still a grave with opinions. Write that on a promise and see who salutes.”

“I would salute the promise if it were wearing boots,” said Gaius.

Laberius didn’t hate the Senate as an idea. He hated unpaid gaps. Anger lived in unpaid gaps. He didn’t know a funeral would be the match. He only knew the powder was soft underfoot already, and soft powder still burned.

Someone sang a dirty song about kings that borrowed the Forum’s February panic and made it tavern-shaped. Laberius did not laugh. Capua unpaid was not a joke tonight, only powder waiting for a name he still trusted on the wind.

# SC-34: GO

POV: Brutus / Cassius · closed room (D)
Date / place: Night of 13–14 March
Canon refs: App. 2.113 energy · matrix · hour map
Info ENTER: Final roster · EXIT: GO; daggers ready · overhear near-zero
Overhear risk: Must be near-zero

---

Names checked. Roles set, almost.

Tillius for the petition, brother’s exile on the tongue, hands ready to insult with touch. Lucius Tillius Cimber, a senator whose brother Caesar had exiled, was to approach Caesar with a petition to pardon the brother. It was a respectable request, and a respectable request is the best cover for a crowd of men to gather round a chair. When Caesar refused, Tillius would seize his toga and pull it down from the neck, and that tug was the signal. Casca for first ugly courage: Publius Servilius Casca was to strike first, the nerve the others needed someone else to spend. Decimus for the door of trust: he would walk Caesar in. Trebonius for Antony: the hold in the portico. The Cascas, Cimber, the Appian edges who had said yes without making speeches: present as weight, not as a hymn. Galba still owed him a brother’s winter for a torn patron seal. Cassius did not ask whether the grain debt had already called him away from rock and blade.

Cassius’s finger stopped on one blank place in the list he kept only in his head. “Ligarius’s cousin,” he said. “The one who swore he would stand at the door with a message if the Forum thickened. Ligarius’s pride was supposed to buy us that body. He sent fever instead. Or a man who smelled like fever.”

“Ligarius is sick?” Brutus said.

“Ligarius is always sick when there is work. It is a very reliable fever. It comes with a courtesy note.”

“He was Pompey’s man once. He came back and was pardoned. I would not blame him for wanting to stay out of it.”

“I do not blame him,” Cassius said. “I only note that fever has never once fallen on a man at a banquet.”

Brutus waited. Missing men made rooms louder. Outside the door a creditor’s soft cough advertised itself, polite, exact, the Kalends still smiling in a throat. The Kalends was the first of the month, the day when interest fell due and men paid or were shamed, and the cough wanted an answer as much as the blank line did. Brutus gave neither.

“We do not wait for cousins,” Cassius said. “We redistribute the door. If he talks before dawn, fever takes him and we take the door. We do not invent another postponement for a man who already chose not to stand.”

Brutus’s money sat outside the door like a creditor not invited. A note had arrived that afternoon, polite, exact, a lender who still smiled when he named the Kalends. Brutus had not answered. He could still walk out of this room, pay the man, dine as a spared philosopher, and keep the masks as decoration instead of law.

“If anyone talks between now and dawn,” Cassius said, “we are not liberators. We are a joke that bleeds.”

Brutus looked at his own hands. They were clean. He rubbed a thumb across the palm. Dirt might appear and explain him. It did not. “For the Republic,” he said, and the sentence landed thinner than the unpaid note outside the door.

Cassius did not soften. “Enough delay. Parthia will not wait for our conscience. Neither will your lender. Choose which fear runs you.”

“You make me sound like a man with only two fears.”

“I make you sound like a man with a calendar,” said Cassius.

The room was small enough that fear had to stand close. Brutus’s hesitation lasted a breath past comfort, then released.

“GO,” Brutus said.

Not inventory. Choice. The word tasted like metal and unpaid interest.

Cassius’s nod was small. Small nods moved mornings. He named the redistributed door once, checked Trebonius’s leash in principle, and stopped talking before talk became a way to unmake the lock.

Brutus nodded once. The ancestral gesture: the nod of a Junius, a family that boasted the first Brutus who expelled the kings. He didn’t sleep well after. Sleep was for mornings that weren’t knives. Cassius slept worse and called it planning.

They left the room separately. Outside, a baker’s boy argued ordinary March prices with a customer and, in the same breath, that Caesar had already slipped the city for Parthia in the night, rumour with flour on it, already wrong. Neither man looked twice at the two who had just spent a breath on knives.
