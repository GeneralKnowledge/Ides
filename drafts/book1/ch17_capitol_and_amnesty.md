# SC-45 — The Joy That Does Not Arrive

POV: Brutus  
Date / place: Capitol (N5) · 15 March afternoon  
Canon refs: strategic fail · *Att.* 15.1a seed  
Info ENTER: Liberty script ready; dagger still metaphorically warm  
Info EXIT: Waits for popular joy that does not come clean; begins to understand the city did not read the same text  
Overhear risk: Crowd below partial

---

The Capitol was height, and height was supposed to mean clarity.

Brutus stood where Rome could see him if Rome wished to look up. Some of the Liberators still held their blades openly—theatre of virtue. Cassius kept his voice low and his eyes on approaches. Decimus was already half elsewhere in his mind: Gaul, legions, the practical after.

Brutus spoke. He heard himself speak. The words were right—*libertas*, tyrant, law, ancestors—each one a stone from a good road. Below, the Forum did not become a choir. It became a rumour mill with legs. Cheers happened in patches and died. Someone shouted for Caesar. Someone shouted for the dead man’s veterans. Someone shouted for grain as if grain were a consul.

“They will come round,” said a man beside Brutus who needed it to be true.

Brutus watched a knot of women shove a knot of men and felt the first cold thought that was not philosophical: *we have killed a man the city loved more than it loves our vocabulary.*

He did not say it. Saying it would make it a motion.

A runner brought nonsense: Antony dead; Antony marching; Lepidus burning houses; Cicero already writing the age. Brutus filed each report under *incomplete*. He had been trained for incompleteness in books. The street’s incompleteness had teeth.

Toward evening the light went copper. The Capitol’s stone held heat. Brutus’ hands smelled of metal no matter how often he washed them in a basin someone provided like a sacrament.

He drafted, in his head, the speech he might later write down so the day would have a cleaner spine than it deserved. Writing was how you forced the Republic to sit still.

Cassius said, “We cannot sleep on this rock and call it victory.”

“We can call it beginning,” Brutus said.

Cassius’ mouth twitched. “Beginnings bleed.”

Below them, Rome did not kneel. It shopped, wept, armed, and waited to see who paid.

# SC-46 — Force Available

POV: Antony  
Date / place: Campus / city troop orbit near Lepidus · late 15 March  
Canon refs: M11 · FT14  
Info ENTER: Caesar dead; Antony alive; armed force nearby  
Info EXIT: Negotiation chosen over instant massacre  
Overhear risk: Officers

---

Antony’s first clear desire was to kill everyone in a toga who had ever smiled at Brutus.

His second, slower desire—the one that kept him consul instead of a corpse in an alley—was to count who still had swords that would move when he pointed.

Lepidus’ men were a fact in the city’s body. Facts could be marched into the Forum and made into a butcher’s festival. M. Pacuvius—scar through the eyebrow, Antony’s old donative contact—said as much, hopeful as a dog.

“Give the word,” Pacuvius said. “We take the Capitol before dark.”

Antony looked at the man’s hope and found it childish. Childish things started civil wars before you had a will, a funeral, and a story.

“And then what?” Antony asked. “We paint the rock with senators and wake up owning a hungry city that hates us for the paint?”

“They killed him,” Pacuvius said.

“I noticed.” Antony’s voice came out flatter than grief. Grief would come when he had time to drink it properly. “They also left me alive. That was their first gift. I am not going to repay it by becoming their excuse.”

Lepidus spoke carefully, as Lepidus always did when weight leaned on him—not weak, not heroic, only present where maps got drawn. “Negotiation preserves options.”

“Negotiation preserves my throat,” Antony said. “And yours. And the chance to make the people choose us when they see the body.”

He did not yet know how he would show the body. He knew only that a corpse in the right light was worth more than a thousand virtuous daggers on a hill.

Rumour had him dead in the Subura. He almost liked that. Let the bakers bury him; he would arrive at his own funeral as a miracle with a consul’s temper.

“Hold the men,” Antony told Pacuvius. “Feed them. Promise them nothing you can’t spell. Tomorrow we talk in rooms with doors.”

Pacuvius looked cheated. Good. Cheated soldiers still listened. Satisfied soldiers invented orders.

Antony spat blood from a bitten cheek he did not remember biting and stared toward the Capitol as if he could collapse it with contempt.

“You wanted liberty,” he said to the hill, too soft for officers. “Congratulate yourselves. Now learn the price of a crowd.”

# SC-47 — Brokerage

POV: Cicero  
Date / place: Temple / meeting spaces · 16–17 March 44  
Canon refs: amnesty trajectory · acts ratified trap · *Att.* retrospective energy without future-wink  
Info ENTER: Chaos; Liberators on Capitol; Caesarians armed in potential  
Info EXIT: Amnesty path; Caesar’s acts to stand — seed of later trap; Cicero feels useful  
Overhear risk: Elite high

---

Cicero arrived at each room as if rooms had asked for him personally. Some had. Panic made men send for words the way fever made them send for doctors.

“If we do not settle this,” he told a cluster of faces that wanted settling, “we will have a second war before we have buried the first man’s meat.”

He enjoyed—privately, shamefully—the return of necessity. Necessity had always been his favourite climate. In necessity, eloquence looked like courage.

Brutus’ people wanted praise and protection. Antony’s people wanted the dictator’s papers to remain law so the city did not tip into a void where every debt could be renegotiated with a knife. Cicero heard both and performed the old trick: show each side the part of its face that could still be called Republic.

Amnesty. The word went around the marble like wine. Amnesty for the killers; validity for Caesar’s acts. A bargain with two handsome hands and a hidden mouth.

Cicero said, “Peace is not surrender to tyranny. Peace is the space in which law returns.”

Someone asked if Caesar’s arrangements—colonies, magistracies, the sticky gifts already promised—would stand.

“If they fall,” Cicero said, quick, “half of Italy stands up with a claim and a torch. We are not children.”

He did not hear, in his own sentence, any trap’s hinge. He heard prudence—and liked the sound of himself hearing it. He saw Atticus in his mind as audience and felt the old warmth of being the man who could still arrange a city with clauses.

A messenger muttered that Antony had not stormed the Capitol.

“Good,” Cicero said. “Then we still have a consul who prefers a forum to a pyre.”

He went home—or to someone’s home that would do—and dictated notes until his hand cramped. Outside, Rome buried rumour under rumour. Cicero slept two hours and dreamed he was consul again, which was not a dream so much as a habit.

On the seventeenth, when the shape of settlement had begun to harden, he allowed himself a thin satisfaction. The state had not immediately become a slaughterhouse. That was a kind of immortality.

He did not stand in the future and watch the funeral. He stood in the present and congratulated the present on taking his advice.
