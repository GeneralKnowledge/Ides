# SC-20: Mandate

POV: Cassius · house / portico
Date / place: Days after Lupercalia · Cassius’ portico, late afternoon
Canon refs: A4 · FT3 · matrix PERIPH
Info ENTER: Diadem public fact · EXIT: Recruitment mandate personal; Galba priced
Overhear risk: Medium

---

Cassius treated the Lupercalia as proof the disease had symptoms in daylight.

The diadem had been offered, refused, offered again, theatre that left his mouth dry and metallic. In the street men still argued whether Caesar had wanted the crown or wanted the refusal. Cassius didn’t argue. The Forum would not vote a fever down.

The clock was simple, and he said it to himself every morning like a prayer. Caesar would leave for the East within weeks. When he went, he would take his legions, his Senate and his promises with him, and when he came back, if he came back, he would come as the man who had beaten Parthia, and no one in Rome would be in a position to say no to him again. If anything was going to be done, it would be done while he was still within reach of a Roman street.

He walked his own portico until the tiles knew his step. Galba’s name sat in his mouth like a stone he meant to spit into the right palm. Ligarius next: gratitude was a door that opened from the inside. Then the Appian shortlist’s edges, a list he kept in his head, of men whose villas lined the old southern road where the pardoned Pompeians had retired to be grateful in private. Men who hated the calendar of forever offices. Men who still smelled Pharsalus on their clothes. Men who smiled too hard at Caesarian dinners.

A clerk hovered with a tablet about a boundary dispute in some dull estate. Cassius took it, read nothing, handed it back.

“Not now,” he said. “Send to Atticus’s people if they want Latin about ditches.”

“Master, it concerns the drainage.”

“Then let the drainage wait. It has been waiting since Romulus.”

He called for wine and didn’t drink it. He wanted his hands free.

Servius Galba came at the hour Cassius had named, not early, not late, the etiquette of a man who still thought timing was honour. They stood where the street noise could steal a sentence if a neighbour’s ear got hungry.

“You saw the Forum,” Cassius said.

“I saw a joke that forgot it was a joke,” Galba answered.

Cassius watched Galba’s hands: no cup, no fidget, one ring turned once and stopped. “We need men who can stand in a room and finish what a petition starts.”

Galba’s mouth tightened. Not refusal. Calculation. “And Brutus?”

“Brutus is the brand,” Cassius said. “Brands do not recruit. They bless. I recruit. Tonight I make him listen with company, so he cannot pretend it was only my temper.”

“His temper is exactly what he will say it was.”

“Then he will have to say it to Casca.”

A slave crossed the far end of the portico with a basket. Both men spoke for a breath about rain. The basket passed.

Galba did not leave clean. He took from his sleeve a small note, wax already soft from being carried too long, and held it where Cassius could see the seal of a Caesarian patron who still paid Galba’s brother’s grain debts.

“If I tap doors for you,” Galba said, quiet, “this man hears a silence where my gratitude used to live. He will not forgive silence. He will call in the brother’s debt before the next Kalends.”

“Then choose which debt you prefer,” Cassius said. “Grain that keeps a brother soft, or a city that keeps making kings out of jokes.”

Galba’s thumb cracked the seal. He tore the note once, then again, until the patron’s compliments were scraps too small to reassemble into courtesy. He let them fall between the tiles. “Name the doors,” he said. “Softly. If Brutus blesses, I stand. If he only watches, I still stand, but you will owe me the brother’s winter.”

Cassius named two other doors Galba might tap, and did not name the full list. Compartments kept corpses from becoming libraries. He did not promise the brother’s winter with poetry. He nodded once, as men nod when a cost has entered the room and refused to leave.

When Galba reached the street, Cassius finally drank. The wine was ordinary. That pleased him.

# SC-21: The Room That Would Not Let Him Postpone

POV: Brutus · domus · Cassius + Casca + Servilia
Date / place: Same week · Brutus’ house, after dinner lamps
Canon refs: D11 · ancestral script · C2
Info ENTER: Hesitation / spoken resistance · EXIT: Costly inch toward commitment (not clean yes)
Overhear risk: High (Servilia); household

---

Porcia had gone to bed with a headache that sat behind the eyes like watchfulness. The masks on the wall did what they always did: judged without moving. Servilia remained at the dining room’s edge, wine untouched.

Cassius arrived with Publius Servilius Casca on the threshold as if they had met by chance. They had not. Brutus saw the choice at once: Casca was a Servilius, his mother’s name, and a Servilius could not be turned away at this door. He saw the rest too: Cassius had brought a second voice so the evening could not become a private essay.

“We disturb a quiet house,” Cassius said, formal enough for station.

“Quiet houses are where Rome invents its courage,” Servilia answered, equally formal. She did not rise to kiss cheeks. She let them stand.

Brutus did not offer couches at once. Offering couches was how he postponed. “If this is about dinners,” he said, “I have already burned one invitation this month. I can burn another.”

Casca smiled with his teeth. “We are not here to dine you into a better mood. We are here because the Forum still tastes of that wreath, and men who wait for Parthia will wake up subjects.”

“Subjects is a street word,” Brutus said. “Speak like the Senate, or leave my masks out of it.”

“The masks are not in the Senate.”

“The masks are in every Senate that ever mattered. That is the point of masks.”

Cassius set his cup down without drinking. “You told me law’s face. I am bringing you law’s face with a witness. Casca can stand in a room when a petition becomes a finished act. I cannot bless this alone, your name is the only one that makes a surgery look like tradition.”

“Then find another tradition,” Brutus said, and heard his own voice go sharp. “I will not be the respectable front for a gang. Caesar spared me. That spare still sits in this house whether we like the masks or not.” He hated how thin *debt* sounded. The man had not only signed a tablet. He had kept a place for him at ordinary tables afterward, hand on his shoulder like kinship that politics had not earned. Killing that hand later would not be surgery in the blood. It would be answering warmth with steel and calling the answer law.

Servilia’s eyes flicked once to the masks, then to Cassius. “Debts to living men are not the same as debts to names. Do not confuse the two while my son is still practising courtesy as a career.”

“You are very fond of my courtesy, mother.”

“I have invested in it.”

Casca leaned forward. “Courtesy is how we all stayed alive. It is also how the diadem got a second cheer. I am tired of surviving beautifully.”

Brutus walked the corridor strip where the older Brutus lived in paint. He wanted the ancestors to forbid him. Paint forbade nothing useful. The shoulder-touch from the spared years sat in his muscle anyway, closer than paint, harder to draft away. For a breath he let himself picture Caesar’s face if the door ever opened with a knife behind it, and the picture hurt enough that he almost ordered them out for that reason alone.

“If it is done as murder,” he said, back still half-turned, “I am not with you. If it is done as a street, I am against you. I have not said yes. I have said I hate kings. Hating kings is not the same as opening a friend’s door with a knife behind it.”

Silence held long enough for a slave’s foot to hesitate at the curtain and retreat.

Cassius did not soften. Softening made Brutus write periods. “Then hear this without poetry: we will choose a morning with you or without you. Without you, it still happens, and then the city will ask where the Brutus was while lesser men did the ugly arithmetic. With you, you can still demand surgery. Refuse the room entirely, and you get to write essays about Cato while Casca counts mornings.”

Casca added, almost mild, “I prefer your essays to your absence. Absence looks like approval of whatever we become.”

Brutus’s hands found the side table and the polite refusal draft still waiting, another dinner, another crown-talker. He did not burn it yet. Burning in front of them would have been theatre.

“I will not recruit,” he said. “I will not smile men into this. If, if, there is a Senate face to it, I will hear the plan. I will not hear massacre. I will not hear Antony stacked with the rest like cordwood. That is not courage. That is panic with ancestors painted on it.”

Cassius’s jaw worked once, interest stored. “Spare arguments later. Hearing is enough for tonight.”

Servilia rose. Her hand brushed Brutus’s shoulder, brief, private, not a blessing for Cassius to claim. “Worthy men sleep badly,” she said to the room, then to Cassius with perfect courtesy: “You have been heard. The house is tired.”

They left with festival manners intact, clasps, wishes for health, and Casca’s very old joke about the mule elected tribune that declined the honour because it would have to speak in public. Servilia’s mouth almost moved.

When the door had shut on them, Brutus held the refusal draft over the lamp until names blurred. He set it down carefully. He had not said yes. He had said he would hear, and the hearing already felt like a door he could not fully close.

He slept little. When he did, the Forum would not stop cheering, and a crown kept returning like a fly.
