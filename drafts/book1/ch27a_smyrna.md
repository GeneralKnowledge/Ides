# SC-74a: Good Walls

POV: F5 Amyntas · Smyrna
Date / place: January 43 · the city treasury; the walls; the agora
Canon refs: T10 · App. *BC* 3.26 · Cic. *Phil.* 11.5–9 · Dio 47.29
Info ENTER: Trebonius governs Asia; Dolabella marching through to Syria · EXIT: Trebonius killed in the night; his head on the governor’s chair
Overhear risk: Clerks; Dolabella’s soldiers

---

Amyntas son of Menodotus had kept the treasury of Smyrna for eleven years, and in all that time he had never been asked to feed an army that was outside the walls on purpose.

“Not in,” the proconsul said. “Under no circumstances in. But fed. Generously. I want him to remember us fondly when he’s in Syria.”

Gaius Trebonius said this in good Greek, with a Roman accent he did nothing to hide, sitting in the council chamber with his feet on a stool because the January damp got into them. He had governed Asia for eight months. Amyntas had come to like him, which he had not expected. He was a short, cheerful man who asked the price of things before he asked their history, and who had once, on a tour of the harbour, stopped to buy figs from a stall and paid what the woman asked without haggling, which had been talked about in the agora for a week.

So the treasury fed Dolabella.

Amyntas wrote it down as he did everything. Barley, so many medimni. Wine, so many jars, the second quality. Oil. Fodder for four hundred horses. Sheep, live, driven out of the north gate each morning to the camp on the plain where the consul of last year, the proconsul of Syria, the son-in-law of Cicero, sat with his legions and waited to be let in and was not. Each night the gates were shut and the watch doubled. Each morning the carts went out again. Amyntas entered the cost under a heading he invented for it, *hospitality to a passing magistrate*, and looked at it, and thought that the city had paid many kinds of hospitality to many Romans in his lifetime, and that this was the first he had been asked to pay from behind a locked gate.

Dolabella sent courteous letters. He understood entirely. He would not dream of troubling the city. He was on his way to his province and wanted only supplies and a little rest for his men. He sent the treasurer of Smyrna, by name, a cup of good silver as thanks for the barley, and Amyntas entered it in the books as a gift to the city and put it in the strongroom and did not drink from it.

On the eleventh day the camp on the plain was struck.

Amyntas watched it from the wall with half the council. It was done briskly, in the early afternoon, tents down and packed, the column formed on the Ephesus road and marching away south with its baggage and its standards, and Dolabella himself riding at the head of it in a scarlet cloak and waving to the walls as he went, the way a man waves to a house where he has dined well.

“Well,” said Trebonius, beside him, pleased. “There we are. I told you. Friendly town.”

The watch was stood down to its usual strength that night. Amyntas went home to his house below the temple of Nemesis and ate with his wife and his two daughters and went to bed early, because it had been a long eleven days, and slept, and was woken in the dark by a sound he did not at first understand.

It was the sound of a great many men moving quietly.

He went up on to his roof in his night tunic. The streets below the temple were full of soldiers. Not running. Walking fast, in file, with their shields covered and their swords drawn and nobody talking. They had come over the wall on the seaward side, where it was lowest, with ladders. Later he learned they had turned back on the Ephesus road at dusk, a few cohorts of them, and come up through the olive groves in the dark while the rest of the column went on making noise to the south. Later he learned all of it. That night he only stood on his roof and watched the files go by below, all going the same way, uphill, toward the governor’s house.

There was no fighting to speak of. There was no one to fight. The city had been told it was safe.

In the morning the soldiers were on the gates and in the agora and the city was very quiet. Amyntas dressed and went down to the treasury, because it was the treasury, and someone had to be there when they came for it. On the way he crossed the agora.

They had set up the governor’s chair on the tribunal at the top of the steps, the ivory chair Trebonius sat in to hear cases, and on the chair, where a man would sit, they had put his head.

Amyntas did not stop. He kept walking at the same pace, across the agora and up the steps of the treasury and in, and sat down at his table, and found that he had walked past it so quickly he could not have said for certain whether the eyes were open.

The clerks came in one by one. Nobody said anything for a long time. Then the youngest, who had been in the street at first light, told it, because he could not help telling it. Trebonius had been taken in his bed. He had asked them to bring him to Dolabella. A centurion had said that Dolabella was not here, but that he would take the head to him, and the rest could stay. Afterwards some of the soldiers had…

“Enough,” said Amyntas.

The boy stopped. Later Amyntas would hear other versions. That the governor had been kept alive two days and made to tell where the money was. That he had been killed at once and the two days were a story told in Rome. That the soldiers had used the head in the street like a ball, kicking it from one to another between the colonnades until there was nothing about it a wife would know. Amyntas never asked which was true. He did not go out into the street that day, or the next. He had two daughters.

Dolabella rode into the city at noon, by the main gate, which was opened for him. He wore the scarlet cloak. He went up the steps of the tribunal and stood by the chair and made a short speech to whoever was in the agora, which was not many, about Caesar, and the debt of blood owed to Caesar, and the first of the murderers to pay it. Then he sent for the city’s treasurer by name.

Amyntas went. He took the books.

“The cup,” Dolabella said pleasantly. He had a handsome flushed face and very good teeth. “I sent you a cup. I hope you liked it.”

“It is in the city’s strongroom, Imperator.”

“Good. Good. I shall want the rest of what’s in there.” Dolabella glanced at the chair beside him without interest, the way a man glances at a table that has not yet been cleared. “Asia is a rich province. Syria is a long way. I’m sure Smyrna will be generous. It was so generous all last week.”

Amyntas opened the books on the steps and began to read the figures aloud. His voice was perfectly steady. He found that if he kept his eyes on the columns of figures, the barley and the oil and the sheep, the hospitality to a passing magistrate, he did not have to see anything else at all.

# SC-74b: Enemy

POV: Cicero · Rome
Date / place: Early March 43 · the Senate; Cicero’s house after
Canon refs: T10 · Cic. *Phil.* 11 · *Att.* 14.15 (recalled)
Info ENTER: News of Smyrna reaches Rome · EXIT: Dolabella declared a public enemy; Cicero asks for Cassius to have the war
Overhear risk: The House; Tiro

---

The news from Smyrna reached Rome at the end of February in letters from Asia, and by the first of March every house on the Palatine was telling it with more blood than the last.

Cicero told it with the most. That was his work.

He stood in the House on the day of the debate and told it so that the fathers would see it: the ladders in the dark, the man in his bed, the two days, the questions, the chair, the head. He did not know that the two days were true. He knew that they were true enough to move a vote, and that a vote was what Trebonius needed now, more than accuracy. *Gaius Trebonius*, he said, *my friend*, and he let his voice catch on *friend*, and it caught by itself, which surprised him. He called Dolabella a monster, a beast in a man’s shape, a torturer, a thing that had crawled out of Caesar’s camp still wearing Caesar’s scent. He said the Senate must declare him a public enemy that day, and it did, with only a handful against.

Then he asked the House to give the war against Dolabella to Cassius, who was somewhere in Syria already with no commission from anyone, and on that he lost. The consuls wanted it for themselves, once Mutina was done. The fathers preferred a consul they could see to a Liberator they could not. Cicero went home with half a victory and the rest of his speech in his head, still talking.

Tiro had the boxes out when he came in. Tiro kept the letters, copies of everything, the outgoing and the incoming, in cedar boxes by year, so that one day there might be a book. He had the box for the year of the Ides open on the table, and he did not say anything when Cicero came in. He only moved the lamp a little nearer.

Cicero knew what was in it. He did not need to read it. *O my wonderful Dolabella. I call him mine now.* The best paragraph. The copy he had sent to the young man himself, with warm congratulations, because it was always well to be generous to a consul.

“Put it back,” he said.

Tiro put it back, and closed the box.

Cicero sat down. His throat hurt from the speech. He thought, not for the first time and with no more profit than the other times, that the young man had pulled down a column in April and cut off a head in January with exactly the same face, and that he, Cicero, had praised the face once and cursed it once and been sincere both times. He thought of Tullia at twenty-eight in her bridal veil, laughing. He still could not remember what had made her laugh. He had a feeling it had been something Dolabella said, and that it had been funny.

“The fathers will want the speech written up,” Tiro said.

“Yes.” Cicero held out his hand for a tablet. “The eleventh. Leave out the part about friend. It sounded rehearsed.”

It had not been rehearsed. That was why it had to go.
