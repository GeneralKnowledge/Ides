# SC-43a — The Petition

POV: Cassius  
Date / place: Curia of Pompey · mid-morning 15 March 44  
Canon refs: CHOREO-1.1–4 · A5 · M8  
Info ENTER: GO; Caesar seated / arriving among them; Antony outside  
Info EXIT: First blows landed; chaos begun  
Overhear risk: Extreme inside

---

Cassius counted exits the way other men counted clients.

One door. Too many togae. Pompey’s statue at the far thought of the room—enemy’s face in marble, watching. Caesar came in with that slight forward lean of a man late for his own power, Decimus’ cheer still faintly on him like perfume. He sat. Or half-sat. The golden chair made its small theatre of precedence.

Tillius Cimber went forward first, as arranged—brother’s exile on his tongue, hands already dishonest with urgency. Cassius watched Caesar’s patience perform itself: the slight turn, the listening face, the clemency brand still wet on the Republic’s memory.

*Now,* Cassius thought, not as prayer. Prayer was Brutus’ department.

Tillius’ hands found the toga. Pulled.

The signal was ugly and ordinary—cloth, fist, the insult of touch. Caesar’s surprise was real enough to please Cassius and frighten him. Real surprise meant the secret had held. Real surprise also meant a living man’s strength still in the shoulders.

Publius Casca struck—neck, shoulder, the awkward angle of a first wound made by a man who had practised on air. Caesar caught Casca’s arm. Latin burst out of him, sharp: a question, an outrage, *what is this violence* in the old register of men who still believed Senate rooms were safer than camps.

Cassius moved.

He didn't see a tyrant in that instant. He saw a body that had walked through Gaul and Alexandria and the stubborn Spanish hills, a body that might yet stand up and make this morning a botched thing. Cassius’ blade went in where flesh allowed it. Heat ran over his knuckles.

Around him the room became teeth.

Senators who were not in it screamed anyway. Benches shrieked on stone. Someone fell without being struck—panic’s own casualty. Cassius smelled blood and dust and the sweet oil in Caesar’s hair. He kept his face tight. Soft faces in a killing lost the killing.

Caesar fought. Of course he fought. The man had never learned how to be an offering.

Cassius glimpsed Brutus on the far side of the storm—pale, committed, somehow still looking like a statue asked to commit a robbery—and then Caesar’s hand was on a blade and red and the marble of Pompey was closer than it should have been.

*Finish,* Cassius told the morning. *Finish before the door brings Antony.*

# SC-43b — At the Base

POV: Brutus  
Date / place: Same minutes · Curia Pompeia  
Canon refs: CHOREO-1.5–7 · M9 · M5  
Info ENTER: Strike begun; his own oath heavy  
Info EXIT: Caesar down at Pompey’s statue; Brutus hears a sound he will not agree on later  
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

Brutus had told himself it would feel like law.

It felt like a bathhouse brawl with better cloth.

Men he respected shoved. Men he did not respect shoved harder. Caesar’s mouth opened on pain and command at once. Brutus’ ancestors—imagined, arranged on their shelves at home—did not lean in to help. They watched, as masks watch.

He had to step close. Distance was cowardice; closeness was the thing that would be written, if anything was written truly.

His blade found Caesar because the room tilted that way—bodies made a funnel toward the statue’s base. Pompey’s marble face had no advice. Brutus struck as a man strikes when the alternative is being the only clean hand in a pile of red ones—filth either way.

Caesar looked at him.

That was worse than knives.

What Caesar said—if he said—Brutus already knew he would not keep the same as the man at his shoulder. He heard something broken, breath and consonant, perhaps *kai su*, perhaps only air leaving a throat that had spoken to the whole world. Beside him someone swore he heard a child’s name. Someone else swore silence was cleaner.

Brutus did not answer. Answers were for courts.

Caesar folded at the base of Pompey’s statue as if the old dead rival had finally won a small, filthy trick. The toga drank. The floor drank. The number of wounds would become a story; Brutus did not count. Counting would keep him in the room. He needed to leave as liberty, not as butcher.

“People—Senate—” His voice sounded written. He hated that. “The tyrant is dead. There is nothing to fear—”

No one arranged themselves into a people. They ran.

Cassius’ hand found Brutus’ elbow hard enough to bruise. “Capitol,” Cassius said, practical as salt. “Before Antony finds a sword and a crowd.”

Brutus looked once more at the shape on the stones. Softness tried to rise in him—memory of dinner, of Caesar’s forgiveness after war, of the ugly debt of being spared. He crushed it the way he crushed unearned tears: privately, completely, with the lie that ancestors approved.

They went out into light that did not know what it was lighting.

# SC-44 — The Consul Is Dead (False)

POV: Nysa (F1)  
Date / place: Streets from Campus approaches → Forum fringe → Subura edge  
Canon refs: Info net · rumour mutation  
Info ENTER: Panic wave from Theatre/Campus  
Info EXIT: Carries false report that Antony is dead into denser streets  
Overhear risk: She is the overhear

---

Nysa hadn't meant to follow the litter far. Girls who followed too far became bodies in gutters. But the Domus had become unbreathable with Calpurnia’s silence, and Phileros had said, “If you are going to hover, hover where news comes,” as if news were a fishmonger’s tray.

She was still on the edge of the Campus crowd when the Curia began to spit men.

Togas with red. Togas without dignity. A senator vomited against a column and wiped his mouth like a boy. Someone screamed Caesar’s name as if names could reverse blood.

“Knives!”  
“Brutus!”  
“The consuls—both—”  
“Antony—”

The name Antony snagged. Nysa did not see Antony. She saw a gap where a large man might have been and heard, already, two freedmen inventing the rest.

“They killed the consul too,” one said, eyes bright with the pleasure of worst cases. “Had to. He was Caesar’s dog.”

“I heard Trebonius held him and they opened him in the portico,” said the other, not to be outdone.

Nysa’s mouth moved before her caution did. “Antony is dead?”

“Dead as the dictator,” the first freedman said, happy to be source. “Tell them in the Subura before the bakers lie about it.”

She ran because standing still made you a witness, and witnesses were questioned. The wax fragment under her tunic knocked against her ribs like a second heart that had failed earlier and wanted usefulness now.

In a street that smelled of garum and wet stone she told a woman with bread under her arm, “They have killed Caesar—and Antony.”

The woman clutched the bread. “Both?”

“Both,” Nysa said, and felt the story settle into the city like dust. She didn't know if it was true. Such stories travelled on the legs of people who thought they were being precise.

By the time she reached the Subura’s first choke-point, the tale had grown a Dolabella and, somehow, a list. Lists always arrived early in panics. Lists made fear feel organised.

Nysa stopped under a balcony and vomited nothing. Then she wiped her mouth, as the senator had, and hated the kinship.

She still had the wax. Incomplete. Unopened by any dictator. Around her, Rome opened every seal at once.
