# SC-65: Empty Seals

POV: F1 Nysa · Domus Publica after masters shift
Date / place: Service court / tablinum edge · M12
Canon refs: NOTE-1 residue · HOUSE-1
Info ENTER: Grief logistics · EXIT: Tablets/seals become others’ weapons; Nysa takes the new bow and serves in Antony’s house
Overhear risk: Extreme staff

---

There were men in the house whom Nysa did not know, and they had the keys.

They had come the day after the mistress’s women began to pack: a steward and two clerks, sent by the consul’s people, or by whoever the house belonged to now. Phileros said that it went with the priesthood, and the priesthood had gone to Lepidus, and Lepidus had it from Antony. She kept the first part of that and let the rest go. The steward was polite. He knocked at doors inside the house, which nobody had done before. He broke the seal on the linen cupboard with his thumbnail while he was asking her name.

“These are the people’s now,” he said, of a chest that she and Dama had carried out between them. “In a manner of speaking. You will find us careful.”

“We are grateful for the care,” Nysa said, and bowed as the atrium had taught her.

Phileros laughed. It was one short laugh. The steward looked at him, and Phileros looked down at the outer key in his own hand as if somebody else had done it. He had been drinking since the Ides. Now he began before noon.

She counted the chests out. Nobody had asked her to. She counted because she had counted the first ones, on the night of the Ides: eleven, by lamplight, which she had helped to carry as far as the gate, where the consul’s men had taken them on into the dark. *Safe*, the consul had said to the mistress. These were what safe had left behind. There were seven by midday.

Dama said from the chicken court that the hens had stopped laying out of respect. Nobody answered him.

On the stair, where the steward could not hear, Phileros said it over. “The people’s chest. The people’s hands in our cupboards. It is the chief priest’s house and they are the chief priest’s cupboards. We were the furniture of the office, girl. We were never anything else.”

She had linen over her arm. “Which cupboard is next?”

“All of them,” he said.

She still carried her left arm a little away from her side. There had been nothing under it since the night she burned the wax. She noticed it when she passed the shelf of blank tablets, and made the arm go down.

There was a cracked oil flask on the mistress’s washstand, which the mistress had used to fill with her own hands. It was worth nothing. Nysa put it behind the big water jar in the passage. The steward’s clerk found it within the hour.

“Careful includes corners,” he said, not unkindly, and set it on the people’s pile in the atrium. She saw where on the pile.

Before dusk the steward sent her across the Sacra Via with a sealed receipt for Antony’s people. “The Carinae,” he said. “Pompey’s old house. The consul’s now.” In the kitchen they said Antony had bought it at Caesar’s auction and never paid. The door she reached was not Domus. It smelled of different oil and men who borrowed authority. A clerk there took the seal without looking at her face. Then he offered, mild as a gift, that a careful girl might stay attached to the new inventory if she learned the new bow.

Nysa looked past him into the atrium. Calpurnia’s women were already chosen for wherever Calpurnia went next, and Nysa was not one of them. The Domus was going to Lepidus’s people with its cupboards. The Subura had rooms, and rooms had rent. The clerk waited the way men wait who have already counted the answer.

A kitchen girl came through the door behind her with a basket, and Nysa watched her bow to the clerk: shallower than the Domus bow, quicker, the eyes down before the head moved, a bow that took less time because in this house there were more people to give it to. Nysa gave it back to the clerk on the first try.

He nodded. “The kitchen,” he said. “Tomorrow, before light. The steward likes to know who carries.”

She walked back across the Sacra Via for her bundle while the streets were still hers to cross. The cracked flask sat on top of the people’s pile in the atrium with nobody watching it, and she put it in the bundle with her tunics. She did not tell Phileros which bow she had given. He saw it anyway in the morning, when she gave it to him at the gate out of a night’s practice, and he laughed his short laugh, and this time it was not too short.

# SC-66: Not on the Tablet

POV: Statilius · a dinner on the Quirinal
Date / place: Late April 44 · after the Liberators have left Rome
Canon refs: Plut. *Brut.* 12 (callback) · Cic. *Att.* 14.14 (men who cheered and then went quiet)
Info ENTER: The March question · EXIT: keeps his name off the open declaration; writes to Brutus as a friend, not a signer
Overhear risk: Medium

---

The tablet came round after the second course, under a napkin, as if it were something to be ashamed of, which Statilius thought was the first honest thing about it.

It had six names on it already. A declaration of support, open, for Brutus and Cassius and the rest, now that they were out of the city and the city was deciding what it thought of them. The man who carried it was a cousin of Cassius’s wife, a decent young man with a new beard, and he set it down by Statilius’s elbow with a stylus already warmed.

“You were at his house,” the cousin said. “Everyone knows you’re his friend.”

“I am his friend.”

“Then sign.”

The dinner was a good one. The host had been at the Capitol on the first night and talked about it as if he had held a sword, which he had not; he had held a lamp, and Statilius had seen him hold it. Six weeks ago every couch in Rome had been full of men like that. Statilius had watched them. They had cheered in March, and in April Antony had put a guard on his house and read Caesar’s papers aloud in the Senate, and the cheering had gone into the kind of quiet that waits to see who is winning. Some of the men who had cheered loudest had already gone to Antony’s morning reception. Statilius had seen them too, from the street.

He looked at the names. Two of them belonged to men who would be in Antony’s atrium by the Kalends.

“I won’t sign,” he said.

The cousin’s face changed. Down the couches the talk dropped, the way talk drops when a dish is sent back.

“You said at his table,” said the cousin, who had clearly been told, “that a wise man should not take risks for fools. Is that what we are?”

“That is what I said. I said it in March.” Statilius picked up the stylus and looked at it and put it down again, on the napkin, so it would not mark the couch. “I said it when he asked me a question he was not asking. I answered the question he was asking with a joke, and he was kind enough to let me. He went to the Curia without me. I will not go to the tablet after him, now that it costs nothing to sign and will cost a great deal later, and call that friendship.”

“It costs nothing?”

“Not tonight. Tonight it is a dinner.” He smiled. “Ask me when it costs something.”

The cousin took the tablet back. He did not say *coward*; he was too well brought up. He said it with his shoulders instead, going back down the couches, and the host cleared his throat and called for the next course, and the talk came back up the way water closes over a stone.

Statilius ate the next course. He made himself. A man who left a dinner early after refusing a tablet was a man who had something to be ashamed of, and he was not sure yet whether he had.

At home he did not go to bed. He sat in the small garden he had made behind the house, a few herbs and a fig tree and a bench, the way his master taught, a garden as a place to be left alone in. It was too dark to see the fig. He could smell it.

Live unnoticed. He had built his whole life on the sentence, and it had held, through Pharsalus and Africa and the pardons and the ten years and the Ides. He had never been on anyone’s list. He had never put his name on anyone’s tablet. He had been free, which his master said was the only thing worth having, and he had spent the spring watching his friends leave the city one by one by the Appian road while he stayed in his garden being free.

He called for a lamp and wax. He did not write to the Senate, or to the cousin, or to anyone who would read it aloud at a dinner. He wrote to Brutus, at Lanuvium.

*I will not sign for the Republic. I don’t believe in it, and you know I don’t, and you were right not to ask me in March. But if there is ever a thing to be done that wants a friend and not a cause, I am the same man you used to argue with, and I am not doing anything else.*

He read it over and thought it sounded like a man who had drunk the host’s wine. He sealed it anyway.

In the morning he gave it to a carrier going south and went back to his garden, and found, to his annoyance, that he could not sit in it.
