# SC-64: Laws for Sale

POV: F4 Hordeonius · basilica / Puteoli paper
Date / place: Rome stalls · TONE-1 Batiatus medium
Canon refs: *Att.* 14.12 flavour · SIDE-1 · C9
Info ENTER: Chaos premiums · EXIT: Citizenship/land “acts” priced; SIDE-1 murmur useless
Overhear risk: Medium

---

Hordeonius arranged his face into mourning for the Republic and his stylus into appetite.

“Caesar’s acts,” he told a man from Puteoli, “are a market now. A sacred market. Do not blaspheme ready coin.”

Citizenship shortcuts, land whispers, exemptions with seals that looked tired: each had a number. Hordeonius wrote numbers as priests wrote prodigies: neat, certain, slightly insane. Sosia muttered that forged acts smelled like wet leather. Hordeonius priced the smell.

“Who says what’s in Caesar’s papers?” the Puteoli man asked.

“The man whose house they’re in,” Hordeonius said.

“Antony’s,” Sosia said. “The widow sent the chests over the night of the Ides, notebooks and money both. Safer with the consul, she was told.”

“The whole of Sicily got made Roman off one posting last month,” the Puteoli man said. “Every farmer on the island. Caesar meant it, apparently. Nobody heard him say so.”

“Wholesale,” Hordeonius said. “You’re retail. An eastern king bought his kingdom back in Fulvia’s sitting-room. You want a harbour exemption. Adjust your expectations and your purse.”

The Puteoli man and Sosia argued the next question in the same breath. “Young Caesar” changed land risk, the Puteoli man insisted. Sosia said only veterans changed land risk. A third voice from two stalls down asked whether Servilia’s people were already rewriting clauses in Antium talk, and whether old eagle men would take the boy’s pay or spit it back as wine-shop joke.

“Antium?” the Puteoli man said.

“Brutus’s sea house,” Sosia said. “Where else does a praetor sit when he can’t walk the Forum? His mother has moved her clerks in after him.”

The Puteoli man wiped his brow. “You weigh a dead man like a sack of grain.”

Hordeonius’s stylus paused, then kept writing. “Then don’t mourn on my stool. I sell you which rumour empties a purse first. Certainty costs extra, and I don’t stock it.”

He priced the Puteoli question twice, once for Antony’s veterans, once for a boy’s name, and entered *boy-Caesar rumour: watch veterans* beside *Antium soft paper: watch Servilia orbit* in a margin. He almost smiled at the neatness of the entry, then stopped. Clerks who smiled at neatness became funny in taverns.

# SC-65: Empty Seals

POV: F1 Nysa · Domus Publica after masters shift
Date / place: Service court / tablinum edge · M12
Canon refs: NOTE-1 residue · HOUSE-1
Info ENTER: Grief logistics · EXIT: Tablets/seals become others’ weapons; Nysa takes the new bow and serves in Antony’s house
Overhear risk: Extreme staff

---

The Domus Publica filled with new footsteps wearing old authority.

Nysa packed what Calpurnia ordered packed and watched men she didn’t know break seals she had once guarded with her body. A steward appointed by Antony’s people, or by the house’s new legal fiction, spoke kindly while taking. Phileros hovered with the outer key. Dama watched from the chicken court. Birds made poor witnesses. Kindness that emptied chests still counted as kindness in the atrium’s mouth.

“These are the people’s now,” he said of a chest. “In a manner of speaking. You will find us careful. Careful is kindness in a house between masters.”

Nysa bowed the bow the atrium taught. “We are grateful for the care.”

Phileros laughed once, short, too short for the steward’s kindness, and then looked at the outer key as if it had changed religion. Drink made him loyal to whoever held that key. Dama claimed the chickens had gone silent out of respect. Nysa put chickens with *noise* and chests with *danger* and kept walking.

Phileros kept the short laugh for the stair, where no steward’s kindness could rewrite it. “People’s chest. People’s seals. People’s careful hands in our cupboards. Empty is empty. House goes with the priesthood, and the priesthood went to Lepidus with Antony holding the ladder. Chief priest’s house, chief priest’s cupboards. We were only ever furniture of the office.”

Nysa had carried the first chests herself as far as the gate, the night of the Ides, by lamplight, and watched Antony’s men take them into the dark. Safe, the consul had promised Calpurnia. These were the leftovers. Careful hands were collecting what safe had missed.

Dama muttered that the chickens had better taste than the new authority. Nysa did not join the joke loud enough to travel. She let it land as a breath through the nose, enough that Phileros saw he’d been heard. She thought of her burned fragment and touched the empty place in her tunic where it had sat. She hid one blank tablet there out of habit, then put it back on the shelf. Blank wax in the wrong hands had already become an entry in someone else’s daybook. The incomplete *spare*/*stay* splinter was ash. The house’s new seals were not. Danger continued without the old wax.

Between chests she tried one small want: a cracked oil flask Calpurnia had once filled with her own hands, worth nothing to the people’s inventory, worth a morning to Nysa. She set it behind a water jar. The steward’s clerk found it before the next prayer of inventory. “Careful includes corners,” he said, almost kind, and took the flask for the people’s pile. Nysa’s want failed without a speech. Phileros saw the take and did not laugh. Dama’s chickens made a sound like agreement.

Before dusk the steward sent her across the Sacra Via with a sealed receipt for Antony’s people, kindness that used her legs. “The Carinae,” he said. “Pompey’s old house. The consul’s now.” In the kitchen they said Antony had bought it at Caesar’s auction and never paid. The door she reached was not Domus. It smelled of different oil and men who borrowed authority. A clerk there took the seal without looking at her face. Then he offered, mild as a gift, that a careful girl might stay attached to the new inventory if she learned the new bow.

Nysa looked past him into the atrium. Calpurnia’s women were already chosen for wherever Calpurnia went next, and Nysa was not one of them. The Domus was going to Lepidus’s people with its cupboards. The Subura had rooms, and rooms had rent. The clerk waited the way men wait who have already counted the answer.

A kitchen girl came through the door behind her with a basket, and Nysa watched her bow to the clerk: shallower than the Domus bow, quicker, the eyes down before the head moved, a bow that took less time because in this house there were more people to give it to. Nysa gave it back to the clerk on the first try.

He nodded as if a jar had been shelved. “The kitchen,” he said. “Tomorrow, before light. The steward likes to know who carries.”

She walked back across the Sacra Via for her bundle while the streets were still hers to cross. She did not tell Phileros which bow she had given. He saw it anyway in the morning, when she gave it to him at the gate out of a night’s practice, and he laughed his short laugh, and this time it was not too short.

# SC-66: Not on the Tablet

POV: Statilius · a dinner on the Quirinal
Date / place: Late April 44 · after the Liberators have left Rome
Canon refs: Plut. *Brut.* 12 (callback) · Cic. *Att.* 14.14 (men who cheered and then went quiet)
Info ENTER: The February question · EXIT: keeps his name off the open declaration; writes to Brutus as a friend, not a signer
Overhear risk: Medium

---

The tablet came round after the second course, under a napkin, as if it were something to be ashamed of, which Statilius thought was the first honest thing about it.

It had six names on it already. A declaration of support, open, for Brutus and Cassius and the rest, now that they were out of the city and the city was deciding what it thought of them. The man who carried it was a cousin of Cassius’s wife, a decent young man with a new beard, and he set it down by Statilius’s elbow with a stylus already warmed.

“You were at his house,” the cousin said. “Everyone knows you’re his friend.”

“I am his friend.”

“Then sign.”

The dinner was a good one. The host had been at the Capitol on the first night and talked about it as if he had held a sword, which he had not; he had held a lamp, and Statilius had seen him hold it. Six weeks ago every couch in Rome had been full of men like that. Statilius had watched them. They had cheered in March, and in April Antony had put a guard on his house and read Caesar’s papers aloud in the Senate, and the cheering had gone into the kind of quiet that waits to see who is winning. Some of the men who had cheered loudest had already gone to Antony’s morning reception. Statilius had seen them too, from the street.

He looked at the names. Two of them belonged to men who would be in Antony’s atrium by the Kalends.

“I won’t sign,” he said.

The cousin’s face changed. Down the couches the talk dropped, the way talk drops when a dish is sent back.

“You said at his table,” said the cousin, who had clearly been told, “that a wise man should not take risks for fools. Is that what we are?”

“That is what I said. I said it in February.” Statilius picked up the stylus and looked at it and put it down again, on the napkin, so it would not mark the couch. “I said it when he asked me a question he was not asking. I answered the question he was asking with a joke, and he was kind enough to let me. He went to the Curia without me. I will not go to the tablet after him, now that it costs nothing to sign and will cost a great deal later, and call that friendship.”

“It costs nothing?”

“Not tonight. Tonight it is a dinner.” He smiled. “Ask me when it costs something.”

The cousin took the tablet back. He did not say *coward*; he was too well brought up. He said it with his shoulders instead, going back down the couches, and the host cleared his throat and called for the next course, and the talk came back up the way water closes over a stone.

Statilius ate the next course. He made himself. A man who left a dinner early after refusing a tablet was a man who had something to be ashamed of, and he was not sure yet whether he had.

At home he did not go to bed. He sat in the small garden he had made behind the house, a few herbs and a fig tree and a bench, the way his master taught, a garden as a place to be left alone in. It was too dark to see the fig. He could smell it.

Live unnoticed. He had built his whole life on the sentence, and it had held, through Pharsalus and Africa and the pardons and the ten years and the Ides. He had never been on anyone’s list. He had never put his name on anyone’s tablet. He had been free, which his master said was the only thing worth having, and he had spent the spring watching his friends leave the city one by one by the Appian road while he stayed in his garden being free.

He called for a lamp and wax. He did not write to the Senate, or to the cousin, or to anyone who would read it aloud at a dinner. He wrote to Brutus, at Lanuvium.

*I will not sign for the Republic. I don’t believe in it, and you know I don’t, and you were right not to ask me in February. But if there is ever a thing to be done that wants a friend and not a cause, I am the same man you used to argue with, and I am not doing anything else.*

He read it over and thought it sounded like a man who had drunk the host’s wine. He sealed it anyway.

In the morning he gave it to a carrier going south and went back to his garden, and found, to his annoyance, that he could not sit in it.
