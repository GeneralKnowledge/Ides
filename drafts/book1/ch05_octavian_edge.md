“A Greek town with Italy’s heel for a view,” Agrippa had said from the boat rail, and that was description enough. Caesar had chosen Apollonia for the army and not for him. Over the hills in Macedonia the army was gathering, and the officers who came down to the coast said the numbers the way other men said prayers: sixteen legions, ten thousand horse, the best of what Spain and Africa had left alive. Then Parthia, beyond Syria, where Crassus’s eagles still hung in some temple. Whoever brought the eagles home, the officers said, would be remembered longer than the man who had only won the war at home, and then they glanced to see whether the boy had heard.

Somewhere in that plan, Caesar had given his great-nephew a place. Octavian was to study with the army and sail for the East beside the general’s staff. He was to learn the work of a master of the horse, the second chair, the man who rode behind the dictator. Nobody at the dinners had been told. If they had been, they would have laughed.

The last time the dinners had seen him at all was on the temple steps above the Forum in late summer, heat coming up through his thin shoes. Gaius Octavius, seventeen, Caesar’s sister’s grandson: near enough to be invited, far enough to be stood at the edge. A senator’s attendant had come down the steps with a reserved place nearer the rail, meant for Caesar’s kin, and offered it to Agrippa. Then offered it to Agrippa again, and did not look at Octavian at all. Marcus Agrippa was the same age, farm-bred, with a soldier’s forearms and no family name worth the price of a seat. Agrippa did not take the place. He tipped his chin a fraction toward the boy beside him, and the attendant looked, and understood, and bowed to Octavian a full breath too late and a little too low. Octavian stepped into the gap and said nothing. His ears were hot. He hoped that from the Forum it would pass for the sun.

“They don’t know your name yet,” Agrippa had murmured.

“Good. Names that arrive early get spent.”

He had heard it as he said it. It sounded like a boy reciting his tutor, and he looked at his shoes.

Caesar’s glance had found him once that day, and moved on. In the spring, in Spain, Octavian had followed his uncle late, half-wrecked by sea and sickness, and arrived to find the battle already won. Caesar had asked, with great politeness, whether the journey had been bad. It had been the longest conversation they had ever had. Within days of the steps the same thin shoes stood on a ship’s plank, and Apollodorus, the Greek hired to turn him into a statesman, was being sick over the rail.

At Apollonia he learned war the way he had learned his set speeches at school, in case he was the one called on.

Agrippa drilled him with the wooden sword until neither of them had breath to talk. By the second hour Octavian’s chest hurt as it had hurt in Spain. He went round the back of the baggage shed to cough, and came back, and Agrippa, who had certainly heard, handed him the sword without remark. He asked the officers questions that sounded younger than he was, about supply, and winds, and what the older men feared when they joked about the East.

“What do they fear?” Agrippa asked, on the second night, while they scrubbed their gear.

“The bowmen. Mounted archers who shoot backwards while they run. They say you can chase them until you drop and never touch a man.”

“That’s not a fear. That’s a boast about the enemy.”

“It’s both. When a man brags about how clever the other side is, he’s already asking for a bigger army.”

The officers grew loud whenever Parthia came up: the months of marching, the eagles, the map running off the edge of the table. One sketched a route in dust with a stick and erased it with his boot, laughing. Octavian copied the erased lines before the boot finished. Then he looked at the sea long enough that Agrippa followed his gaze.

“When he calls us,” Agrippa said once, meaning Caesar.

“When he calls,” Octavian agreed. He had the route in his tablet by then, and the winds by month. He had the attendant’s face from the temple steps as well, which he had not meant to keep and could have drawn.

An older officer clapped his shoulder and called him Caesar’s hopeful shadow, loud enough for the whole mess, and the officers’ letters carried it home to Rome before the winter was out. Octavian smiled the polite smile.

When the officer moved off, Agrippa bumped Octavian’s elbow. “Cheer up, Shadow. At least they remember you have edges.”

Octavian stopped smiling. “Don’t.”

“Don’t what?”

“That name. Not from you.” He wiped his face with the back of his sword hand. “They can call me furniture. You don’t.”

Agrippa looked at him for a moment. “Noted. No shadows. Only drill.” He paused. “Though you do look like a boy arguing with the sun.”

Octavian almost laughed, then turned it into a cough so the older men would not own the sound. “Keep close,” he said, and heard how stiff it came out.

Apollodorus found them at the end of one day, grey with dust and distaste, carrying a roll of Greek he had been trying to teach Octavian for a month. “If the young master is finished being a soldier,” he said, “there is a speech of Demosthenes that has been waiting since breakfast.”

“What does he say?” Agrippa asked.

“He says that Athens should have fought Philip while there was still time.”

“Did Athens listen?”

“No.”

“Then he’s no use to us,” said Agrippa, and Octavian laughed in earnest and had to turn it into a second cough. Apollodorus rolled up the scroll and did not look at either of them.

Before sleep they shared a heel of bread Agrippa had bullied from a cook who had refused Octavian the same bread an hour before. The officers had gone up to their dinner. Agrippa said it again while he tore the bread, no shadows, as flatly as he would have said whose turn it was to fetch water. Octavian got a thank-you as far as his teeth and could not make it sound like anything but an exercise. He bumped Agrippa’s shoulder instead, clumsily, and lay down sore. He dreamed that night of the reserved place offered to the wrong chest, and then of missing a drill step in front of men whose names he needed, and he woke with his jaw tight.
