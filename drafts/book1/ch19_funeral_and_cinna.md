# SC-50a — The Forum’s Mouth

POV: Antony  
Date / place: Forum Romanum · 20 March 44  
Canon refs: A7 · M14 · will already in public weather  
Info ENTER: Crowded grief; body; amnesty still theoretically holding  
Info EXIT: Riot; pyre in Forum; Liberators’ capital position broken  
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

Antony did not need a poet. He needed a corpse, a toga that could still show holes, and a crowd that had been paid in promises since the will leaked.

The Forum was a single throat. Heat rose off stone and people. Veterans stood in knots with the ugly patience of men who knew what blades weighed. Ordinary Romans came for money, gardens, grief, spectacle—the four truths of the city.

Antony spoke as a consul speaks when he intends the words to become weather: not verse, refrain. He named Caesar’s honours. He let the decrees be heard. He held up the bloodied cloth the way a soldier holds up a torn standard—evidence, not poetry. He did not invent the crowd’s anger. He aimed it.

“An extraordinary man,” he said, and heard the phrase catch, repeat, become a chant without music. “An illustrious man—”

Someone sobbed. Someone cursed Brutus by name. Antony kept his face between mourning and command. Too many tears and he was soft. Too few and he was a usurper of grief.

The body was the point. Fire found it in the Forum because the Forum decided—or because men who liked fire decided for it. Antony did not pretend he controlled every spark. He controlled permission: no massacre on the Capitol on day one; yes, this heat now.

When the first shops began to burn toward Liberators’ houses, Antony felt the old campaign clarity: the enemy’s city had become the enemy.

“Hold enough order to keep the grain,” he told an officer. “Let the rest teach.”

Teaching looked like smoke.

# SC-50b — Crowns of Noise

POV: Sex. Laberius Cordus (F3)  
Date / place: Forum edge among veterans · same hour  
Canon refs: B4 veterans as force  
Info ENTER: Paid grief / unpaid rage  
Info EXIT: Understands the Liberators have lost Rome’s street  
Overhear risk: High

---

Laberius had not loved Caesar like a poet. He had loved the idea that land and donatives arrived when Caesar’s name was on the wind.

In the Forum he stood with men who smelled of leather and cheap wine, watching Antony lift cloth and hearing the will’s money clink in every syllable even when unspoken. A one-eyed corporal beside him said, “Philosophers did this,” as if philosophers were a people you could bayonet.

“They’ll run,” Laberius said.

“They’ll run to their rock,” the corporal said. “Then we’ll see how liberty tastes without bread.”

When the pyre caught, Laberius felt the heat on his teeth. He did not cry. He measured distances to the streets where Liberators’ doors would be. Measuring was a soldier’s prayer.

Someone shoved a torch into Quintus the fuller’s hand. Quintus looked pleased—the particular pleasure of a man finally asked to do what he had always wanted permission for. Laberius did not take a torch. He took a mental note of who did. Notes survived campaigns.

By dusk he knew the only Senate that mattered today was this crowd. The Capitol could keep its speeches. The street had voted with smoke.

# SC-50c — Water for No One

POV: Nysa (F1)  
Date / place: Forum crowd · same  
Canon refs: household INF in public  
Info ENTER: Sent toward news / supplies chaos  
Info EXIT: Sees Rome tip; returns toward Domus through altered streets  
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

Nysa entered the Forum because Calpurnia’s house could not pretend the day was ordinary, and because Phileros wanted confirmation the way gamblers wanted dice.

She saw the cloth. She heard the refrains. She smelled the first wrong smoke—not sacrifice smoke, furniture smoke.

A man lifted a child to see the fire and laughed, and Nysa thought of Calpurnia’s lap in the dream and had to look away.

She did not see Antony as clever or monstrous. She saw a large man steering weather with his arms. She did not see Brutus at all. Height had become irrelevance.

When the crowd surged, she surged or be crushed. Her incomplete wax stabbed her ribs. She kept it. Relics were stupid. She was allowed one stupidity.

# SC-51 — The Wrong Cinna

POV: Nysa (F1)  
Date / place: Forum side streets · hours after pyre catches  
Canon refs: M15 · FS19 · D5 mistaken identity  
Info ENTER: Mob hunting “Cinna”  
Info EXIT: Poet Helvius Cinna dead; INF lethal failure visible  
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

The name travelled faster than faces.

“Cinna!”  
“He was in it—”  
“Cornelius—”  
“The poet—”  
“Same filthy name—”

Nysa was pinned in a side street’s mouth when the mob found a man who answered to Cinna because that was what he was called when he bought bread. Helvius—poet, some said tribune, some said Caesar’s friend, ink under his nail. He tried to say he was not Cornelius, not the praetor who had spat on the dead man’s name the day before, not the dagger—only a throat full of syllables.

The mob did not do literary criticism.

Nysa saw the first stone. She did not see the last. She saw enough to understand the lesson Phileros would never teach: information did not have to be true to kill. It only had to be fast, simple, and shared by enough hands.

Someone yelled that this Cinna had signed with the Liberators. Someone else yelled that he had insulted Caesar in a poem years ago. A third yelled nothing, only hit.

When it was done, the street stepped around the shape as around spilled grain. Nysa’s legs took her toward the Domus because houses, even broken ones, were maps.

On the Sacra Via a stranger asked her if Antony was still dead.

“No,” Nysa said. Her voice surprised her with its flatness. “That was wrong. This—” She gestured behind her, uselessly. “This was also wrong.”

The stranger shrugged, disappointed, and went to find a better story.

In the vestibule Phileros wanted details. Nysa gave him the only ones that mattered: Caesar’s body had become fire in the Forum; houses would burn; a poet had died for a name.

Calpurnia, hearing the poet part later, closed her eyes. “They will write that as omen,” she said.

“They will write it as crowd,” Nysa said, and then softened it, because softening was survival. “Or omen. People prefer omen.”

She took the wax fragment from her tunic at last, looked at its incomplete warning—danger from friends, a consul spared in someone’s mutter—and set it on the charcoal until the letters drowned.

It changed nothing. That was why she could finally burn it.

Outside, Rome practised the new weather: smoke, lists forming in mouths, amnesty becoming a word you said while packing.
