# SC-50a: The Forum’s Mouth

POV: Antony
Date / place: Forum Romanum · 20 March 44
Canon refs: A7 · M14 · will already in public weather
Info ENTER: Crowded grief; body; amnesty still theoretically holding
Info EXIT: Riot; pyre in Forum; Liberators’ capital position broken
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

In the Forum Romanum Antony didn’t need a poet. He needed a corpse, a toga that could still show holes, and a crowd that had been paid in promises since the will leaked.

The Senate had voted the funeral in the same sitting as the amnesty, and hoped, without saying so, for dignified and brief. Antony had read the hope on their faces and declined it. He was the consul, the dead man’s colleague, and the one man in Rome with the right to stand up and speak.

The Forum was packed to the temple steps. Heat came off the stone and off the people. Veterans stood in knots, waiting. Ordinary Romans had come for the money and the gardens, for grief, or to see.

Antony did not make much of a speech. He had a herald read out the decrees, the long run of honours and powers the Senate itself had voted, and then the oath by which every senator had sworn to guard Caesar’s person with his own body. Read in a flat voice, in that light, each one sounded like a charge against the men who had voted it. After each Antony added a few words of his own, no more. Then he held up the toga with the holes in it, the way a soldier holds up a torn standard. The crowd’s anger was already in the air; he gave it somewhere to stand.

“An extraordinary man,” he said, and heard the crowd take the words up and say them back to him. “An illustrious man…”

Someone sobbed. Someone cursed Brutus by name. A veteran near the front reached for the cloth the way men reached for a donative. Antony’s arm did not yield. For a breath the two of them pulled at it. Antony kept his face between mourning and command. Too many tears and he was soft. Too few and he was cold.

“Hold,” he told the nearest ranks, not loud. Holding looked like piety. It was also the only way the evidence stayed in one piece long enough to aim.

“You are enjoying this,” said the officer at his shoulder, low.

“I am burying my friend,” said Antony.

“You are doing both.”

Antony did not answer that.

The body was the point. Fire found it in the Forum because the Forum decided, or because men who liked fire decided for it. Antony’s marshals had a pyre waiting out on the Campus. The crowd had other ideas, and the other ideas arrived with benches, tables and the wood from the lawcourts. An officer at Antony’s shoulder, a grey Gallic-war veteran who had watched towns burn from the wrong side, caught his sleeve. “Keep them off the shops. If the bakers burn, the city is hungry by the Kalends, and it will be you they are hungry at.”

A second officer, younger, smelling of oil and ambition, spoke across him. “Let them have the Liberators’ doors. That is what they came for.”

Antony looked from cloth to pyre-smoke starting wrong and early. He didn’t pretend he owned every spark. “Keep them off the granaries,” he told the first officer. “Let the rest go where it likes.” The first officer’s mouth went flat. The second grinned.

The pyre took. Benches fed it. A veteran dragged a brand from the Forum fire. Another man took the next brand off toward the streets where the Liberators lived. Antony watched the first flame leave the body for a house and did not call it back. When the first shops began to burn toward those doors anyway, farther and faster than anyone was guarding the granaries, he felt the old clearness of a campaign, and something new under it that he did not like. The city was not waiting for his nod any more. The second officer was already too far forward to call back, and calling him back would have looked like fear.

# SC-50b: Crowns of Noise

POV: Sex. Laberius Cordus (F3)
Date / place: Forum edge among veterans · same hour
Canon refs: B4 veterans as force
Info ENTER: Paid grief / unpaid rage
Info EXIT: Understands the Liberators have lost Rome’s street
Overhear risk: High

---

Laberius had come up from Capua for the funeral because it was Caesar’s name on his land, and nobody else’s.

In the Forum he stood with men who smelled of leather and cheap wine, watching Antony lift the cloth. A one-eyed corporal beside him said, “Philosophers did this.”

“They’ll run,” Laberius said.

“They’ll run to their rock,” the corporal said. “Then we’ll see how liberty tastes without bread.”

“I have never tasted liberty,” Laberius said. “What is it like?”

“A bit like Capua,” said the corporal. “Everyone talks about it. Nobody has seen it.”

When the pyre caught, Laberius felt the heat on his teeth. He didn’t cry. He looked, from habit, to see how far it was to the streets where the Liberators’ doors were. A brand left the pyre in a stranger’s fist and turned the corner toward them.

Someone shoved a torch into the hand of a fuller called Quintus, who had stood next to Laberius since noon. Laberius could smell the vats on him, stale urine and trodden wool, the bottom of every neighbourhood’s smell. Quintus looked pleased, the particular pleasure of a man finally asked to do what he had always wanted permission for. The corporal shoved the next torch at Laberius.

Laberius closed his fist on air, not wood. “I came for Capua,” he said. The land had never once come with a date, and the man who had promised it was burning. “Not for which door burns first.” The corporal heard Capua and did not laugh. Laberius looked to see who took torches.

Quintus the fuller shoved past with his torch already lit, robe wet from a spilled jar someone had meant as water for the pyre-line. “Water for the body,” a household woman shouted at him, jug still empty, one of Caesar’s household by the seal on her belt. “Not oil for doors.” Quintus laughed wrong. “Doors burn cleaner than philosophers.” The corporal took Quintus’s side with a shove. The jug fell and broke. Water that might have cooled cloth became a slick under veterans’ boots.

An older veteran, scar through the lip, no torch, caught Laberius’s sleeve once the surge thinned. “Capua had a date coming while he breathed,” the man said. “Now it’s only dirt. I’ll take the torch. A man has to do something with his hands.” Laberius did not argue with him.

By dusk the smoke stood over half the hill. He had not carried a torch. He did not yet know whether that would be counted for him or against.

# SC-51: The Wrong Cinna

POV: Nysa (F1)
Date / place: Forum side streets · hours after pyre catches
Canon refs: M15 · FS19 · D5 mistaken identity
Info ENTER: Mob hunting “Cinna”
Info EXIT: Poet Helvius Cinna dead; INF lethal failure visible
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

Nysa heard the name before she saw anyone.

“Cinna!”
“He was in it…”
“Cornelius…”
“The poet…”
“Same filthy name…”

Nysa was pinned at the mouth of a side street when the mob found a man who answered to Cinna because that was what he was called when he bought bread. He was Helvius the poet, with ink under his nail, and some said he had been Caesar’s friend. He tried to say he was not Cornelius. Not the praetor who had spat on the dead man’s name in the Senate three days ago. He was not one of the men with daggers.

“I am Helvius,” the man said. “Helvius Cinna. I write poems. I was his friend, I came to mourn him. I am Helvius, not Cornelius, I am…”

“Then you are a poet who killed him,” someone said.

Nysa saw the first stone. She didn’t see the last. She saw that there was ink under his thumbnail, black, on the hand he had put up in front of his face.

Someone yelled that this Cinna had signed with the Liberators. Someone else yelled that he had insulted Caesar in a poem years ago. A third yelled nothing, only hit.

When it was done, the street stepped round the shape. Nysa’s legs took her toward the Domus.

On the Sacra Via a stranger asked her if Antony was still dead.

“No,” Nysa said. Her voice surprised her with its flatness. “That was wrong. This…” She gestured behind her, uselessly. “This was also wrong.”

The stranger shrugged, disappointed, and went to find a better story.

In the vestibule Phileros wanted details. Nysa told him in order. The body had been burned in the Forum and not on the Campus. Men had gone off with torches toward the houses. A poet had been killed for his name.

Calpurnia, hearing the poet part later, closed her eyes. “They will call that an omen,” she said.

“He had ink under his nail,” Nysa said.

She took the wax fragment from her tunic at last. She read her five words. She knew now which consul. She had watched him that afternoon on the Rostra, with the torn gown in his hands. She set the wax on the charcoal and stayed until the letters had gone.
