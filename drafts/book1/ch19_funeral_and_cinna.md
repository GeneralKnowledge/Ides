# SC-50a — The Forum’s Mouth

POV: Antony  
Date / place: Forum Romanum · 20 March 44  
Canon refs: A7 · M14 · will already in public weather  
Info ENTER: Crowded grief; body; amnesty still theoretically holding  
Info EXIT: Riot; pyre in Forum; Liberators’ capital position broken  
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

Antony didn’t need a poet. He needed a corpse, a toga that could still show holes, and a crowd that had been paid in promises since the will leaked.

The Forum was a single throat. Heat rose off stone and people. Veterans stood in knots with the ugly patience of men who knew what blades weighed. Ordinary Romans came for money and gardens, for grief or spectacle—the city’s honest reasons.

Antony spoke as a consul speaks when he intends the words to settle in the Forum: not verse, refrain. He named Caesar’s honours. He let the decrees be heard. He held up the bloodied cloth the way a soldier holds up a torn standard—evidence, not poetry. The crowd’s anger was already in the air; he gave it a place to stand.

“An extraordinary man,” he said, and heard the phrase catch, repeat, become a chant without music. “An illustrious man—”

Someone sobbed. Someone cursed Brutus by name. A veteran near the front reached for the cloth as if cloth were a donative. Antony’s arm did not yield. For a breath the Forum became a wrestle over fabric—grief wanting a relic, the consul wanting a steering oar. He kept his face between mourning and command. Too many tears and he was soft. Too few and he was a usurper of grief. Too loose a grip and the cloth became theirs to tear into a hundred private wars.

“Hold,” he told the nearest ranks, not loud. Holding looked like piety. It was also the only way the evidence stayed in one piece long enough to aim.

The body was the point. Fire found it in the Forum because the Forum decided—or because men who liked fire decided for it. An officer at Antony’s shoulder—same man who had hissed on the grain line before—caught his sleeve. “Hold the shops. Heat without bread turns on whoever holds the cloth. If you let the first street go, you do not get to choose the second.”

A second officer, younger, smelling of oil and ambition, spoke across him. “Let it teach. The Liberators’ doors are the lesson. Grain can wait an hour.”

Antony looked from cloth to pyre-smoke starting wrong and early. He didn’t pretend he owned every spark. “Hold enough order to keep the grain,” he told the first officer. “Let the rest teach.” The first officer’s mouth flattened—permission he hadn’t wanted to hear. The second grinned like a man handed a torch by policy. When the first shops began to burn toward Liberators’ houses anyway, farther and faster than the grain line, Antony felt the old campaign clarity and a newer friction: the enemy’s city had become the enemy, and it was no longer waiting for his nod—and the second officer was already too far forward to call back without looking afraid of his own heat.

Teaching looked like smoke. The smoke did not ask which Monday had left him alive to aim it. It only asked whether the man with the cloth could still tell heat from a plan once the Forum had chosen fire.

# SC-50b — Crowns of Noise

POV: Sex. Laberius Cordus (F3)  
Date / place: Forum edge among veterans · same hour  
Canon refs: B4 veterans as force  
Info ENTER: Paid grief / unpaid rage  
Info EXIT: Understands the Liberators have lost Rome’s street  
Overhear risk: High

---

Laberius had not loved Caesar like a poet. He had loved the idea that land and donatives arrived when Caesar’s name was on the wind.

In the Forum he stood with men who smelled of leather and cheap wine, watching Antony lift cloth and hearing the will’s money clink in every syllable even when unspoken. A one-eyed corporal beside him said, “Philosophers did this,” as if philosophers were a people you could bayonet.

“They’ll run,” Laberius said.

“They’ll run to their rock,” the corporal said. “Then we’ll see how liberty tastes without bread.”

When the pyre caught, Laberius felt the heat on his teeth. He didn’t cry. He measured distances to the streets where Liberators’ doors would be—the old habit, distance before commitment.

Someone shoved a torch into Quintus the fuller’s hand. Quintus looked pleased—the particular pleasure of a man finally asked to do what he had always wanted permission for. The corporal shoved the next torch at Laberius.

Laberius closed his fist on air, not wood. “I came for land,” he said. “Not for which door burns first.” He took a mental note of who took torches. Notes survived campaigns. Torches made you someone else’s note.

By dusk he knew the only Senate that mattered today was this crowd. The Capitol could keep its speeches. The street had voted with smoke—and Laberius had voted, by refusal, to keep his name off the first fire-list.

# SC-50c — Water for No One

POV: Nysa (F1)  
Date / place: Forum crowd · same  
Canon refs: household INF in public  
Info ENTER: Sent toward news / supplies chaos  
Info EXIT: Sees Rome tip; returns toward Domus through altered streets  
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

Nysa entered the Forum because Calpurnia’s house couldn’t pretend the day was ordinary, and because Phileros wanted confirmation the way gamblers wanted dice.

She saw the cloth. She heard the refrains. She smelled the first wrong smoke—not sacrifice smoke, furniture smoke.

A man lifted a child to see the fire and laughed, and Nysa thought of Calpurnia’s lap in the dream and had to look away.

She didn’t see Antony as clever or monstrous. She saw a large man steering the crowd with his arms. She didn’t see Brutus at all. Height had become irrelevance.

When the crowd surged, she surged or be crushed. Her incomplete wax stabbed her ribs. She kept it. Relics were stupid. She was allowed one stupidity.

# SC-51 — The Wrong Cinna

POV: Nysa (F1)  
Date / place: Forum side streets · hours after pyre catches  
Canon refs: M15 · FS19 · D5 mistaken identity  
Info ENTER: Mob hunting “Cinna”  
Info EXIT: Poet Helvius Cinna dead; INF lethal failure visible  
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

The name travelled faster than faces.

“Cinna!”  
“He was in it—”  
“Cornelius—”  
“The poet—”  
“Same filthy name—”

Nysa was pinned in a side street’s mouth when the mob found a man who answered to Cinna because that was what he was called when he bought bread. Helvius the poet—ink under his nail, Caesar’s friend in some mouths, tribune in others. He tried to say he was not Cornelius, not the praetor who had spat on the dead man’s name the day before, not the dagger—only a throat full of syllables.

The mob didn’t do literary criticism.

Nysa saw the first stone. She didn’t see the last. She saw enough to understand the lesson Phileros would never teach: information didn’t have to be true to kill. It only had to move faster than a face.

Someone yelled that this Cinna had signed with the Liberators. Someone else yelled that he had insulted Caesar in a poem years ago. A third yelled nothing, only hit.

When it was done, the street stepped around the shape as around spilled grain. Nysa’s legs took her toward the Domus because houses, even broken ones, were maps.

On the Sacra Via a stranger asked her if Antony was still dead.

“No,” Nysa said. Her voice surprised her with its flatness. “That was wrong. This—” She gestured behind her, uselessly. “This was also wrong.”

The stranger shrugged, disappointed, and went to find a better story.

In the vestibule Phileros wanted details. Nysa gave him the only ones that mattered: Caesar’s body had become fire in the Forum; houses would burn; a poet had died for a name.

Calpurnia, hearing the poet part later, closed her eyes. “They will write that as omen,” she said.

“They will write it as crowd,” Nysa said, and then softened it, because softening was survival. “Or omen. People prefer omen.”

She took the wax fragment from her tunic at last, looked at its incomplete warning—danger from friends, a consul spared in someone’s mutter—and set it on the charcoal until the letters drowned.

It changed nothing. That was why she could finally burn it.

Outside, Rome practised the new air: smoke, lists forming in mouths, amnesty becoming a word you said while packing.
