# SC-50a: The Forum’s Mouth

POV: Antony
Date / place: Forum Romanum · 20 March 44
Canon refs: A7 · M14 · will already in public weather
Info ENTER: Crowded grief; body; amnesty still theoretically holding
Info EXIT: Riot; pyre in Forum; Liberators’ capital position broken
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

In the Forum Romanum Antony didn’t need a poet. He needed a corpse, a toga that could still show holes, and a crowd that had been paid in promises since the will leaked.

The Senate had voted the funeral in the same sitting as the amnesty, and hoped, without saying so, for dignified and brief. Antony had read the hope on their faces and declined it. He was the consul, the dead man’s colleague, and the one man in Rome with the right to stand up and speak.

The Forum was a single throat. Heat rose off stone and people. Veterans stood in knots with the ugly patience of men who knew what blades weighed. Ordinary Romans came for money and gardens, for grief or spectacle, the city’s honest reasons.

Antony spoke as a consul speaks when he intends the words to settle in the Forum: refrain more than verse. He named Caesar’s honours. He let the decrees be heard, the long list of titles and powers the Senate itself had voted, each one read aloud in a flat voice and sounding, in this light, like an accusation against the men who had voted it. He held up the bloodied cloth the way a soldier holds up a torn standard, evidence before poetry. The crowd’s anger was already in the air; he gave it a place to stand.

“An extraordinary man,” he said, and heard the phrase catch, repeat, become a chant without music. “An illustrious man…”

Someone sobbed. Someone cursed Brutus by name. A veteran near the front reached for the cloth the way men reached for a donative. Antony’s arm did not yield. For a breath the Forum became a wrestle over fabric. Grief wanting a relic. The consul wanting a steering oar. He kept his face between mourning and command. Too many tears and he was soft. Too few and he was a usurper of grief. Too loose a grip and the cloth became theirs to tear into a hundred private wars.

“Hold,” he told the nearest ranks, not loud. Holding looked like piety. It was also the only way the evidence stayed in one piece long enough to aim.

“You are enjoying this,” said the officer at his shoulder, low.

“I am burying my friend,” said Antony.

“You are doing both.”

“It is a very efficient funeral.”

The body was the point. Fire found it in the Forum because the Forum decided, or because men who liked fire decided for it. Antony’s marshals had a pyre waiting out on the Campus. The crowd had other ideas, and the other ideas arrived with benches, tables and the wood from the lawcourts. An officer at Antony’s shoulder, a grey Gallic-war veteran who had watched towns burn from the wrong side, caught his sleeve. “Hold the shops. Heat without bread turns on whoever holds the cloth. If you let the first street go, you do not get to choose the second.”

A second officer, younger, smelling of oil and ambition, spoke across him. “Let it teach. The Liberators’ doors are the lesson. Grain can wait an hour.”

Antony looked from cloth to pyre-smoke starting wrong and early. He didn’t pretend he owned every spark. “Hold enough order to keep the grain,” he told the first officer. “Let the rest teach.” The first officer’s mouth flattened: permission he hadn’t wanted to hear. The second grinned like a man handed a torch by policy.

The pyre took. Benches fed it. A veteran dragged a brand from the Forum fire. Another man took the next brand toward the streets where Liberators kept doors: same heat, new address. Antony watched the first flame leave the body for a house and did not call it back. When the first shops began to burn toward those doors anyway, farther and faster than the grain line, he felt the old campaign clarity and a newer friction. The enemy’s city had become the enemy. It was no longer waiting for his nod. The second officer was already too far forward to call back. Calling him would look like fear of his own heat.

Teaching looked like smoke that had walked. The smoke did not ask which market day had left him alive to aim it. It only asked whether the man with the cloth could still tell heat from a plan once the Forum had chosen fire.

# SC-50b: Crowns of Noise

POV: Sex. Laberius Cordus (F3)
Date / place: Forum edge among veterans · same hour
Canon refs: B4 veterans as force
Info ENTER: Paid grief / unpaid rage
Info EXIT: Understands the Liberators have lost Rome’s street
Overhear risk: High

---

Laberius had not loved Caesar like a poet. He had loved the idea that land and donatives arrived when Caesar’s name was on the wind.

In the Forum he stood with men who smelled of leather and cheap wine, watching Antony lift cloth and hearing the will’s money clink in every syllable even when unspoken. A one-eyed corporal beside him said, “Philosophers did this.”

“They’ll run,” Laberius said.

“They’ll run to their rock,” the corporal said. “Then we’ll see how liberty tastes without bread.”

“I have never tasted liberty,” Laberius said. “What is it like?”

“A bit like Capua,” said the corporal. “Everyone talks about it. Nobody has seen it.”

When the pyre caught, Laberius felt the heat on his teeth. He didn’t cry. He measured distances to the streets where Liberators’ doors would be, the old habit, distance before commitment. A brand left the pyre in a stranger’s fist and turned the corner toward those doors; the Forum’s fire had found legs.

Someone shoved a torch into Quintus the fuller’s hand. Laberius could smell the vats on him, stale urine and trodden wool, the bottom of every neighbourhood’s smell. Quintus looked pleased, the particular pleasure of a man finally asked to do what he had always wanted permission for. The corporal shoved the next torch at Laberius.

Laberius closed his fist on air, not wood. “I came for Capua,” he said: the dated promise, the after-Parthia land that had just died with the paymaster. “Not for which door burns first.” The corporal heard Capua and did not laugh. Capua without a living name to date it was only rage looking for a street. Laberius took a mental note of who took torches. Notes survived campaigns. Torches made you someone else’s note. Capua unpaid had just learned it could walk without waiting for clerks.

Quintus the fuller shoved past with his torch already lit, robe wet from a spilled jar someone had meant as water for the pyre-line. “Water for the body,” a household woman shouted at him, jug still empty, Domus-adjacent by the seal on her belt. “Not oil for doors.” Quintus laughed wrong. “Doors burn cleaner than philosophers.” The corporal took Quintus’s side with a shove. The jug fell and broke. Water that might have cooled cloth became a slick under veterans’ boots.

Laberius closed his fist on air. Capua was still unpaid.

An older veteran, scar through the lip, no torch, caught Laberius’s sleeve once the surge thinned. “Capua was dirt with a date when he breathed,” the man said. “Now Capua is only dirt that walks. I take the torch because dirt without a paymaster still needs a street. You keep your note. Notes don’t feed boys.” Laberius felt the cost change. Refusal had just priced him as the man who would not burn for unpaid land while other men burned for the same unpaid word. He did not argue. Argument would have been a second yes.

By dusk he knew the only Senate that mattered today was this crowd. The Capitol could keep its speeches. The street had voted with smoke. Laberius had voted, by refusal, to keep his name off the first fire-list while Capua’s unpaid date burned in other men’s hands.

# SC-50c: Water for No One

POV: Nysa (F1)
Date / place: Forum crowd · same
Canon refs: household INF in public
Info ENTER: Sent toward news / supplies chaos
Info EXIT: Sees Rome tip; returns toward Domus through altered streets
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

Nysa entered the Forum because Calpurnia’s house couldn’t pretend the day was ordinary, and because Phileros wanted confirmation the way gamblers wanted dice.

She saw the cloth. She heard the refrains. She smelled the first wrong smoke: furniture burning, not sacrifice.

A man lifted a child to see the fire and laughed, and Nysa thought of Calpurnia’s lap in the dream and had to look away.

She didn’t see Antony as clever or monstrous. She saw a large man steering the crowd with his arms. She didn’t see Brutus at all. Height had become irrelevance.

When the crowd surged, she surged or be crushed. Her incomplete wax stabbed her ribs. She kept it. Relics were stupid. She was allowed one stupidity.

# SC-51: The Wrong Cinna

POV: Nysa (F1)
Date / place: Forum side streets · hours after pyre catches
Canon refs: M15 · FS19 · D5 mistaken identity
Info ENTER: Mob hunting “Cinna”
Info EXIT: Poet Helvius Cinna dead; INF lethal failure visible
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

The name travelled faster than faces.

“Cinna!”
“He was in it…”
“Cornelius…”
“The poet…”
“Same filthy name…”

Nysa was pinned in a side street’s mouth when the mob found a man who answered to Cinna because that was what he was called when he bought bread. Helvius the poet, ink under his nail, Caesar’s friend in some mouths. He tried to say he was not Cornelius. Not the praetor who had spat on the dead man’s name in the Senate three days ago. Not the dagger. Only a throat full of syllables.

“I am Helvius,” the man said. “Helvius Cinna. I write poems. I was his friend, I came to mourn him. The other one is Cornelius, the praetor, his dead wife’s brother, who threw off his robe and called him tyrant from the Rostra. I am Helvius, not Cornelius, I am…”

“Then you are a poet who killed him,” someone said, with the terrible logic of the afternoon.

The mob didn’t do literary criticism.

Nysa saw the first stone. She didn’t see the last. She saw enough to understand the lesson Phileros would never teach: information didn’t have to be true to kill. It only had to move faster than a face.

Someone yelled that this Cinna had signed with the Liberators. Someone else yelled that he had insulted Caesar in a poem years ago. A third yelled nothing, only hit.

When it was done, the street stepped around the shape as around spilled grain. Nysa’s legs took her toward the Domus because houses, even broken ones, were maps.

On the Sacra Via a stranger asked her if Antony was still dead.

“No,” Nysa said. Her voice surprised her with its flatness. “That was wrong. This…” She gestured behind her, uselessly. “This was also wrong.”

The stranger shrugged, disappointed, and went to find a better story.

In the vestibule Phileros wanted details. Nysa gave him the only ones that mattered: Caesar’s body had become fire in the Forum; houses would burn; a poet had died for a name.

Calpurnia, hearing the poet part later, closed her eyes. “They will write that as omen,” she said.

“They will write it as crowd,” Nysa said, and then softened it, because softening was survival. “Or omen. People prefer omen.”

She took the wax fragment from her tunic at last. She looked at its incomplete warning: danger from friends, a consul spared in someone’s mutter. She set it on the charcoal until the letters drowned.

It changed nothing. That was why she could finally burn it.

Outside, Rome practised the new air: smoke, lists forming in mouths, amnesty becoming a word you said while packing.
