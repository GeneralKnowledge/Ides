# SC-79: Forum Gallorum

POV: F3 Laberius · the Aemilian Way between Bononia and Mutina
Date / place: 14 April 43 · Hirtius’s camp; the road; the marsh by Forum Gallorum
Canon refs: S4 · Cic. *Fam.* 10.30 (Galba) · App. *BC* 3.66–70
Info ENTER: Pansa’s recruits on the road to join Hirtius · EXIT: Antony beaten back at dusk; Pansa carried off wounded
Overhear risk: High camp

---

Gaius’s boot split at the heel before the sun was up, and he took it as a sign.

“Spain,” he said, holding it up so the lamp showed the gap. “Same boot, same heel. It knows something.”

“It knows you walk like a mule,” said Vibius.

Laberius gave him a strip of hide from his own pack and Gaius bound the heel and stamped on it twice. Two years since Munda and he still talked to keep his fingers warm. He had been talking for an hour already, about the colony he would found with his face if he died filthy, and about the girl in Bononia who had sold him figs and could not count, and about whether a man who took the boy’s five hundred and Hirtius’s rations at the same time owed his loyalty to the coin or to the bread.

“To whoever’s standing on your left,” Laberius said.

“You always say that.”

“It’s always true.”

In the night Antony had gone out of his lines with two of his legions and his cavalry, south along the Aemilian Way toward Bononia. The scouts said so, and then the tribunes said so, louder. Pansa was on that road. Pansa was coming up from Rome with four new legions, boys from the levy who had held a shield for three months and thought it was a long time, to join Hirtius and break the siege. Antony meant to meet him first, on the road, before the two consuls could stand together. Hirtius had seen it coming. Before light he had sent the Martian legion down the road to meet Pansa and bring him in, and two cohorts of picked men with it, his own and the boy’s.

So the Martians were out on the road, and the rest of the camp stood to arms and waited, and Laberius’s century sat on its shields by the north gate and listened to nothing.

Waiting was the worst part of soldiering and the part nobody sang about. Laberius ate. Vibius checked rivets that did not need checking. Gaius talked. The sun came up over the flat country and the marsh to the south steamed, and somewhere a long way off down the road, so far it might have been the blood in his own ears, Laberius thought he heard the noise a battle makes when you are not in it.

Then the gallopers began to come in.

The first said the Martians had met Antony’s men at the village of Forum Gallorum and gone at them without waiting for orders. The second said Pansa’s recruits had been caught strung out on the road and broken. The third said Pansa was dead. The fourth said Pansa was fighting with his own hands at the head of the line. The fifth had a javelin wound in his thigh and said nothing at all, only drank, and then lay down by the gate and slept with his eyes half open.

Laberius believed the fifth one.

At noon the trumpets went. Hirtius himself was taking out twenty cohorts of the old legions, Laberius’s among them, down the road to see what was left. They went at the quick step with their packs left behind. Nobody talked now, not even Gaius. The road ran straight and raised between the fields, and on each side of it the ground went soft, then wet, then into reeds and willow and standing water that gave back the sky.

The first of Pansa’s boys met them six miles out.

They came up the road in ones and twos and then in a crowd, without shields, some without helmets, a few still carrying their javelins because nobody had told them to drop them. One had lost both sandals. One was crying with his mouth open and no sound coming out. They looked at the old cohorts going the other way as if the old cohorts were mad. A centurion on a lame horse was trying to turn them round and failing, and when he saw the standards he stopped trying and only sat and watched them pass.

“Three months,” Vibius said. “Three months of drill and they put them on a road in column with Antony in the reeds.”

“They’re alive,” said Laberius.

“Today.”

Further on, the road was full of what an army leaves when it runs. Shields. A wagon on its side with the mules still in the traces, one dead, one trying to stand. Javelins stuck in the road like stalks. And bodies, not many on the road itself, more in the ditches either side, and more beyond that in the marsh, where the reeds were broken flat in long lanes as if cattle had been driven through them.

The Martians had fought in the marsh. Laberius could read it from the road. They had gone in on both sides of the causeway after Antony’s men, the way the Martians went at everything, too fast and too proud, because they had left Antony in the autumn and wanted to show him what he had lost. The reeds had taken their formation from them. In the water it had been man against man, nobody able to see more than ten paces, nobody able to give way because there was nowhere dry to give way to. Laberius saw a Martian face down in a pool with an Antonian beside him, the two of them lying almost tidy, as if they had agreed it.

“They held,” said a Martian sitting by the road with his arm in a sling made from his own belt. He did not seem to be speaking to anyone. “We held all morning. Then the boys broke on the road behind us and the cavalry came round, and we went back to the camp by the village. One of the picked cohorts stayed on the road to cover us. All of them. They stayed.” He looked at Laberius’s face and then away. “All of them.”

There was a litter going the other way, south, toward Bononia, eight men carrying it at a jog with a mounted escort. The escort was shouting for the road to be cleared, and the cohorts opened to let it through.

“Pansa,” somebody said.

Vibius broke ranks without asking, as an optio could, and ran alongside the litter for a dozen paces, talking to one of the bearers he seemed to know. Then he dropped back and fell in again beside Laberius, breathing hard.

“Javelin,” he said. “In the side. Low. They’re taking him to Bononia.”

“Did you see him?”

“I saw the litter.” Vibius wiped his mouth. “The curtain was down. I didn’t see inside it.”

The litter went on south and the cohorts closed behind it and went on north, toward the village and the noise that had stopped.

Antony’s men came back up the road at dusk.

They had won, and they knew it, and they were tired the way only winners are tired: loose, singing, their shields slung on their backs, their column long and ragged with prisoners and Martian standards carried upside down. They had been fighting since before light. They had broken a consul’s army and driven the Martians into a camp, and now they were marching home to their lines at Mutina to be thanked. They did not expect anyone on the road in front of them. Nobody had told them Hirtius had come out.

Hirtius let them come close. Then the trumpets went again, and the twenty cohorts went into them off the march in the half dark.

It was not a battle afterwards. Laberius had been in battles. This was a mugging. Antony’s men turned and tried to make a line on the causeway and the line would not hold, because men who think the day is over cannot be made to believe it has started again. Laberius’s century hit them on the left of the road where the ground was firm, and he put his shield into a man and felt the man go back, and stabbed low under the rim the way they had taught him when he was a recruit, and the man went down. He stepped over him. Gaius was on his left, shouting something about figs. Vibius was behind them, a hand in the small of Laberius’s back, pushing.

They came out on the far side of a knot of Antonians and there was a man on the ground in front of Laberius with his helmet off and a cut across his scalp that had filled his eyes with blood. He was trying to get up on one knee. He wiped the blood out of his eyes with the back of his hand, and looked up, and Laberius knew him.

Rufrius. From the next tent at Munda. Rufrius who had shared his wine in the mud lines before the battle, and lost his front teeth at the battle, and gone with the Second legion afterwards when the Second went to Antony. He still had the gap in his teeth. He opened his mouth and Laberius saw it.

“Cordus,” Rufrius said. Laberius’s third name. Nobody had used it in years.

There was a sword in Laberius’s hand and nothing between it and the man’s throat but a decision.

He thought, very clearly, the way you think in the half second before you fall: *this is what the boy paid five hundred for.* And then: *and Antony paid this one a hundred, and laughed about it.*

“Get in the reeds,” Laberius said.

Rufrius stared at him.

“Get in the reeds, you toothless bastard, and stay there till it’s dark.”

Rufrius went. He went on his hands and knees, off the edge of the causeway into the black water, and the reeds closed over him, and Laberius turned round and found Gaius looking at him.

Gaius did not say anything. That was the strangest thing Laberius saw all day. Gaius, who talked to keep his fingers warm, looked at him and then looked at the reeds and then shrugged and went back into the line on Laberius’s left, and stayed there.

Antony got away. He had cavalry, and the cavalry took him back up the road toward his lines while his legions came apart behind him. The cohorts chased them until the dark made chasing a way of killing each other, and then the trumpets called them back, and they stood in the road among the dead and drank from their helmets and did not know yet how many of their own were in the marsh.

By the light of the first fires the count began. Pansa wounded, gone to Bononia. The recruits broken and scattered over six miles of road. The Martians half destroyed, and proud of it. One of the picked cohorts gone, all of it, dead on the causeway where it had stood to cover the Martians’ retreat. Antony’s two legions broken in their turn, their eagles taken, their dead in the water with everyone else’s.

A tribune came along the fires saying it was a great victory. He said Hirtius would write to the Senate tonight. He said Rome would hear of it in a week.

“Rome will hear it wrong,” said Vibius, “and late.”

Gaius sat down by the fire and unwound the strip of hide from his heel. The boot had split right through now. He held it up again so the fire showed the gap.

“It knew,” he said.

“It knew you walk like a mule,” said Laberius, and Gaius laughed, the first time in hours, and threw the strip in the fire. Neither of them said anything about the reeds. Laberius slept that night with his shield under his head, and dreamed of Spain, and of a gap in a man’s teeth.

# SC-80: Mud

POV: F3 Laberius · Forum Gallorum / Mutina
Date / place: April 43 · ditch / standard line
Canon refs: A11 · Galba letter energy · B4 · D4
Info ENTER: Orders, mud, pay · EXIT: Tactical pressure on Antony; consuls falling
Overhear risk: High camp

---

Laberius’s world narrowed to mud and which eagle still stood.

In rooms with clean sandals this would be named success before the mud dried. In the ditch it was screaming. Martians refusing to be held. Someone’s javelin nearly killing him until a voice yelled his century’s joke-name. Antony’s men came out hard. Hard enough that Laberius smelled the old Spanish stubbornness again, the kind that did not care who Rome called consul. Hirtius hit harder when the day turned. Laberius didn’t see strategy. He saw a standard tilt. He saw Gaius open at the neck. Blood mixed with meltwater and ran pink off the rim of his shield.

Vibius spat black grit. “Win,” he said. “Then count.”

Yell-rumours arrived before facts. Someone claimed a consul down. Someone claimed both. Someone claimed a victory large enough to make the ditch look like a lie. Laberius believed the ache in his forearms and nothing else. Until mid-afternoon, when a staff officer he recognised from Hirtius’s tent came past on a blown horse, cloak dark with more than rain, and said to a tribune three paces from Laberius’s ear: “The consul Hirtius is dead. I held his shoulder. Do not invent a better story.”

That was one fact Laberius could keep: Hirtius down, confirmed by a man who had touched him. Pansa’s name still swam in the yell-water, wounded, dying, fine, poisoned, blessed. Vibius had run beside a litter a week ago and seen a curtain. Nobody had a shoulder.

Near dusk Antony’s near line pulled back toward the higher ground. Not a rout Laberius could swear to. Only a thinning of shields where shields had been thick. He saw that with his own eyes. A wounded Antonian left behind tried to crawl and was finished by someone Laberius did not know. Finishing was not glory either. Withdrawal or regroup: clerks would decide later. Laberius decided only that his century still had an eagle. And a gap where a friend had been.

Water ran into his boots. He had stopped noticing wet hours ago; noticing returned when the fighting thinned and the cold found room. Vibius pointed at Mutina’s wall, still closed. “Town’s still his,” Vibius said, meaning Decimus. Laberius grunted. Walls didn’t feed the ditch.

A mule train stuck in the same ditch as the dead. Drivers cursed gods and officers with equal accuracy. Laberius helped push because pushing was orders you could obey without understanding Senate talk. One mule went down and stayed down; they cut the traces and left it.

He stacked a shield that was not his. The owner would not need it. Somewhere behind, gallopers already carried “great success” toward Rome. Laberius carried Gaius’s boots, the left one split at the heel, still warm somehow. He knew Hirtius was dead. He knew Antony’s near shields had thinned. What Rome would buy with those two facts, he left to the gallopers. He didn’t follow their dust.

# SC-81: Poison Talk

POV: F4 Hordeonius · rumour ledger / letter traffic
Date / place: Late April 43 · Hordeonius’s stall, Rome
Canon refs: AMB-2: not confirmed · M17 · FT11
Info ENTER: Pansa dying · EXIT: Poison rumour spreads, unconfirmed
Overhear risk: Extreme rumour

---

Hordeonius recorded three explanations for Pansa’s death before the man had finished dying:

1. Battlefield medicine
2. Antony’s smear
3. The boy’s friends prefer fewer consuls

He labelled all three *Rumour* in Greek abbreviation, because Latin made gossip look like evidence. A client wanted to buy the third as fact, wanted a price on conspiracy that would make his dinner clever. Sosia sat on the next stool with a cup; a runner from camp hovered with ink still wet.

Hordeonius refused the sale and charged for the refusal.

“Do not sell poison as fact,” he told the man, gentle as a father closing a child’s eyes. “I keep seals. Seals are not dinner cleverness. Pay for the refusal if you want a clean margin.”

The client bowed, grateful as a man receiving virtue. “Clean margins. Of course. Wisdom.”

Sosia tipped his cup toward Hordeonius without drinking. “Only you could make refusing a sale sound like a temple tax.”

Hordeonius did not smile. “Silence clears. Cleverness does not.”

Sosia muttered, after, that dead consuls moved land prices either way. A letter from camp arrived with three different inks and one shared panic. Hirtius down as fact in one hand. Pansa still a menu of causes in another. Hordeonius priced land by the panic without crowning a cause and entered *Pansa: cause open*.

Seius’s interest came in on the same day, a day before it was due, carried by his doorkeeper, an old man called Dromo who counted it out on the board himself and then watched Hordeonius count it again. The extra half in the hundred was there. It had been there every month since the Ides. Seius no longer came in person. Hordeonius entered it under the farm he had asked for and not been given, and thought, not for the first time, that a man who paid that much to keep his wife’s father’s fields was either a fool or the best risk on his tablets.

Sosia brought a tablet up from the Carinae end of the Sacred Way: Fulvia’s paper, and her steward asking for a month. “The Senate has voted her husband an enemy,” Sosia said. “Everybody on the Janus is calling her in.” Hordeonius called it in, in full, charged the steward a fee for the haste, and wrote the fee under *prudence*. Sleep would argue with him later. For now the market argued louder.

# SC-81b: Three Verbs

POV: Cicero · his house on the Palatine
Date / place: Last days of April 43 · dinner
Canon refs: T12 · Cic. *Ad Brut.* 1.3.2 · *Fam.* 11.20–21
Info ENTER: Mutina relieved; both consuls dead; the Senate’s honours for Decimus · EXIT: The saying out of the room
Overhear risk: One guest

---

A week earlier the crowd had carried him up the Capitol.

Cicero kept coming back to it the way the tongue goes back to a sore tooth, except that this did not hurt. The news from Forum Gallorum had reached Rome wrong first, as news did. For a day the city believed Antony had won, and Cicero’s enemies had begun saying in the Forum that he meant to take the fasces for himself and should be stopped before he did. Then a second courier came in, and the truth with him, and by the afternoon a crowd Cicero had not summoned and could not have counted was at his door. They took him up the hill to Jupiter, and down again to the Rostra, and set him there, and shouted his name until he had to raise his hand to stop them. He had written it to Brutus that night and been careful to sound modest about it. He had not felt modest. He had felt, for the first time since the Ides, like a man whose sentences moved bodies.

Then Mutina, and the siege broken, and the price of it: Hirtius dead in the ditch, Pansa of his wound a day after. Two consuls gone in a week. The Senate had sat with their chairs empty and voted what it could. A triumph for Decimus, who had held the wall. The dead consuls’ legions to Decimus, to finish Antony. Thanksgivings. Statues. And for the boy, whose veterans had stood in the mud beside the consuls, an ovation and a vote of thanks, and a quiet agreement among the older benches that he had served his turn.

Cicero had argued for more. Not much more. Enough that the boy would not feel the slight.

“He’ll feel it anyway,” said Tiro, clearing tablets at the door of the dining room. Tiro said such things at doors, where they could be taken as housekeeping.

There were six at dinner. Two young senators who had found their courage since the news came; a cousin of Atticus; a magistrate’s brother whose name Cicero kept losing; and Segulius Labeo, who was nobody in particular and went everywhere, and who collected sayings the way other men collected cameos, to take out and show at other tables.

The fish was good. The wine was better. Somewhere past the second jug one of the young senators, flushed and pleased with himself, asked what was to be done with young Caesar now.

“Now that he’s won,” the young man said. “Now that we’ve won. He has legions. He has that name. He has no consul above him any more. What do we do with him?”

Everyone looked at Cicero. Cicero had been waiting for the question all evening without knowing it.

He knew a dozen true answers. That the boy was nineteen and grieving and owed money he could not pay. That the veterans loved the name more than the man and would leave him the day the name stopped feeding them. That he was clever, much cleverer than the older benches thought, and had written to Cicero every week since the autumn in a tone of respectful sonship that Cicero did not entirely believe and did not entirely disbelieve. That nobody knew what to do with him, least of all Cicero, who had brought him into the Senate as a sword and now had to decide where to hang it.

None of those was an answer for the second jug.

“*Laudandum adulescentem,*” Cicero said. “*Ornandum. Tollendum.*”

Praise the young man. Honour him. Raise him up.

And the last word turned over in the air, as he had meant it to, and showed its other face. *Tollere.* To lift. To lift away. To remove.

There was half a breath of silence, which was the sound of the table hearing the second meaning. Then the laugh came, and it was a good one, the kind of laugh Cicero had been buying with sentences for forty years. The young senator slapped the couch. Atticus’s cousin wiped his eyes. The magistrate’s brother said it again, *tollendum*, to taste it.

Labeo laughed last. He laughed with his mouth while his eyes did something else, a small movement like a man putting a coin in his purse. Cicero saw it and was flattered by it. A saying worth keeping. He had made it. It would go round the city by the end of the week, and men who had never heard him plead would know he was still the quickest tongue in Rome.

At the door Tiro had stopped clearing.

Later, when the guests had gone and the lamps were guttering, Tiro said, “Labeo dines with everyone.”

“That is why one says things in front of him.”

“He dines with the boy’s friends too.”

Cicero waved a hand. He was tired and warm and a week ago a crowd had carried him up the Capitol. “It was a joke, Tiro. A pun. The boy has had a Greek education. He knows what a pun is.”

“He knows what *tollendum* means,” said Tiro.

Cicero thought about rebuking him and found he could not be bothered. He went to bed. He slept well. He did not dream of anything at all, and in the morning he could remember only that the dinner had gone off well and that he had been, for one moment, very good.
