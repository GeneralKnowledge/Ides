# SC-84: Bononia

POV: Octavian · Bononia bargain
Date / place: Autumn 43 · river island below Bononia
Canon refs: A12 · Chain 7 · Cicero naming = C contested
Info ENTER: Mutual need · EXIT: Triumvirate; lists begun
Overhear risk: Low (guards)

---

They met because none could kill the other two cheaply.

The island sat low in the river below Bononia, a gravel tongue with a tent on it and two camps on the banks watching each other’s fires. Lepidus crossed first over the plank bridge and walked the island with his own eyes before he waved the other two across. On the sand each man let the others’ people pat him down for blades. Nobody pretended it was ceremony.

Antony had come with his soldier’s weight and a hunger Octavian could almost smell across the table, the hunger of the man who had opened the treasury at the temple of Ops. He looked at Octavian for a long moment, and Octavian watched a joke arrive in his face and not come out. Octavian had brought the name, and a cold that still cracked sometimes when a room laughed wrong. He felt it crack now, and seal, like wax under a thumb. Lepidus had brought what Octavian had long ago decided was his only talent, being present when maps were drawn and surviving other men’s sentences without becoming their subject. He had also brought the legions that had waded the river to Antony in the spring, which was why he had a chair. A table held bread nobody ate.

Lepidus wanted the law first. In a month of letters he had wanted the law first every time. They had haggled it to the word, and he read it out anyway, and Octavian thought he had never met a man who believed less in a thing he had not heard in his own voice. “A tribune carries it in Rome. Three men for setting the state in order. Five years. A consul’s power for each of us, and no veto over any of it.”

“Triumvirs,” Antony said, tasting it. “In Rome a board of three counts copper at the mint and minds the jail.”

“Then this one minds both,” Octavian said.

Nobody laughed. Antony laid his palm on the tablet where the money should have been. Forty-odd legions wanted paying, and the colonies Caesar had promised them were still surveyor’s stakes. “Sulla did this,” Lepidus said quietly, as if the dead could be asked for a precedent.

“Sulla posted names,” Antony said.

Lepidus had brought a secretary, because Lepidus always brought a secretary, and the man had been holding a draft since the bread came in. He read it in the voice clerks keep for inventories. “‘Whoever kills one of those named below and brings in the head shall have from the treasury twenty-five thousand denarii. A slave, ten thousand, with his freedom and his master’s citizenship. Informers the same.’”

“Sulla paid half as much,” Lepidus said.

“Sulla had fewer legions to pay,” Antony said. “Read the rest.”

“‘The goods of the condemned to be sold at auction. Whoever hides one of them, or helps him away, shall be one of them.’”

Antony laid his palm on the tablet again. “There. Names, heads, and a sale, and the sale pays the legions.”

Across the sea, Brutus had been keeping Antony’s brother since the spring. Octavian watched the hand on the tablet for any sign of that and found none, which told him nothing either way.

It began with a name, then a joke, then silence when the joke hit someone each man still half-liked. A guard outside coughed. Conversation became rain and cousins for three breaths. Then it went back to the names.

Antony’s stylus paused on a lesser enemy first, a man who had borrowed money and returned sermons, an easy one. The stylus moved. Then it paused again on a blank that was not blank in anyone’s throat.

“Cicero,” Antony said. He did not look up. Looking would have made it a request.

“No,” Octavian said.

He had not meant to say it first. It came out before the count, and it came out young, and he heard Antony hear that. Lepidus looked at his hands.

“No,” Antony agreed, pleasantly. “Go on. Tell me why not.”

Octavian told him. He said the old man had brought him into the Senate when no one else in it would stand beside him. He said the veterans did not care about Cicero one way or the other, and the fathers would read the name as a boy settling his debts in blood. He said that a dead Cicero would write better than a living one, because every house in Rome had the speeches and the speeches would outlast whoever had him killed. He heard himself going on too long, the way he had gone on at the Temple of Castor with his hand raised to a statue, and could not find the place to stop.

Under it, in an old man’s voice, three verbs went past in order, praise, honour, lift, and the last one turned over and showed its other face. He did not say that. He noticed that he had to decide not to.

Antony let him finish. Then he took a piece of the bread nobody had been eating.

“Fourteen speeches,” he said. “I had them read to me. All of Rome had them read to it. He called me a drunk and a gladiator and worse, and the Senate voted me an enemy on the strength of them, and you marched beside the consuls who came to kill me.” He put the bread down. “I have forgiven you the marching. I am not forgiving the speeches.”

They rose at dusk with the name still on the wood.

On the second day Octavian came back to it with a better argument, the one he had lain awake building: that the three of them needed the Senate’s face for a year at least, and that Cicero was the face. Antony heard it out and said it was a good argument and that Cicero would have made it better. Lepidus said nothing at all. Octavian came back a third time after the noon bread, and heard his own voice go thin on the same sentences, and knew before Antony moved that he had already lost and was only making Antony wait to say so.

On the third morning Antony brought the price.

“Here is a bargain,” he said. “Nobody at this table gets to say afterwards that the others were cruel and he was not.” He put his finger on a name further down. “Lucius Caesar. My mother’s brother. He sat in the House in the spring and voted me an enemy with the rest of them. He goes on.” The finger moved. “Lepidus.”

“Paullus,” Lepidus said, to his hands, as if it were a debt he had already paid. His own brother.

Antony looked across the table. “And you give me the old man.”

Octavian looked at the three names on the wood. He had argued for two days and had nothing left to argue with. He set his seal ring on the tablet’s edge beside Cicero’s name, metal clicking wood, because he had lost and the ring was what losing looked like at this table.

Lepidus cleared his throat. “He still has friends who write.”

“Then they can write his epitaph,” Antony said. “Or they can join him.” He tapped the tablet beside the ring. “We buy the war with this.”

Octavian did not answer. He took the ring back. The click leaving wood was quieter than the click arriving.

By morning both camps would know which names had been traded for which, and would argue for years about which of the three had been the cruellest. They sealed what could be sealed.

Antony poured wine. He did not look at Octavian while pouring. Octavian let him not look, and didn’t drink deep. Lepidus drank quickly, with both hands round the cup. Outside, a horse screamed at nothing. Inside, the lists grew.

# SC-85: Proscription Economy

### Hordeonius

The garden went under the spear on the fourth day of the sales. It was a strip behind a house on the Quirinal with a well, eleven fruit trees and a right of way to the street. It had belonged to a man whose name was on the second board. His widow had the house still, for the present.

Hordeonius bid because the garden marched with a lease he already held. He bid also because the man beside him would have had it otherwise, and the man beside him was one of Antony’s centurions, who had never owned a tree. He got it for a third of its worth. Nobody at the sale looked at him, and he looked at nobody.

The widow’s steward was waiting at the garden door with the key. He was old. He held the key out, and as Hordeonius took it he spat, and it landed beside Hordeonius’s shoe.

“Your pardon. The dust.”

“It is a dusty year,” Hordeonius said. He gave the man the customary coin for the handing-over. He was not obliged to. The steward took it and looked at it as he might have looked at something found in a drain.

“I am preserving the value,” Hordeonius said to Sosia, going down the hill. “That soldier would have had the trees for firewood by the Saturnalia. She will be able to see them from her window.”

Sosia said nothing. Sosia always said something.

At the Janus that week Bucco’s stool was back in its old place. He was lending to the triumvirs’ soldiers against their share of the sales, and there was a queue. He nodded to Hordeonius across the paving, as one man of business nods to another. No magistrate in Rome was going to ask Antony’s moneylender about a freedman’s son and his throat. Hordeonius found that he had nodded back.

That night he entered the garden in the book he kept at home, in the cipher: *acquired: unclean.* He had never needed the second word before, and had to stop and work out how it was spelled. He slept late and badly. He woke with the smell of a room above a fuller’s in his nose.

### Nysa

On the corner below the Carinae a boy was chalking the names on a wall.

He had them from the boards in the Forum, copied on a potsherd, for people who could not get near the wood for the crowd. He was nine or ten and wrote with his tongue between his teeth. Nysa was coming back with the bread. She stopped because everyone stopped.

Two women beside her were talking about Festus. All the Subura had that name by the third day. He was a slave from the Esquiline who had walked the soldiers to the place where his master was hiding and had walked home a citizen. One of the women had heard that he wore the cap already, indoors.

Medullina came through the crowd from the other side. She had a good shawl on. She put a coin into the boy’s free hand, and bent, and said something into his ear, and drew two letters on the air with her finger.

The boy looked at the coin and then at the wall. He rubbed out one stroke with the heel of his hand and chalked another. It was a different name now. A man at the front who had been reading with his lips went on down to the next.

Nysa could read, slowly. She had read it before and she read it after. She did not know whether Medullina had saved the first man or sold the second. She kept both names and said neither. Medullina saw her, and saw the basket, and whose kitchen the bread was for, and did not look away. It was Nysa who looked away.

Phileros came to the kitchen door of the Carinae house that evening. He was sober. She had not seen that before, and his hands were worse for it. The Domus was Lepidus’s now and Lepidus’s people had their own doorkeeper.

“Your mistress,” he said. “Is she one to be afraid of?”

Nysa thought about it. “She keeps a book.”

He asked if she would speak for him to the steward. She said she would say that he knew doors. She gave him the heel of a loaf, and he bowed to her as he took it, the short Carinae bow, with the eyes down first. He had not meant to. Neither of them laughed.

# SC-85a: Your General’s Mother

POV: F1 Nysa · Julia’s house
Date / place: Dec 43 · Julia’s house, a lane below the Carinae
Canon refs: T11 · Plut. *Ant.* 20 · App. *BC* 4.37
Info ENTER: Lucius Caesar on the first list · EXIT: Julia holds the door; Nysa keeps a name
Overhear risk: Soldiers at the door

---

The steward gave her two jars of Alban, a basket of bread and a direction. “The master’s mother has sent half her people home to their villages,” he said. “She wants hands that carry and don’t talk. You don’t talk.”

Nysa didn’t ask why a house would send its people away in the week the walls grew names. She took the basket on her hip and the jars by their necks and went down the lane with her eyes on the cobbles.

Julia’s house was older than Antony’s and smaller, and it had kept its old masks on the atrium shelf. A doorkeeper with white stubble let her in and locked behind her. The atrium smelled of lamp oil and quinces. Two maids were left, both grey, and they looked at Nysa the way the Domus Publica women had once looked at a new girl: the size of her, whether she would stay.

Julia came out of the inner room herself. She was small and straight, with Antony’s jaw gone thin with age, and she looked at the jars before she looked at Nysa.

“My son sends wine,” she said. “Of course he does. Put it in the back room. My steward is doing the accounts there. Pour for him. He drinks slowly, and he forgets to ask.”

The back room had a brazier, a table, a lamp, and a man of sixty in a plain brown tunic bent over a wax tablet as though accounts were the whole world. He looked up when she set the jar down.

Nysa had poured for this face before. It had come to the Domus Publica in the winter before the Ides, in a better tunic, and stood with Caesar under the pediment talking about a priesthood. It had the nose that all the Julii seemed to grow, and it had been on the first board in the Forum, near the top. The Carinae kitchen had talked about little else for two days. *The master has put his own uncle on it.* The cook had laughed. Nobody else had.

She poured. Her hand did not shake, which surprised her.

“You were in my cousin’s house,” said Lucius Caesar. “On the Sacra Via.”

“I carried there.”

“Then you know how to carry a cup without asking what is in it.” He drank. He did drink slowly. “Does my nephew’s kitchen know where you are?”

“The steward does.”

“Then the steward knows something.” He set the cup down and went back to the tablet, and Nysa saw that there were no figures on it, only the same line of wax smoothed over and over with the flat of the stylus.

She stood by the brazier because there was nowhere else to stand. In the Carinae kitchen they had read the edict out, one of the boys who could read, standing on a bench while the cook stirred. Twenty-five thousand denarii for a head. A slave, ten thousand, with his freedom and his master’s citizenship. Informers the same. Nysa was not a slave. She had worked out, without wanting to, what *informers the same* would be for a freedwoman who knew which back room held a consular. She had worked it out on the walk down the lane, and she had carried the number the rest of the way like a coin held under the tongue.

Julia came in and sat on the only other stool. “He has eaten nothing,” she said to Nysa, as if Lucius were a child or a dog. “Bread. Then wine.”

Nysa broke bread and set it by him. He ate a corner to please his sister.

“My first husband was a fool who lost a fleet,” Julia said to the brazier. “My second, Cicero had strangled in the prison under the Capitol, without a trial, because the Senate was frightened. That was twenty years ago. I have given this city one husband for a consul’s speech.” She smoothed her skirt over her knees. “It does not get my brother for my son’s arithmetic.”

“Your son did not write my name alone,” Lucius said, mild. “I sat in the Senate that voted him an enemy of the state in the spring. He is only returning the tablet.”

“He is my son. He can return it to me.”

The knock came on the street door while Nysa was pouring the second cup. It was not a client’s knock. It was the knock of a man who had a list and a key to every house on it.

Julia stood up. For a moment she was old: one hand on the table, the other on the stool, the knees deciding. Then she was not.

“Stay with the wine,” she told Nysa. To her brother she said nothing at all.

Nysa heard the doorkeeper’s voice, then a louder one. Boots on the threshold stone. She stood in the passage with the jug because her feet had taken her there, and from the passage she could see the length of the atrium to the street door.

A centurion stood in it, helmet under his arm, a tablet in his other hand. Four men behind him in the lane. He was polite. That was worse.

“By order of the triumvirs,” he said. “We have a name for this house.”

Julia walked down the atrium to him. She did not hurry. When she reached the door she put one hand on each post, so that her arms made a bar across it, and she looked up at him.

“You will not kill Lucius Caesar,” she said, “unless you first kill me. I bore your general.”

The centurion did not move. His men did not move. In the lane a cart went by, and the driver looked, and looked away.

“Lady,” the centurion said.

“Say it to him. Say it to my son. Tell him his mother stood in her own door and you went through her.”

The centurion’s eyes left her and travelled down the atrium, past the masks, past the grey maids, to the passage, and found Nysa with the jug.

“You,” he said. “Whose house do you serve?”

The whole of her life had been a training for this question, and for the answer that left the most out.

“The triumvir’s,” Nysa said. “Antony’s. His steward sent me with wine for his mother.”

“Is there a man in this house who is not of it?”

She thought of the coin under her tongue. She thought of the stylus smoothing a line of wax over and over. She thought, absurdly, of Medullina in the Subura, who had always wanted names and never got any.

“There is the mistress’s steward,” she said. “I pour for him. He drinks slow.”

The centurion looked at her a breath longer than he needed. Then he looked back at Julia, at the two thin arms across the door, and he did the arithmetic every soldier in Rome had learned that month. A head was twenty-five thousand denarii. The general’s mother was not a sum anyone had written.

“I will put a man at the end of the lane,” he said. “For your protection, lady.”

“Put two,” said Julia. “It is a long lane.”

He went. The doorkeeper shut the door, and barred it, and then sat down on the floor with his back against it, which made one of the grey maids laugh in a way that turned into something else.

Julia came back up the atrium. Her hands were shaking now, both of them. She held them out in front of her and looked at them as if they belonged to somebody else’s mother.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “I go to the Forum. He sits on a tribunal with the other two. He can say no to me in front of the whole city or he can say yes.”

In the back room Lucius Caesar had not moved. The cup was empty. He had heard all of it.

“Pour,” he said to Nysa. “Please.”

She poured. Her hand still did not shake. She noticed it the way she noticed a seal that had been lifted and pressed again, a thing that was the same and not the same.

At dusk she went back up the lane past the soldier at the end of it, who did not look at her basket. In the Carinae kitchen the steward asked how the old lady did.

“Short of hands,” Nysa said.

“Then you’ll go back tomorrow.”

She would. She had a name on her tongue now, a consul’s name, the kind Medullina had begged her for in every winter she could remember. She had always kept names off her tongue by having none. This one she had, and kept. It sat there all evening while she scrubbed the pans, heavier than chickens.
