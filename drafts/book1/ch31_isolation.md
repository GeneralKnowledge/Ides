# SC-82: Allies Evaporate

POV: Decimus · roads after Mutina
Date / place: Late Apr → Aug 43 · Mutina, Eporedia, Cularo under the Alps
Canon refs: A10 end · death path fog OK
Info ENTER: Siege broken · EXIT: Lepidus has taken Antony in; Decimus and Plancus camped side by side on the Isara, waiting
Overhear risk: Dwindling staff

---

Decimus left Mutina at the end of April, after the relief, with Hirtius’s grave still fresh and Antony thinned but not finished. At the first milestone past the gate he waited half the morning for Plancus’s promised meeting-mark, and no cohorts came. Near noon a rider from the Lepidus channel he had seeded on the road north handed back Decimus’s latest wax unopened. “The meeting near the approaches cannot yet be kept,” the man said, practising courtesy. “My principal sends goodwill.” Decimus kept the returned tablet.

Antony was beaten and not finished, and he was already on the road to Lepidus.

Cicero’s praise arrived sweet and late. Decimus read one line of congratulation twice, then folded it into a pack already too light on grain. He wrote one more letter that tried to sound steady, gates held, honour intact. He stopped mid-sentence when his hand cramped. He didn’t send a second copy.

At Eporedia, at the foot of the Alps, in the last week of May, Segulius Labeo rode in with the dust of the boy’s camp still on him and ate Decimus’s bread as if he had been invited. Labeo went everywhere. That was his use. Over the second cup he told Decimus that he had been with young Caesar, that they had talked a great deal about Cicero, and that the boy had no complaint against Cicero at all, none, except for one saying that was going about.

“*Laudandum adulescentem,*” Labeo said, “*ornandum, tollendum.*” He waited for Decimus to laugh. Decimus didn’t. “The boy says he has no intention of being lifted,” Labeo added. “In either sense.”

That night Decimus wrote it to Cicero exactly as he had heard it, with Labeo’s name, because a man in his position could not afford to soften news for an old man’s feelings. He wrote that the veterans were talking too, that they believed Cicero meant them harm, and that the boy’s soldiers were not a mood to be joked with. He asked again for money. He did not write what he thought, which was that the best tongue in Rome had found the one word in Latin that could not be taken back, and spent it at dinner.

The news from the coast came at the end of May, from a trader who had it from a boatman, and three days later from a letter. Antony had come down to the river by Forum Julii, where Lepidus lay in camp, and had not attacked. He had stood on the far bank in a dark cloak with the beard he had let grow since Mutina and talked across the water to Lepidus’s soldiers, many of whom had served Caesar beside him. In the night they pulled down their own palisade and brought him over. Laterensis, the legate who had sworn to hold them, fell on his sword. Lepidus wrote to the Senate that his soldiers had made him do it, and begged the fathers not to count it a crime.

Decimus read the copy twice and gave it to his aide. “He means it as an apology,” he said.

Plancus came after all, in June, and there was no longer anything between Plancus and Antony but a river and a decision.

Decimus brought his army over the passes to meet him at Cularo, a town of the Allobroges where the Isara came down green out of the mountains. Plancus rode out to greet him a mile from the walls with a mounted escort in clean cloaks. He smelled, as his couriers smelled, of an Eastern scent that cost more than a horse. He embraced Decimus from the saddle and said that the Republic had two arms again.

“And one head,” he said, laughing, “and I’ll let you decide whose.”

They camped side by side on the plain below the town, Plancus’s veterans on the higher ground by the river, Decimus’s army on the lower. Decimus had recruits from the levy, boys who had learned to dig at Mutina and little else, and a few cohorts of veterans who had stayed with him through the siege because they had nowhere else to go. Plancus had more, and the best of them were old ones from Caesar’s Gaul, well fed, who looked at Decimus with interest and without warmth.

They wrote to Rome together; that was the work of June. Decimus had read Plancus’s letters to Cicero over the winter and thought them the best prose in the army and the least committed. Now he watched them being made. Plancus dictated walking up and down the tent with a cup in his hand, pausing for the right word, sending the secretary back two lines to change *our* to *the Republic’s*. He wrote that he and Decimus were of one mind. He wrote that Antony and Lepidus together were too strong to be attacked by the two of them alone, and that they would hold the line of the Isara and wait. He wrote, every week, that the young Caesar must be sent north with his legions, and the Senate must make him come.

“He won’t come,” Decimus said, the first time.

“Then the Senate will look foolish, and we will look patient.” Plancus sealed the letter. “Patience is very underrated in a general.”

“So is arriving.”

Plancus laughed, and poured, and changed the subject to his brother Plotius in Rome, who wore too much of the same scent.

Decimus wrote his own letters too, shorter. He asked for pay. He asked for grain. He wrote to Cicero that the recruits were good boys and would be soldiers by the autumn if anybody fed them till then. He wrote, in one letter he did not send, that he had spent his life being trusted by men he was not sure of, and that he had never before been on the other side of it.

The valley went brown. The foragers went further every week and came back with less, and with stories about Antony’s cavalry seen across the river. Across the river, a day’s march south, Antony and Lepidus sat together in one camp with more legions than the two of them could count, and did not attack, and did not need to.

# SC-82b: The Watched Room

POV: Porcia · Rome, Brutus’s house
Date / place: Late May 43 · her room; the day before the news leaves for Macedonia
Canon refs: T13 · Plut. *Brut.* 53 (the three versions; Nicolaus and Valerius Maximus on the watching) · Cic. *Ad Brut.* 1.9 · pays Ch 25 SC-68d, sets SC-82a and Ch 36 SC-88b
Info ENTER: Fever since the spring; Brutus in Macedonia, Gaius Antonius held · EXIT: The room shut and the brazier lit; the scene ends before anything is done
Overhear risk: The whole household (she is the thing they listen to)

---

In Rome at the end of May, in Brutus’s house, the fruit came up from the kitchen already cut.

Porcia looked at it on the tray: a pear in eight pieces, laid out like a hand of tiles, and beside it the little bone-handled knife that had always come with fruit in this house was not there. Yesterday it had been bread, torn and not sliced. The day before, her women had taken the pins out of her hair to brush it and had not put them back, and Leda, who had dressed her since she was a girl in her father’s house, had said it was the fever, the pins would only make her head ache.

She ate a piece of the pear to show she could.

The fever had come in the spring and stayed, the way a guest stays who has been told the house is glad of him. Some days it lifted and she walked in the garden and read Brutus’s letters again. Most days it sat in her joints and behind her eyes, and the room was kept close against the damp because the doctor said so, and the brazier was kept in because the doctor said that too, and the doctor was her mother-in-law’s doctor.

Servilia came at the tenth hour, as she had every second day since April. She sat on the chair by the window and did not take off her mantle, which meant she would not stay, and told Porcia the news in the order she had decided it should be heard.

Mutina was relieved; Porcia knew that. Both consuls dead; she knew that too. Decimus had not pursued Antony, or could not, and Antony had gone over the Alps toward Lepidus, and Lepidus was writing to the Senate that he was loyal in letters that were each a little shorter than the last. In Macedonia Brutus held Gaius Antonius under guard in a good house and would not kill him, though Cicero wrote to him every month that he should.

“And Marcus?” Porcia said. “Not the legions. Him.”

“He writes that he is well.”

“He writes that to you because you will read it to the Senate. What does he write to me?”

Servilia looked at the brazier. “He asks after your health.”

“He asks after it. Who answers?”

“I do,” said Servilia. “I tell him you are being cared for.”

Porcia laughed, which hurt. “You tell him the knives have gone to the kitchen.”

Servilia did not pretend not to understand. She had never in her life pretended that, and it was the one thing Porcia had always liked in her.

“I tell him what is true,” Servilia said. “That your father’s daughter has been ill since March, and that the house is watching her because it loves her, and because it remembers Utica. I would rather he heard that from me than heard the other thing from a stranger.”

“There is no other thing.”

“Then the house will have watched for nothing, and I will be glad to have been a fool.”

Porcia turned her face to the wall, where nobody had hung anything. At Velia she had cried in front of a painting, and Brutus had not given her Hector’s answer, and she had loved him for it more than for anything he had ever said in a speech. *She has everything else.* She had gone back to Rome with the baggage properly corded and kept her part of it. She had kept the house. She had answered the clients at the gate, and sent the grain bills on to Atticus, and written to Macedonia every week in her own hand, plainly, without periods. Then the fever had come and taken the hand, and now Leda wrote her letters for her, and Servilia read them before they went.

“I want to write to him myself,” she said.

“You can’t hold the stylus.”

“Then I want to say it myself, and have it written as I say it, and sealed before you see it.”

Servilia stood. She settled her mantle on her shoulder, slowly, as if the decision were in the cloth.

“My freedman goes to the coast in two days,” she said. “If there is a letter sealed in your seal in his bag, I will not open it. I will give him mine as well. Mine will tell Marcus how you are.” She paused at the door. “Yours had better tell him something else, or he will think we have both gone mad.”

When she had gone, Porcia called Leda and dictated three lines and stopped. She could not find the fourth. In the end she had Leda read back the three and seal them as they were.

“Is that all, mistress?”

“That is all.”

Leda put the tablet in the box for the freedman and came back and stood by the bed, not quite looking at her, the way the whole house had stood for a month.

“Shut the door when you go,” Porcia said. “And the shutter. The doctor wants the room close.”

Leda shut the shutter. She knelt and built up the brazier, carefully, the way she had been told, and the coals woke under the bellows and glowed. The heat came across the room. Porcia lay and watched it on the ceiling.

“Mistress, shall I sit with you?”

“No,” said Porcia. “Go to bed. You have been watching me since April. Go to sleep.”

Leda hesitated at the curtain. Then she went, because in all the years she had served her, Porcia had never once asked for anything she did not mean.

The latch fell. The room was very quiet. The coals ticked as they settled, and their light moved on the ceiling, and Porcia lay with her hands open on the cover and looked at it for a long time.

# SC-82a: The Legion That Stood

POV: Brutus · Macedonia, the camp
Date / place: June 43 · the parade ground; the gate of his quarters
Canon refs: T13 · T8 · Cic. *Ad Brut.* 1.9 · Plut. *Brut.* 26, 53 · pays Ch 36 SC-88b (the standing mutiny; the June letter)
Info ENTER: Gaius Antonius working on the legions · EXIT: Porcia dead; Brutus does not ask how; the legion moves; Gaius to the ship
Overhear risk: The whole camp

---

In Macedonia, in Brutus’s camp, in June, on the second morning the legion stood again.

It stood on the parade ground in its lines with its shields grounded and its standards up, in good order, and when the trumpet went it did not move. Nobody shouted. Nobody threw anything. Five thousand men stood in the sun as if they had been told to, and looked across the camp at the good house where Gaius Antonius had dined with their centurions every night that week.

Brutus watched from the gate of his quarters. He had a speech ready. He had had it ready since the first morning, and each time he looked at the lines it seemed more likely that the speech was what they were waiting for, so that they could refuse to listen to it.

“They want to be asked,” said one of his officers.

“They want to be paid,” said another.

“They want to be his brother’s,” Brutus said, “and they do not want to say so.”

A rider came up the coast road in the middle of that, grey with dust, with his mother’s seal on the bag, and the guards let him through. It was Servilia’s freedman, the old one who had carried her letters for forty years and had once carried Brutus on his shoulders round the atrium to look at the masks. He got down off the horse badly. He did not hand over the bag. He stood in front of Brutus with it held against his chest, and Brutus knew from the way he held it.

“Tell me,” Brutus said.

“The lady Porcia, master. In Rome. Before the Kalends.”

Brutus nodded. He found he was still nodding and stopped.

“Your mother writes that she had been…”

“No,” Brutus said. “Not how. Not yet.”

The freedman opened the bag. There were three letters on top, and the uppermost had Cicero’s seal. Cicero must have written the same day the news went round the Palatine and sent it to Servilia’s house to travel with hers, because Cicero would always know which courier was fastest. Brutus took that one. He could bear Cicero. Cicero would be well written.

It was. It told him he was a philosopher, and that philosophers were watched. It told him that the whole army was looking to see how he bore it, and that a man who had read what he had read would know how to set a measure to grief.

He looked up from the wax. Across the parade ground five thousand men were looking at him.

They had seen the rider. They had seen the old man’s face, and the bag held like that, and their general at his gate reading. Word was already going down the ranks the way word does in a camp, faster than a trumpet: the front cohort, then the second, a turn of heads like wind going over barley.

He gave the letter to the freedman. “Put it with the others.”

Then he walked out onto the parade ground without his lictors and without the speech. The sun was hot on his head. He walked until he was in front of the first cohort, near enough to see the sweat on the standard-bearer’s lip, and he stopped there and said nothing at all. He had nothing he could say that would not be either about her or not about her, and both were impossible.

“Sound it,” he said to the trumpeter, over his shoulder.

The trumpet went.

For three breaths nothing happened. Then the centurion of the front cohort, a broad Picene with a scar through one eyebrow who had dined at Gaius’s table two nights running, lifted his shield off the ground. His men lifted theirs. Down the line, cohort by cohort, the shields came up, and the standards dipped and rose, and the legion turned the way it was meant to turn and marched off the parade ground to its drill.

Brutus stood and watched it go. He knew why they had moved. It was not the Republic, and it was not his speech, and it was not the pay chest. They had moved because their general’s wife was dead and he had walked out into the sun with no words, and they could not bring themselves to make him stand there.

He would have given a great deal for it to have been the Republic.

That evening he gave the order for the ship. Gaius would be well treated, with his own servants and a guard, anchored off the coast and out of reach of anyone’s dinner. Brutus wrote it to the Senate as a measure for Gaius’s own safety, and it was well written.

His mother’s letter he did not open. He knew it would tell him how. He put it in the cedar box with Cicero’s, unopened. Porcia’s own, the third, he read: three lines in a servant’s hand under her seal, dated the day before the news. They said nothing about the fever. He did not put that one in the box. He sat with his hand on the lid until the lamp went out on its own. He thought of her hand in March, hot in his until the window greyed, and her voice saying *I can*.

# SC-87a: Laodicea

POV: F5 Amyntas · the coast of Syria
Date / place: Summer 43 · Cassius’s siege lines before Laodicea; the town after
Canon refs: T10 · App. *BC* 4.60–62 · Dio 47.30
Info ENTER: Dolabella shut in Laodicea; Smyrna emptied · EXIT: Dolabella dead by his own guard’s sword; the Smyrna treasurer counts what is left
Overhear risk: Cassius’s officers

---

That summer Smyrna sent its treasurer to Cassius in Syria, because there was nothing left in the treasury to send instead.

Amyntas went by sea in the hot months, with a letter from the council and the books, down the coast past Rhodes and Cyprus to where the Syrian shore ran flat and white under the sun and a town sat on its headland with the sea on three sides and an army on the fourth: Laodicea. Dolabella had gone in there in the spring, when Cassius came against him with more legions than he had, and the town had opened its gates to him, because he had been consul of Rome last year and had good teeth. Now it had walls round it on the land side, Cassius’s walls, a double ditch and a rampart across the neck of the headland, and on the sea side Cassius’s ships rode at anchor in a line, and nothing went in.

Amyntas was given a tent in the lines and told to wait. He waited nine days.

He spent them as he spent everything, counting. The siege works: so many feet of ditch, so many towers. The ships. The bread ration in the camp, which was better than Smyrna’s had been since January. The men who came out of Laodicea by night, swimming or on rafts or let down from the wall on ropes, to sell what they knew for a loaf: that the town was eating its horses; that Dolabella paid his soldiers in promises now and in silver plate cut up with chisels; that his guards were Gauls, and loved him, and that the rest did not.

On the tenth day Amyntas was brought to Cassius.

He had expected a philosopher, from what was said of the Liberators. He found a lean grey man in a cuirass at a table covered in tablets, who listened to the letter from the council without looking up, and then asked one question.

“How much did he take?”

Amyntas told him. To the drachma. He had the figures by heart. The strongroom, the temple deposits, the harbour dues for the year, the cup.

Cassius looked up then. “The cup?”

“He sent me a silver cup, Imperator. As thanks for the barley. Before.” Amyntas found that his voice was steady, as it had been on the steps. “I entered it as a gift to the city. He took it back with the rest.”

Cassius looked at him for some time. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of a man reading an account and finding the sum correct.

“Smyrna will owe me,” he said. “Not today. Today it owes me nothing, because it has nothing, and I don’t waste clerks. Stay. Watch. When this is done you can tell your council what you saw, and then they’ll know what the sum is for.”

Amyntas thought of the small book under his floor, the orphans’ dowries, which Dolabella had never asked for, and said nothing. Cassius’s eyes rested on him a moment longer, as if there were a line in the account he had not been given. Then they moved on.

It was done four days later.

Amyntas did not see the gate open. Nobody in the lines did; it happened in the dark before dawn, on the landward side, where the centurions who held the gate for Dolabella had been talking to Cassius’s centurions across the ditch for a week. There was a noise, not loud. Then the trumpets in the lines, and the cohorts going in at a run over a causeway of fascines, and by full light Cassius’s standards were on the wall.

He was taken in at noon with the officers, because Cassius had told him to watch.

The town smelled of horse. The streets were full of soldiers sitting down, Dolabella’s men, with their shields at their feet, waiting to be told whose they were now. Nobody was fighting. Amyntas had begun to understand that this was how cities fell, in these wars between Romans: not with a storm but with a gate and a price and men sitting down in the street.

Dolabella was in a house near the harbour.

He lay in the courtyard on his back, without his head. A Gaul lay beside him, a big fair man in a Roman mail shirt, with his own sword through his own chest and his hand still on the hilt. The head was on a cloak on the paving between them, set down carefully, face up, as if somebody had meant to bring it somewhere and then not had the heart.

Cassius’s officers stood round and told it to each other. When the gate went, Dolabella had sent for the captain of his guard and bared his neck and told him to take the head to Cassius and buy his own life with it. The Gaul had done the first part. Then he had sat down by the body and done the other thing instead.

Amyntas looked at the face on the cloak. It was the same face. Handsome, flushed even now, the very good teeth. He waited to feel what he had felt on the steps at Smyrna, and it did not come. What came instead was the thought of the ivory chair on the tribunal, and of how Dolabella had glanced at it, the way a man glances at a table that has not been cleared.

Somebody had cleared this one.

Cassius came into the courtyard a little later and stood for a moment by the body. He did not make a speech. He looked at the Gaul longer than at Dolabella, and then he told an officer to have both of them burned properly, together, and to find out the Gaul’s name.

Then he turned to the paymaster beside him and asked what Laodicea had in its treasury, and the paymaster began to read the figures, and Amyntas found himself listening to the sums with a professional ear, adding them as they went, and noticed that the town was going to be asked for more than it had. He knew the shape of that sum. He had read it aloud himself, in January, on the steps.

He went home to Smyrna on the next ship with Cassius’s receipt for nothing, and gave the council his report. He told them the figures. He told them about the Gaul. He did not tell them what Cassius had said the sum was for, because when he came to write it down he found he did not know.
