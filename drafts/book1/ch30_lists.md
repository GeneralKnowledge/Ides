# SC-84: Bononia

POV: Octavian · Bononia bargain
Date / place: Autumn 43 · river island below Bononia
Canon refs: A12 · Chain 7 · Cicero naming = C contested
Info ENTER: Mutual need · EXIT: Triumvirate; lists begun
Overhear risk: Low (guards)

---

They met because none could kill the other two cheaply.

The island sat low in the river below Bononia, a gravel tongue with a tent on it and two camps on the banks watching each other’s fires. Lepidus crossed first over the plank bridge and walked the island with his own eyes before he waved the other two across. On the sand each man let the others’ people pat him down for blades. Nobody pretended it was ceremony.

Antony brought soldier gravity and a hunger that still smelled like Ops unlocked. He looked across the table at the Campania squeak grown into a man who could force a bargain, and whatever joke he had kept since spring did not come out. Octavian had brought the name, and a cold that still cracked sometimes when a room laughed wrong. He felt it crack now, and seal, like wax under a thumb. Lepidus brought the useful talent of being present when maps were drawn, and of surviving other men’s sentences without becoming their subject. He also brought the legions that had waded the river to Antony in the spring, which was why he had a chair. A table held bread nobody ate. Appetite had moved to paper.

Lepidus wanted the law first, because Lepidus always wanted the law first. “A tribune carries it in Rome,” he said. “Three men for setting the state in order. Five years. A consul’s power for each of us, and no veto over any of it.”

“Triumvirs,” Antony said, tasting it. “In Rome a board of three counts copper at the mint and minds the jail.”

“Then this one minds both,” Octavian said.

Nobody laughed. Antony laid his palm on the tablet where the money should have been. Forty-odd legions wanted paying, and the colonies Caesar had promised them were still surveyor’s stakes. “Sulla did this,” Lepidus said quietly, as if the dead could be asked for a precedent.

“Sulla posted names,” Antony said.

Lepidus had brought a secretary, because Lepidus always brought a secretary, and the man had been holding a draft since the bread came in. He read it in the voice clerks keep for inventories. “‘Whoever kills one of those named below and brings in the head shall have from the treasury twenty-five thousand denarii. A slave, ten thousand, with his freedom and his master’s citizenship. Informers the same.’”

“Sulla paid half as much,” Lepidus said.

“Sulla had fewer legions to pay,” Antony said. “Read the rest.”

“‘The goods of the condemned to be sold at auction. Whoever hides one of them, or helps him away, shall be one of them.’”

Antony laid his palm on the tablet again. “There. Names, heads, and a sale, and the sale pays the legions.”

Victim lists began like shopping. A name, then a joke, then silence when the joke hit someone each man still half-liked. A guard outside coughed. Conversation became rain and cousins for three breaths. Then it returned to blood accounting. Paper took names faster than mouths could soften them.

Antony’s stylus paused on a lesser enemy first, a man who had borrowed money and returned sermons. Easy. The stylus moved. Then it paused again on a blank that was not blank in anyone’s throat.

“Cicero,” Antony said. He did not look up. Looking would have made it a request.

Lepidus looked at his hands. Hands could not abstain. Octavian kept his face from moving enough to save anyone. For a breath he counted the cost of sparing Cicero: Antony’s favourite wound still open, the lists thinner when the next Kalends asked for blood. Under the count, in an old man’s voice, three verbs went past in order, praise, honour, lift, and the last one turned over and showed its other face. He did not let it into the count. He noticed that he had to decide not to.

Antony’s finger stayed on the blank. “Say it,” he said, not loud. “Or strike a smaller name in his place. I have lesser enemies enough. Choose.”

A lesser name sat two lines up, a man Octavian half-owed from Apollonia’s edge. Useful. Not loved. Killable without rearranging the map. Striking that name instead would buy a night of quieter conscience and leave Antony’s wound open. Octavian felt the cheaper exit in his hands: the stylus, the wax, the easy swap.

Saving the orator would have cost a different war, one with Antony still armed and the coalition already smelling of unfinished business. Octavian kept that cost. Then he spent it aloud.

“Cicero stays,” he said. Voice thin, audible, irreversible. He did not dress it as law. He set his seal ring on the tablet’s edge, metal clicking wood, so the assent had a body in the room, not only silence. “We do not trade him for a quieter cousin.”

Lepidus cleared his throat. “He still has friends who write.”

“Then they can write his epitaph,” Antony said. “Or they can join him.”

Who had first thought the name did not matter on the wood. The name stood because Octavian had said it stood, and no one struck it out.

“We buy the war with this,” Antony said, tapping the tablet beside the ring.

“The war buys us,” Octavian answered, too soft to be a quip, too true to unsay. He took the ring back. The click leaving wood was quieter than the click arriving.

They swapped one quieter enemy in a whisper: a man each of them half-owed and half-hated, traded so that no single list would look like one man’s revenge. The whisper would become three contradictory stories by morning. They sealed what could be sealed.

Antony poured wine that did not taste like celebration. He did not look at Octavian while pouring. Octavian let him not look, and didn’t drink deep. Lepidus drank as men drink when they hope the cup will make them smaller than the map. Outside, a horse screamed at nothing. Inside, the lists grew.

# SC-85: Proscription Economy

POV: F2 / F4 / F1 (triptych) · Rome
Date / place: Dec 43 · Rome streets, Hordeonius’s stall, Antony’s Carinae house
Canon refs: A13 · Chain 7 · Festus pattern
Info ENTER: Lists posted · EXIT: Betrayal market; confiscations
Overhear risk: Extreme terror

---

### F2: Seius

Seius found his name absent and felt holy relief, then shame at the holiness. He walked past a posted list twice to be sure, eyes watering from more than wind. Above the names the square letters ran down to the clause men read twice: *whoever hides one of these, or helps him away…* Eleven lines down, in the same black letters, stood Gavius from the next block, the petition-tablet man who had called him a coward over dessert. Seius didn’t stop walking. Stopping looked like guilt.

At the corner Gavius himself stood under the same wood with a woman who had borrowed Seius’s mule last Kalends. He read the way he returned tools, slowly and from the top, and he had not got eleven lines down. “There,” Gavius said, finger on a cognomen that was almost Seius’s cousin’s, almost a stranger’s, wrong by one letter if you wanted it wrong. “Is that your house’s blood?” The woman looked at Seius. Expectation closed like the old petition trap.

“I do not know that spelling,” Seius said. True enough to keep his door; false enough that Gavius’s mouth flattened. He did not say *eleven lines down*. The woman stepped back. Kinship itself felt contagious. Seius bought oil he did not need so his hands would look busy. He congratulated no one. He locked doors and checked the locks twice. Seia Prima asked if honesty still mattered. He said honesty was a peacetime crop. She didn’t laugh. “Then harvest carefully,” she said. “Peacetime crops still get taxed when the lists are hungry.” He almost smiled at her, old Seia who audited his atrium smile against paper. He stopped before smiling looked like relief worth posting. In bed he rehearsed congratulations for whoever won the next rumour, and hated the rehearsal even as he perfected it.

### F4: Hordeonius

Hordeonius acquired a neighbour’s leases, and a widow’s garden rights, through channels that smelled like lists. The widow’s steward spat near his shoes and called it accident. Hordeonius called it business and paid anyway. He told himself seals were older than pity. Sleep disagreed. Arrived late. Left early. Charged interest he could not collect from his own nerves.

“I am preserving value,” he informed his empty shelf. The shelf didn’t absolve. Sosia looked at him the way he looked at a bad joke. He had not told one. Truth under proscription sounded like a joke whether you meant one or not. He entered *acquired: unclean* in a private cipher and hated that honesty still felt like bookkeeping virtue. In the dark he smelled Visellius’s old room for no reason and told himself archaeology was closed.

### F1: Nysa

Nysa saw Festus sell a household with a sentence. By the third day the whole Subura knew the name: a slave from the Esquiline who had walked his master’s hiding place to the tribunal and walked back a citizen. Informers priced breath. A woman in the street paid with a bracelet and a name; the bracelet was prettier than the mercy it bought. A wrong cognomen nearly got a poet-adjacent man beaten until someone remembered Cinna and laughed wrong. Laughter that sounded like March smoke returning.

Near the Subura corner a boy chalked names from a list onto a wall for men who could not read wood but could read chalk. Medullina stopped him mid-letter, Medullina who had once wanted senators from Nysa and got chickens, pressed a coin into his palm, and whispered a different spelling. The boy hesitated, coin versus chalk. Then he rubbed one stroke out and wrote the lie. Nysa watched the wall become a market. She did not ask whose name Medullina was saving or selling, and she did not correct him. Correction joined you to the wood.

Phileros drank less now; fear sobered some men and ruined others. He came to the Carinae kitchen door, where Nysa had carried for Antony’s steward since the spring before last, and asked her if the Domus still had a mistress worth fearing. She said fear had moved house. She kept her eyes down and her feet moving. In a side court she found a broken seal stamp and left it; broken seals let new law pretend to be old. She passed a posted list without reading every name. Reading every name invited your own onto the wood. She still felt the ink like heat. She did not look for Calpurnia’s old corridors of dread. Those corridors had been sold with the seals.

# SC-85a: Your General’s Mother

POV: F1 Nysa · Julia’s house
Date / place: Dec 43 · Julia’s house, a lane below the Carinae
Canon refs: T11 · Plut. *Ant.* 20 · App. *BC* 4.37
Info ENTER: Lucius Caesar on the first list · EXIT: Julia holds the door; Nysa keeps a name
Overhear risk: Soldiers at the door

---

The steward gave her two jars of Alban, a basket of bread and a direction. “The mistress’s mother has sent half her people home to their villages,” he said. “She wants hands that carry and don’t talk. You don’t talk.”

Nysa didn’t ask why a house would send its people away in the week the walls grew names. She took the basket on her hip and the jars by their necks and went down the lane with her eyes on the cobbles.

Julia’s house was older than Antony’s and smaller, and it had kept its old masks on the atrium shelf. A doorkeeper with white stubble let her in and locked behind her. The atrium smelled of lamp oil and quinces. Two maids were left, both grey, and they looked at Nysa the way the Domus Publica women had once looked at a new girl: the size of her, whether she would stay.

Julia came out of the inner room herself. She was small and straight, with Antony’s jaw gone thin with age, and she looked at the jars before she looked at Nysa.

“My son sends wine,” she said. “Of course he does. Put it in the back room. My steward is doing the accounts there. Pour for him. He drinks slowly, and he forgets to ask.”

The back room had a brazier, a table, a lamp, and a man of sixty in a plain brown tunic bent over a wax tablet as though accounts were the whole world. He looked up when she set the jar down.

Nysa had poured for this face before. It had come to the Domus Publica in the winter before the Ides, in a better tunic, and stood with Caesar under the pediment talking about a priesthood. It had the nose that all the Julii seemed to grow, and it had been on the first board in the Forum, near the top. The Carinae kitchen had talked about little else for two days. *The master has put his own uncle on it.* The cook had laughed. Nobody else had.

She poured. Her hand did not shake, which surprised her.

“You were in my cousin’s house,” said Lucius Caesar. “On the Sacra Via.”

“I carried there.”

“Then you know how to carry a cup without asking what is in it.” He drank. He did drink slowly. “Does my nephew’s kitchen know where you are?”

“The steward does.”

“Then the steward knows something.” He set the cup down and went back to the tablet, and Nysa saw that there were no figures on it, only the same line of wax smoothed over and over with the flat of the stylus.

She stood by the brazier because there was nowhere else to stand. In the Carinae kitchen they had read the edict out, one of the boys who could read, standing on a bench while the cook stirred. Twenty-five thousand denarii for a head. A slave, ten thousand, with his freedom and his master’s citizenship. Informers the same. Nysa was not a slave. She had worked out, without wanting to, what *informers the same* would be for a freedwoman who knew which back room held a consular. She had worked it out on the walk down the lane, and she had carried the number the rest of the way like a coin held under the tongue.

Julia came in and sat on the only other stool. “He has eaten nothing,” she said to Nysa, as if Lucius were a child or a dog. “Bread. Then wine.”

Nysa broke bread and set it by him. He ate a corner to please his sister.

“My first husband was a fool who lost a fleet,” Julia said to the brazier. “My second, Cicero had strangled in the prison under the Capitol, without a trial, because the Senate was frightened. That was twenty years ago. I have given this city one husband for a consul’s speech.” She smoothed her skirt over her knees. “It does not get my brother for my son’s arithmetic.”

“Your son did not write my name alone,” Lucius said, mild. “I sat in the Senate that voted him an enemy of the state in the spring. He is only returning the tablet.”

“He is my son. He can return it to me.”

The knock came on the street door while Nysa was pouring the second cup. It was not a client’s knock. It was the knock of a man who had a list and a key to every house on it.

Julia stood up. For a moment she was old: one hand on the table, the other on the stool, the knees deciding. Then she was not.

“Stay with the wine,” she told Nysa. To her brother she said nothing at all.

Nysa heard the doorkeeper’s voice, then a louder one. Boots on the threshold stone. She stood in the passage with the jug because her feet had taken her there, and from the passage she could see the length of the atrium to the street door.

A centurion stood in it, helmet under his arm, a tablet in his other hand. Four men behind him in the lane. He was polite. That was worse.

“By order of the triumvirs,” he said. “We have a name for this house.”

Julia walked down the atrium to him. She did not hurry. When she reached the door she put one hand on each post, so that her arms made a bar across it, and she looked up at him.

“You will not kill Lucius Caesar,” she said, “unless you first kill me. I bore your general.”

The centurion did not move. His men did not move. In the lane a cart went by, and the driver looked, and looked away.

“Lady,” the centurion said.

“Say it to him. Say it to my son. Tell him his mother stood in her own door and you went through her.”

The centurion’s eyes left her and travelled down the atrium, past the masks, past the grey maids, to the passage, and found Nysa with the jug.

“You,” he said. “Whose house do you serve?”

The whole of her life had been a training for this question, and for the answer that left the most out.

“The triumvir’s,” Nysa said. “Antony’s. His steward sent me with wine for his mother.”

“Is there a man in this house who is not of it?”

She thought of the coin under her tongue. She thought of the stylus smoothing a line of wax over and over. She thought, absurdly, of Medullina in the Subura, who had always wanted names and never got any.

“There is the mistress’s steward,” she said. “I pour for him. He drinks slow.”

The centurion looked at her a breath longer than he needed. Then he looked back at Julia, at the two thin arms across the door, and he did the arithmetic every soldier in Rome had learned that month. A head was twenty-five thousand denarii. The general’s mother was not a sum anyone had written.

“I will put a man at the end of the lane,” he said. “For your protection, lady.”

“Put two,” said Julia. “It is a long lane.”

He went. The doorkeeper shut the door, and barred it, and then sat down on the floor with his back against it, which made one of the grey maids laugh in a way that turned into something else.

Julia came back up the atrium. Her hands were shaking now, both of them. She held them out in front of her and looked at them as if they belonged to somebody else’s mother.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “I go to the Forum. He sits on a tribunal with the other two. He can say no to me in front of the whole city or he can say yes.”

In the back room Lucius Caesar had not moved. The cup was empty. He had heard all of it.

“Pour,” he said to Nysa. “Please.”

She poured. Her hand still did not shake. She noticed it the way she noticed a seal that had been lifted and pressed again, a thing that was the same and not the same.

At dusk she went back up the lane past the soldier at the end of it, who did not look at her basket. In the Carinae kitchen the steward asked how the old lady did.

“Short of hands,” Nysa said.

“Then you’ll go back tomorrow.”

She would. She had a name on her tongue now, a consul’s name, the kind Medullina had begged her for in every winter she could remember. She had always kept names off her tongue by having none. This one she had, and kept. It sat there all evening while she scrubbed the pans, heavier than chickens.

# SC-85b: The Door That Closes

POV: F2 Seius · his house
Date / place: Dec 43 · the same night the lists went up, and the morning after
Canon refs: T11 · Ch 23 SC-66 · the hiding clause
Info ENTER: Gavius listed · EXIT: Gavius in the oil store; the door slave freed on a promise
Overhear risk: The household

---

The knock came after the second lamp, soft, the knock of a man who knew the house had a dog and didn’t want it.

Dromo the doorkeeper looked through the grille and then looked back at Seius with his face gone careful. Seius knew who it was before Dromo said it. He had known since the corner that afternoon, since eleven lines down.

“Tell him I am asleep,” Seius said.

“He says you will say that,” said Dromo. “He says to tell you he read it.”

So Gavius had got to the bottom of the board after all.

Seius went to the door himself because a man should do his own refusing. Through the grille Gavius was a cloak and a nose and two hands holding the cloak shut at the throat. He had shaved off his beard. Without it his face looked like someone else’s younger brother.

“I won’t keep you,” Gavius said. “I know what I called you. At the dinner. At the baker’s corner. To your wife, in front of the Calventii.” His breath smoked through the bars. “You were right not to sign. That’s why my name is up there and yours isn’t. You’re a coward and you’re alive. I’d like to be one of those.”

“Go to your cousins.”

“My cousins have read the clause.”

*Whoever hides one of them, or helps him away, shall be one of them.* Seius had read it too. He had read it twice. He had walked past it with his eyes watering and bought oil he didn’t need.

“I have a household,” Seius said.

“You told me that. At the dinner. You said you still had a door that closes.” Gavius put his forehead against the bars, not begging, only tired. “Close it behind me, then.”

Seius stood with his hand on the bar. Behind him he heard Seia Prima come into the atrium. She had a lamp. She didn’t say anything. She didn’t need to; the old trap of expectation had her face in it, but it was a different expectation now and he could not tell which way it pointed.

He did what he always did. He looked for the cheaper exit. There was a good one. Shut the grille, go back to bed, tell Dromo the man had been a drunk. By morning Gavius would be in someone else’s doorway or in a ditch, and nobody would ever know Seius had been asked, except Gavius, and Gavius would be dead.

“Her father,” Seius said, not turning round, “sold grain at a loss in a famine year and they put up a statue of him for it. Did you know that?”

“Everybody knows that,” said Gavius.

“I didn’t,” Seius said, “until I married her.”

He lifted the bar.

They put him in the oil store, behind the jars. There were more jars than there had been a week ago; Seius had bought oil every day that week so his hands would look busy, and now his hands had made a wall. Gavius sat down behind it on a folded sack and laughed once, a cracked sound, and put his face in his cloak.

In the atrium Seia Prima set the lamp down. “Dromo saw,” she said.

“Dromo sees everything. He is a doorkeeper.”

“Dromo heard the edict read at the fountain. So did every slave in Rome. Ten thousand denarii and his freedom and your citizenship.” She kept her voice low and level, the voice she used on the household accounts. “Do you know Festus? The one from the Esquiline? He walked his master’s hiding place to the tribunal on the second day. He is a citizen now, with his old master’s name, and he drinks in the same wine shop as our cook.”

Seius sat on the edge of the impluvium. The water was black and had a leaf in it.

“Then I free Dromo,” he said.

“Before he frees himself.”

“Before.”

“With what? A freedman needs something to live on, or he is a beggar with a cap, and a beggar sells what he knows.” She folded her arms. “And Gavius cannot stay behind the oil for the rest of the triumvirs’ five years.”

There were boats. Everyone said so, in the way everyone said things that month, with no one’s name attached. Pompey’s son held Sicily, and his ships came in along the coast below Circeii at night and took off anyone who could reach the beach, and paid whoever brought them twice what the triumvirs paid for the head. A carter could be found. A carter cost money. The gate guards cost money. Silence cost money. Every door in the city had a rate now, and Seius had none of it. He had a whitened toga in a chest, and a canvass that had eaten his credit, and a debt to Hordeonius that had been rolling over since before Munda.

In the morning he went to Dromo in the porter’s cell by the door. The old man was sitting on his cot with his sandals on, which he never did before the first client.

“You heard,” Seius said.

“I hear the door, master. That’s the work.”

Seius had prepared something wine-bright and generous to say, the kind of thing he said at dinners. It wouldn’t come. He sat down on the stool instead, which a master did not do in a porter’s cell, and Dromo looked at the stool and then at him.

“I am going to free you,” Seius said. “Before the praetor, as soon as I can find one who isn’t hunting. And I am going to give you enough to live on. Not ten thousand. I haven’t got ten thousand. But enough, and a room, and my name as your patron, which is worth less than it was.”

Dromo said nothing for a while. Then he said, “The Gavius man borrowed my mule-rope two years ago and brought it back with a knot in it I couldn’t undo.”

“I know. He did the same to me.”

“I don’t want his head, master. I don’t want anybody’s head. I want to sleep in a room with a door I can bar from the inside.” Dromo looked at his sandals. “But I’d sleep better with the money in my hand than in your mouth.”

“So would I,” said Seius.

He went to find Hordeonius.

# SC-85c: Surety

POV: F4 Hordeonius · his stall under the Janus
Date / place: Dec 43 · the next morning
Canon refs: T11 · Nepos *Att.* 9–10 · Ch 3 debt · Ch 28 ledger
Info ENTER: Lists posted · EXIT: A loan entered as *clean*
Overhear risk: Medium (the stalls)

---

Sosia brought the news with the bread, which was how Sosia brought all news, so that Hordeonius could never tell whether he was being fed or charged.

“Atticus is off,” Sosia said.

“Off what?”

“Off the board. Not that he was ever on it in ink. He was in a cupboard at Canus’s house with Canus, and Antony sent a letter in his own hand: come out, you have nothing to fear, and Canus too, for your sake.” Sosia tore the bread in half and kept the larger half. “In his own hand. The freedman showed it round the moneychangers at first light. Half the Janus has read it already.”

Hordeonius sat very still on his stool. “Why?”

“Fulvia.”

He knew. He knew before Sosia said the rest, the way a man knows the figure at the bottom of a column he has been adding for a year. But Sosia said it anyway, because Sosia liked the sound of other men’s debts.

“In the spring,” Sosia said, “when the Senate voted her husband an enemy and every creditor in Rome came at her with paper. She’d bought an estate on terms, remember? And the terms came due the week he lost Mutina. Atticus went to court with her. Stood surety. Lent her the money for the estate himself, no interest, no contract.” He chewed. “Everybody on the Janus called in her paper that month. Everybody.”

“Everybody,” said Hordeonius.

“Including you.”

He did not need the tablet. He remembered the entry: *Fulvia, wife of M. Antonius, hostis: called, per Sosia, in full*, and the fee for prudence beneath it, and the day. He had been right. Every man on the Janus had been right. An enemy of the state’s wife was a bad debt by definition. He had called it in with the same blessing-flat face he used for widows and bakers, and her steward had paid it, and gone away down the Sacred Way with his ears red.

“Antony doesn’t keep ledgers,” Hordeonius said.

“No,” said Sosia. “She does.”

A client came, a grain factor wanting to know what a proscribed man’s warehouse would fetch at auction. Hordeonius named a figure and refused to bid. He wrote the refusal under *prudence*, from habit, and then sat looking at the word.

There was a kind of security that held when everything else failed, and it turned out to be the kind nobody on the Janus wrote down. Atticus had put kindness on the books when kindness was the worst risk in Rome, and now it had cleared, in Antony’s own hand, at a rate no banker could match. Hordeonius could not buy it now. You could not backdate a kindness. The market in it had closed in April, and he had been on the wrong side.

Seius came at the third hour. He came the long way round, past the posted board, and stood at the stall with the face Hordeonius had seen on every borrower since the lists went up, the face of a man doing arithmetic he would rather not be seen doing.

“A loan,” Seius said. “Small.”

“Nothing is small this month.”

“Three thousand denarii.” He did not sit. “A manumission and something to live on. A carter to the coast. The gate.”

Hordeonius heard *the coast* and *the gate* and let them go past him like carts. He did not ask who. A banker who asked who was a banker who knew, and a banker who knew was a man who had helped one of them away. He looked at Seius instead: the good tunic gone a little loose, the soft hands, the wine-bright manner switched off for once so that the face underneath showed. It was not a brave face. It was the face of a coward who had made a decision and was now trying to finance it.

“You already owe me,” Hordeonius said. “The canvass. The toga, the gifts, the games you were going to give. Rolled over since the spring before Munda.”

“I know.”

“You will never stand for aedile now. Not this year. Perhaps not under these three.”

“I know that too.”

“Then I want the Sabine land,” Hordeonius said. “All of it, as security. The old debt rolled in, the new one on top, the whole of it secured on the farm. If you default, I take the farm.”

Seius closed his eyes. The farm was where his wife’s father had been born, Hordeonius knew. He knew because Seius had pledged it once before and taken it back, years ago, when the canvass still looked like a road to a statue.

“Done,” said Seius.

Sosia, on the next stool, stopped chewing.

Hordeonius counted it out himself: worn silver, no Caesar on it, nothing anyone would remember. He wrapped it in a cloth that smelled of the shelf and pushed it across the board, and Seius took it under his cloak with the careful hands of a man carrying eggs.

When he had gone Sosia said, “You know what that is for.”

“I know it is secured on a farm.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“It’s what I answered.” Hordeonius opened his private tablet, the one with the cipher, where he had written *acquired: unclean* the week before. Under it he wrote the date, and the sum, and *Seius: lent*. His stylus stopped. Then he wrote one more word after it, small, in the cipher only he could read.

*Clean.*

It looked strange on the wax. He did not rub it out. He did not know yet whether it would clear. He knew only that it was the first entry in a long time he could have shown to Atticus without being ashamed of it.

“Sosia,” he said. “If anyone asks, Seius came about his canvass debt.”

“Nobody will ask,” said Sosia. “Everybody’s too busy asking about everybody else.” But he said it gently, for him, and he gave back the smaller half of the bread.

# SC-85d: Twice the Price

The carter wanted the money first, and Seius gave it to him first, because there was no way to make a carter trust you that did not end with a carter knowing too much.

Gavius lay under sacks of chaff in the bed of the cart with his knees up. Dromo had led the mule round from the stable, Seius’s own mule, the good one, and backed it between the shafts himself, and now he stood at the house door in a cap he had never worn before, holding a purse he kept looking at.

“You could stay,” Seius said to him. “As a freedman. On wages.”

“I’ll stay till he’s through the gate,” said Dromo. “After that I want my own door.”

Seius walked beside the cart. A master walking beside a load of chaff looked strange, but a master riding on it looked stranger, and a master staying home while his mule went out before light looked like a man with something to hide. He had thought about all three for most of the night and chosen the one that let him talk.

At the Capena gate there were soldiers with torches, six of them, and a decurion with a tablet. They stopped every cart. They were bored, which was the only hope Seius had.

“Chaff,” said the carter. “For the villa at Bovillae.”

The decurion lifted his torch and looked at the sacks and then at Seius. “And you?”

Seius had been congratulating men for his whole adult life. He had congratulated Caesar’s friends and Caesar’s killers and Caesar’s heir, sometimes in the same week. He had done it at dinners and in doorways and on the steps of the Senate, with wine-bright eyes and his spine already planning its exit. He had always despised himself a little for how well he did it. He did it now.

“I am Seius,” he said, “nephew of the Seius who gave the people grain, you’ll have heard of him, and I am up before the cocks because the triumvirs have had their first good week and I want to be at my farm before the auctions make the roads impossible. Have you eaten, soldier? You look like a man on a long watch. Three good men saving the state, and the state sends its guards out without breakfast.” He took a coin from his own purse, not Hordeonius’s, and put it in the decurion’s hand as if it were a gift between friends. “For bread. Drink to the three of them.”

The decurion looked at the coin. He looked at the cart. A sack near the back moved, very slightly, as a man under it breathed.

“The mule’s restless,” Seius said, and slapped its rump, and the mule, which had never been restless in its life, threw its head and stamped.

“Go on,” said the decurion.

Beyond the gate the road was dark between the tombs. A mile out, by a milestone, the carter stopped and Gavius sat up in the chaff with it in his hair and his eyebrows. He looked back at the walls.

“There are boats,” Seius said. “Below Circeii. Pompey’s son pays twice the triumvirs’ price for any man brought to the beach alive. The carter knows. He’ll want paying twice, I expect.”

“He can have it,” said Gavius. “If I get there I’ll owe Pompey’s son a head I’m still wearing.” He looked at Seius. “I called you a coward.”

“You were right.”

“I know I was.” Gavius pulled a straw out of his collar. “You did it anyway.”

“Once,” said Seius. “Don’t tell anyone. It would ruin me.”

Gavius laughed, the cracked laugh from the oil store, and the carter clicked to the mule, and the cart went on south into the dark with Seius’s good mule pulling it and not looking back.

Seius walked home. It took a long time. At the gate the decurion nodded to him like an old friend. In the lane Dromo was gone, the porter’s cell swept, the cot stripped, the bar of the door left leaning against the wall so that whoever came next could put it up.

Seia Prima was in the atrium. She had not slept either.

“Well?” she said.

He did not count faces, as he had counted them after the dinner, the faces that would lend him a mule if the wind turned. He had no mule now. He had a debt on her father’s farm and a free man’s empty cell and nothing at all to show anyone.

“Gone,” he said. “South.”

She came and put her hand flat on his chest, over the heart, the way she checked a jar for a crack. He almost made a joke. For once, he didn’t.
