# SC-87a: Laodicea

POV: F5 Amyntas · the coast of Syria
Date / place: Summer 43 · Cassius’s siege lines before Laodicea; the town after
Canon refs: T10 · App. *BC* 4.60–62 · Dio 47.30
Info ENTER: Dolabella shut in Laodicea; Smyrna emptied · EXIT: Dolabella dead by his own guard’s sword; the Smyrna treasurer counts what is left
Overhear risk: Cassius’s officers

---

Smyrna sent its treasurer to Cassius because there was nothing left in the treasury to send instead.

Amyntas went by sea in the hot months, with a letter from the council and the books, down the coast past Rhodes and Cyprus to where the Syrian shore ran flat and white under the sun and a town sat on its headland with the sea on three sides and an army on the fourth. Laodicea. Dolabella had gone in there in the spring, when Cassius came against him with more legions than he had, and the town had opened its gates to him, because he had been consul of Rome last year and had good teeth. Now it had walls round it on the land side, Cassius’s walls, a double ditch and a rampart across the neck of the headland, and on the sea side Cassius’s ships rode at anchor in a line, and nothing went in.

Amyntas was given a tent in the lines and told to wait. He waited nine days.

He spent them as he spent everything, counting. The siege works: so many feet of ditch, so many towers. The ships. The bread ration in the camp, which was better than Smyrna’s had been since January. The men who came out of Laodicea by night, swimming or on rafts or let down from the wall on ropes, to sell what they knew for a loaf: that the town was eating its horses; that Dolabella paid his soldiers in promises now and in silver plate cut up with chisels; that his guards were Gauls, and loved him, and that the rest did not.

On the tenth day Amyntas was brought to Cassius.

He had expected a philosopher, from what was said of the Liberators. He found a lean grey man in a cuirass at a table covered in tablets, who listened to the letter from the council without looking up, and then asked one question.

“How much did he take?”

Amyntas told him. To the drachma. He had the figures by heart. The strongroom, the temple deposits, the harbour dues for the year, the cup.

Cassius looked up then. “The cup?”

“He sent me a silver cup, Imperator. As thanks for the barley. Before.” Amyntas found that his voice was steady, as it had been on the steps. “I entered it as a gift to the city. He took it back with the rest.”

Cassius looked at him for some time. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of a man reading an account and finding the sum correct.

“Smyrna will owe me,” he said. “Not today. Today it owes me nothing, because it has nothing, and I don’t waste clerks. Stay. Watch. When this is done you can tell your council what you saw, and then they’ll know what the sum is for.”

Amyntas thought of the small book under his floor, the orphans’ dowries, which Dolabella had never asked for, and said nothing. Cassius’s eyes rested on him a moment longer, as if there were a line in the account he had not been given. Then they moved on.

It was done four days later.

Amyntas did not see the gate open. Nobody in the lines did; it happened in the dark before dawn, on the landward side, where the centurions who held the gate for Dolabella had been talking to Cassius’s centurions across the ditch for a week. There was a noise, not loud. Then the trumpets in the lines, and the cohorts going in at a run over a causeway of fascines, and by full light Cassius’s standards were on the wall.

He was taken in at noon with the officers, because Cassius had told him to watch.

The town smelled of horse. The streets were full of soldiers sitting down, Dolabella’s men, with their shields at their feet, waiting to be told whose they were now. Nobody was fighting. Amyntas had begun to understand that this was how cities fell, in these wars between Romans: not with a storm but with a gate and a price and men sitting down in the street.

Dolabella was in a house near the harbour.

He lay in the courtyard on his back, without his head. A Gaul lay beside him, a big fair man in a Roman mail shirt, with his own sword through his own chest and his hand still on the hilt. The head was on a cloak on the paving between them, set down carefully, face up, as if somebody had meant to bring it somewhere and then not had the heart.

Cassius’s officers stood round and told it to each other. When the gate went, Dolabella had sent for the captain of his guard and bared his neck and told him to take the head to Cassius and buy his own life with it. The Gaul had done the first part. Then he had sat down by the body and done the other thing instead.

Amyntas looked at the face on the cloak. It was the same face. Handsome, flushed even now, the very good teeth. He waited to feel what he had felt on the steps at Smyrna, and it did not come. What came instead was the thought of the ivory chair on the tribunal, and of how Dolabella had glanced at it, the way a man glances at a table that has not been cleared.

Somebody had cleared this one.

Cassius came into the courtyard a little later and stood for a moment by the body. He did not make a speech. He looked at the Gaul longer than at Dolabella, and then he told an officer to have both of them burned properly, together, and to find out the Gaul’s name.

Then he turned to the paymaster beside him and asked what Laodicea had in its treasury, and the paymaster began to read the figures, and Amyntas found himself listening to the sums with a professional ear, adding them as they went, and noticed that the town was going to be asked for more than it had. He knew the shape of that sum. He had read it aloud himself, in January, on the steps.

He went home to Smyrna on the next ship with Cassius’s receipt for nothing, and gave the council his report. He told them the figures. He told them about the Gaul. He did not tell them what Cassius had said the sum was for, because when he came to write it down he found he did not know.

# SC-87: Necessity

POV: Cassius · Asia / Syria orbit
Date / place: Winter 43–42 · a city’s audience hall, Asia
Canon refs: Chain 8 · C3
Info ENTER: Need pay · EXIT: Liberty felt as extraction
Overhear risk: Interpreters

---

Cassius listened to a city’s elders explain poverty with practised dignity.

The hall smelled of oil and fear-sweat dressed as perfume. Outside, harbour noise argued with temple bells. A fly walked the rim of a cup. Elders in a half-circle, interpreters at their shoulders, a young clerk with a face already practising hatred. “Liberty costs,” Cassius said. “Rome’s disease is expensive. You will help cure it.” He didn’t raise his voice.

Behind him, outside the city, legions ate. Syria’s garrison had come over to him in the spring without a blow, and Dolabella’s men after Laodicea, once Dolabella ran out of wall and asked one of his own soldiers for the sword. Legions that came over came over hungry.

He let silence work after the sentence. Brutus’s tool, borrowed without credit. Silence made elders fill the air with offers, most of them dates that were not dates.

Interpreters softened the edges, turned *pay* into *contribute*, *now* into *in due season*. Softening did not soften the silver leaving. A chest lid knocked; Cassius noted the hinge and did not flinch. Publicly he was mathematics in a cuirass.

An elder with a staff stepped forward before the interpreters could varnish him. “We can send half by the Kalends,” he said in Greek plain enough that Cassius needed no cushion. “The other half after harvest. Take half now and we remain a city. Take all now and you march past hungry walls that will not open for your return.”

The interpreter began, “What he means…”

“I heard what he means,” Cassius said. He had an officer weigh the nearest sack on the spot, grain, not rhetoric. The weight came short of the tablet’s claim by enough to insult geometry. The elder did not look away. Cassius let three breaths pass without raising his voice, long enough for a shout that did not come. He gave them a number instead: half now, sealed; the rest in thirty days under hostage sureties from two merchant houses already in the hall. “Refuse the sureties,” he added, still quiet, “and I take the full claim today and leave your walls the problem of explaining hunger to your own people.”

The elder’s mouth worked. He took the half and the sureties with a bow that could have been gratitude. “The city’s thanks. Favourable gods on your road.” Hatred stayed on the young clerk’s face; survival stayed on the elder’s. On the hall’s outer step, with Cassius three paces below him, the elder’s thanks thinned for the clerk and the nearest merchant surety, not as quietly as the elder believed. “He weighs our hunger and calls it cure. Half now. Hostages. When he needs the road open again, remember what emptied the granaries.”

Cassius signed for ships on the arithmetic. The elder’s step-talk needed no translation. Paid was paid.

Before the audience closed, a courier from the west came in, dust, salt, a seal cracked by too many hands, with three tablets that did not agree. Mutina won and lost. Lists in Italy. The boy with fasces, and a court that had condemned every man of March, Cassius among them, to empty benches. One tablet named Decimus without poetry: gone, or going.

Cassius read none of it aloud to the hall. He didn’t halt the silver for a rumour’s sake. But the Decimus line cracked something the extraction had been keeping neat. The man had opened Caesar’s morning with cheer and taken the nearer knife and Cisalpine as if a road were still a friendship, and Cassius had recruited doors through him. He entered *Decimus: incomplete* in the only ledger he trusted, the one behind his eyes, under Trebonius, who had stood in that column since the winter, since Dolabella’s soldiers came into his bedroom at Smyrna before dawn and carried the market-day man’s head out with them. Laodicea had settled Trebonius. Decimus had no Laodicea.

An officer asked if they were becoming what they killed. Cassius watched the short-weight sack being restacked and said only, “We are becoming paid.” He didn’t offer the Forum a prettier noun. He dismissed the elders with a bow that meant *paid*. He did not look at the west courier again until the hall had emptied enough to pretend the crack was only dust.

# SC-88: EID MAR

POV: Brutus · East camp / mint
Date / place: 42 · mint tent, camp edge
Canon refs: *EID MAR* · Chain 8 · C2
Info ENTER: Philosophical self-image · EXIT: Coin brand; crack vs exaction
Overhear risk: Officers

---

The dies showed two daggers either side of a felt cap, and under them *EID MAR*. A date pretending to be a sacrament.

“The cap they set on a slave’s head the day he’s freed,” the mint-master said, tilting the die to the lamp as though Brutus had not drawn it himself. “Between the knives. The men like it. They say it means the Ides bought them out.”

“It means the Ides bought Rome out,” Brutus said. The mint-master nodded the way men nod at a correction they will not pass on.

Brutus held a fresh coin. The metal still held the heat of striking. His own head was on the other face. In the winter before the Ides he had turned one of Caesar’s new silver pieces over at his own table and called a living face on a coin a king’s habit. He turned this one so the profile lay against his palm. Around him, officers argued yields and marches with the same mouths that praised ancestors: two tribunes and a mint-master making a small Senate of appetite. One man weighed a sack and called the weight patriotic. Brutus’s thumb stayed on the *EID MAR* lettering and did not lift to correct the adjective. He thought of Porcia, of masks at home, of Lanuvium’s gravel, of the night he had said GO while a creditor coughed outside the door. He shut the thoughts before they became speeches no army would wait for.

“We are not pirates,” an officer said, too loud. Volume would not wash the tax roads clean.

“We are paying an army,” Brutus answered. “Call it what you must. The men still need grain and miles.”

News from Italy had reached the mint tent as gossip: lists, fasces, a boy paid in fear, and Decimus’s name travelling wrong, the nearer knife become a rumour of ending. Brutus didn’t ask which version was true. Asking would’ve required a Forum he no longer owned. The GO night’s clean hands felt suddenly like someone else’s. One honest doubt crossed him: *If Cato could see the ledgers.* He shut it down and felt the shut as shame, not cleverness. He kept the coin anyway, brand as comfort, brand as trap. He walked out where the camp smelled of men who would spend *libertas* as wages without reading the Latin on the metal.

A mint worker burned his finger and cursed without poetry. Brutus gave him a nod that was almost kindness and walked on before kindness became a speech.

In the dark he held the warm coin until it cooled, then put it away.

# SC-88b: The Prisoner’s Ship

POV: Brutus · Macedonia, winter camp
Date / place: Early 42 · Brutus’s quarters; the letter box
Canon refs: T8 · Plut. *Brut.* 26, 28 · Cic. *Ad Brut.* (spring 43)
Info ENTER: Gaius Antonius held since Apollonia; news of Cicero’s death · EXIT: Brutus writes the order to Hortensius
Overhear risk: Secretary

---

He kept Cicero’s letters in a cedar box with a bronze clasp, because they were the best-written letters he had ever been sent and he had thought, once, that he might want to read them again when he was old.

There were a great many of them about Gaius Antonius.

The first had come in April, after Apollonia. Cicero had congratulated him on the town and the legions and the young Marcus, in that order, and then in a final paragraph, as if remembering a small thing, had written that Gaius ought to be put to death. *Not out of anger*, Cicero wrote. *Out of arithmetic. While he lives, his brother has a reason to come east, and every Antonian in your ranks has a man to look at.* Brutus had written back that the Senate had not condemned Gaius, and that he would not do with a sword what the Senate had not done with a vote. He had written it well. He had been proud of the letter. He had sent Cicero a copy of a passage from it that Cicero might use in the House, if he liked.

Then, in the summer, Gaius had worked on the legions.

He had done it the way his family did everything, openly and with a good deal of wine. He dined in the lines. He let the men see his lictors, and let them remember whose brother he was, and whose soldiers half of them had been the winter before. One legion had begun to talk. Then it had begun to refuse. For three days Brutus had had a mutiny in his camp, quiet, sullen, the men standing in their lines and not moving when the trumpet went, and Gaius in the good house he had been given, pouring wine and being sorry about it. Brutus had ended it without killing anyone. He had been proud of that too.

He had put Gaius on a ship. That was all. A good ship, anchored off the coast, with a guard and his own servants, and his lictors sent ashore. *For his own safety*, Brutus had written to the Senate, and to Cicero, and Cicero had written back in August a letter so angry the clasp of the box had seemed to sit tighter after it went in. *You are the gentlest man in Rome*, Cicero wrote, *and it will cost the Republic everything it has left. Do you think he would spare you?*

Brutus took that letter out now and read it again by the lamp, in the cold, with the secretary waiting.

Cicero was dead.

The news had come up from the coast the week before, from Italy, wrong at first as everything came wrong, and then right. Cicero dead in December on the road to the sea, and what had been done afterwards in the Forum, on Antony’s orders, told by every rider a little differently. Brutus had not asked for the details, as he had not asked which story about Decimus was true. Decimus dead in the autumn at some ford in Gaul. Cicero dead on the coast road. Both on lists their friends had signed. Both while their friends’ brothers were alive and well and being fed on Brutus’s grain.

He read the August letter through to the end. *Do you think he would spare you?*

He did not know. That was the truth of it, and it was the worst thing about the night. Gaius might have spared him. Gaius might have been kind to him at a camp gate and stopped his hand short of his shoulder and hated himself for the pleasure of it. Men were stranger than Cicero allowed. They were stranger than Brutus had allowed, in the spring, when he had heard Caesar’s voice come out of his own mouth and decided it was only his own, and good.

“Hortensius,” he said.

The secretary dipped the pen.

He dictated it plainly. To Quintus Hortensius, proconsul, who held the coast and the ship. That in view of what had been done in Italy to Decimus Brutus and to Marcus Cicero, by the order of Gaius’s brother, Gaius Antonius was to be put to death. That it was to be done quickly and without insult. That his body was to be given to his servants for burial in a manner fitting his rank.

He listened to his own voice while he said it, waiting for the warmth, and it did not come. Nothing came. It was a clerk’s voice. He had wanted, he found, at least to feel the thing he was doing, and he did not, and that was worse than the warmth had been.

“Read it back.”

The secretary read it back. Brutus listened to it and found nothing to change, and took the stylus and signed it, and sealed it with his own seal, the one with the ancestor on it, Lucius Brutus who had driven out the kings and then sat in judgement on his own sons for plotting to bring them back, and watched them die, and did not look away.

He had always thought of that as the hardest thing a Roman had ever done. It had not seemed hard tonight. It had seemed like a letter.

When the secretary had gone he put the August letter back in the cedar box with the others. At the bottom of the box, under all of them, were the two from June: Cicero’s, which told him that philosophers were watched, and his mother’s, still sealed. Other letters since had told him how she died, in three different ways. An illness since the spring. A shut room and a brazier, to keep her safe, and in the morning the room very quiet. Coals from the brazier, and her own hand, after days of being watched. He had read each once and asked no one which was true.

He laid his hand flat on the letters for a moment. Then he closed the clasp. He did not think he would want to read them when he was old. He did not think, now, that he would be.

# SC-88c: Sardis

POV: Cassius · Sardis
Date / place: Early 42 · the armies’ meeting on the plain; a closed room in the governor’s house; the tribunal next day
Canon refs: S1 · Plut. *Brut.* 30–35 · App. *BC* 4.63–65 · *Iliad* 1.259
Info ENTER: Rhodes sacked by Cassius; Xanthus burned under Brutus; both armies paid in Asia’s silver · EXIT: The quarrel; Favonius; Lucius Pella condemned; “Remember the Ides”
Overhear risk: Friends at the door; Favonius

---

The armies hailed them both as *Imperator* on the plain below Sardis, and Cassius, riding in under the cheering with Brutus a length ahead, thought that the men cheered louder for whichever of them had paid more recently, and that this was the one thing in Asia he did not need to count.

They embraced in front of the standards. They had not seen each other in more than a year. Brutus was thinner and greyer at the temples and his eyes had something in them Cassius had not seen before, a kind of steady tiredness, like a man who has been listening to the same sound for a long time. Brutus said, “Rhodes,” into his ear in the middle of the embrace, quietly, the way a man says the name of a mutual friend who has died. Cassius said, “Xanthus,” back.

Then they let go, and smiled for the legions, and went up into the city to the governor’s house, and shut the door.

Cassius had known the room would be like this. He had not known it would happen so quickly.

He did not afterwards remember who began. Brutus, probably. Brutus had been composing the speech on the road for weeks; Cassius could hear the periods in it, the balanced clauses, *you* and *I* weighed against each other like ingots. Rhodes: a free city, an old ally, a city of philosophers, and Cassius had taken its harbour by storm and its treasury by force and some of its leading men by name, and left it thousands of talents poorer. The Lycians, fined until they sold their temple plate. Laodicea stripped after Dolabella was dead, the dead man’s town made to pay for the dead man. *We are not pirates*, Brutus said. Cassius had heard one of his own officers say the same words in an audience hall in the winter, too loud, and had thought then what he thought now: that men who were not pirates did not need to say so.

“And Xanthus?” he said.

Brutus stopped.

“You besieged a free city,” Cassius said. “Of Lycians. Who didn’t want you. And when they wouldn’t open, and you got in, and offered them terms, they burned their own houses with their wives and children inside rather than take your terms. Your mercy. Your famous mercy. They jumped into the fires off their own roofs to get away from it.” He heard his own voice going up and did not stop it. “You offered your soldiers a reward for every Xanthian saved. They saved a hundred and fifty. Out of a city. I took Rhodes’s money. You took Xanthus’s *people*, Brutus, all of them, and you wept over it so beautifully that the men call you the gentlest general in the world.”

Brutus’s face had gone white. Cassius had seen it go white like that once before, in March, when a slave whispered in his ear in the porch that his wife was dead.

“I did not want them to die,” Brutus said.

“No. You wanted them to be grateful. They preferred the fire.” Cassius sat down. His hands were shaking. He put them flat on the table. “Neither of us is clean, Brutus. We stopped being clean the morning we went into the Curia with knives. The difference between us is that I know it.”

Brutus said something. Cassius said something worse. After a while neither of them was making periods any more. Brutus was crying, which he did easily and without shame, as he had in Athens over a line of Plato; Cassius was not, which cost him more. They said everything. The money Brutus had asked for at Smyrna, which Cassius had given, a third, against the advice of his officers. The letters each had written to Rome about the other. Antony. The night of GO. Porcia, once, from Cassius, and he was sorry the moment it was out, and did not say so.

Outside the door their friends stood in a knot in the corridor and listened to two commanders of the Republic shouting at each other like brothers dividing an inheritance, and did not dare go in.

Favonius dared.

He came in past the doorkeepers, who tried to stop him and failed because he was old and noisy and a senator, a little bald man in a philosopher’s threadbare cloak who had been Cato’s shadow for twenty years and imitated Cato’s manners so faithfully that he had become a kind of joke of them. He stood in the middle of the floor between the two of them, raised one hand like an actor, and declaimed, in Greek, in a fine cracked voice:

“*But listen to me. You are both younger than I am.*”

Nestor. Old Nestor in the *Iliad*, stepping between Agamemnon and Achilles when they were quarrelling over a girl.

Cassius laughed. He could not help it. He laughed so suddenly and so hard that it hurt his ribs, and he put his head down on his arms on the table, and found that some of the laughing was the other thing after all.

Brutus did not laugh. Brutus stood up, very straight, and called Favonius a dog and a counterfeit Cynic, which was true, and told him to get out, and Favonius went, with dignity, and the doorkeepers closed the door behind him, and the quarrel, which had been alive a moment before, was dead on the floor between them.

They looked at each other.

“He’ll want to dine with us,” Cassius said.

“He will.” Brutus wiped his eyes. “He always does.”

He did. That night at dinner Favonius came in uninvited, freshly bathed, and lay down on the couch at the top between them, where the guest of honour lay, and nobody moved him, and the dinner was cheerful, and full of philosophy, and Cassius found he could almost breathe.

The next day Brutus condemned Lucius Pella.

Pella was a former praetor, one of Brutus’s own officers, who had been taking money from the Sardians on the side, for himself, out of the silver they were paying to the army. Brutus heard the case on the tribunal in the morning, in public, and found him guilty and stripped him of his rank and disgraced him, and the Sardians in the square applauded.

Cassius heard of it at noon, and went to him.

“Two of my own friends did the same thing last month,” he said. “I spoke to them privately. Fined them. Kept them. This isn’t a season for nice points, Brutus. We need every officer we have and we need them not to hate us. You’ve made one an enemy and frightened twenty for a few thousand drachmas.”

“Remember the Ides,” Brutus said.

Cassius waited.

“We killed Caesar,” Brutus said, “not because he stole himself, but because he let his friends steal, and protected them. That was the charge. That was the whole charge, under the rest. If we let our own friends rob the cities in the name of the Republic, we had better have let Caesar live.”

He said it quietly. He was not making a period. He meant it, Cassius saw, entirely, the way he had meant *GO*, and it was unanswerable, and it was also exactly the kind of sentence that loses wars.

Cassius did not answer it. He went back to his own quarters and sat for a long time with the accounts for the march into Thrace, the grain and the pay and the ships, and found that every column came out the same whichever way he added it, and that none of them had a line for what Brutus had said.
