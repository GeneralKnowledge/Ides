# SC-82: Allies Evaporate

POV: Decimus · roads after Mutina
Date / place: Late Apr → Sept 43 · Mutina, Cularo under the Alps, north Italian roads
Canon refs: A10 end · death path fog OK
Info ENTER: Siege broken · EXIT: Flight; death-on-road
Overhear risk: Dwindling staff

---

Decimus left Mutina after the relief had made the wall honest again. Hirtius’s dirt still drying on the road. Antony thinned but not finished. The town’s hinges finally someone else’s problem for a morning. At the first milestone past the gate he waited half that morning for Plancus’s promised meeting-mark. No cohorts. No date. Only dust that refused to become wagons. Near noon a rider from the Lepidus channel he had seeded after the funeral handed back Decimus’s latest wax unopened. Same averted eyes as the messenger who would not take a return tablet on the road north in March. “The meeting near the approaches cannot yet be kept,” the man said, practising courtesy. “My principal sends goodwill.” Goodwill without a roof. Decimus kept the returned tablet.

He looked for a destination mark on the itinerary and found only blank margin where a meeting date should have been. He rubbed the blank with his thumb until the wax warmed and still showed nothing. Antony, bleeding but unbroken, was already sliding toward friends Decimus could not buy.

Cicero’s praise arrived sweet and late. Decimus read one line of congratulation twice, then folded it into a pack already too light on grain. He wrote one more letter that tried to sound steady, gates held, honour intact. He stopped mid-sentence when his hand cramped. He didn’t send a second copy.

At Eporedia, at the foot of the Alps, in the last week of May, Segulius Labeo rode in with the dust of the boy’s camp still on him and ate Decimus’s bread as if he had been invited. Labeo went everywhere. That was his use. Over the second cup he told Decimus that he had been with young Caesar, that they had talked a great deal about Cicero, and that the boy had no complaint against Cicero at all, none, except for one saying that was going about.

“*Laudandum adulescentem,*” Labeo said, “*ornandum, tollendum.*” He waited for Decimus to laugh. Decimus didn’t. “The boy says he has no intention of being lifted,” Labeo added. “In either sense.”

That night Decimus wrote it to Cicero exactly as he had heard it, with Labeo’s name, because a man in his position could not afford to soften news for an old man’s feelings. He wrote that the veterans were talking too, that they believed Cicero meant them harm, and that the boy’s soldiers were not a mood to be joked with. He asked again for money. He did not write what he thought, which was that the best tongue in Rome had found the one word in Latin that could not be taken back, and spent it at dinner.

Plancus came after all, in June, when Antony had already walked into Lepidus’s camp and there was no longer anything between Plancus and Antony but a river and a decision.

Decimus brought his army over the passes to meet him at Cularo, a town of the Allobroges where the Isara came down green out of the mountains. Plancus rode out to greet him a mile from the walls with a mounted escort in clean cloaks. He smelled, as his couriers smelled, of an Eastern scent that cost more than a horse. He embraced Decimus from the saddle and said that the Republic had two arms again.

“And one head,” he said, laughing, “and I’ll let you decide whose.”

They camped side by side on the plain below the town, Plancus’s veterans on the higher ground by the river, Decimus’s army on the lower. It was a strange army to bring to a reunion. Legions of recruits from the levy, boys who had learned to dig at Mutina and little else, and a few cohorts of veterans who had stayed with Decimus through the siege because they had nowhere else to go. Plancus had more, and the best of them were old ones from Caesar’s Gaul. Decimus walked Plancus’s lines on the second day, invited, and counted without seeming to count, as he had walked Caesar’s lines in Gaul for ten years. Plancus’s men were well fed. They looked at Decimus with interest and without warmth.

They wrote to Rome together. That was the work of June: letters. Plancus wrote beautifully. Decimus had read his letters to Cicero over the winter and thought them the best prose in the army and the least committed. Now he watched them being made. Plancus dictated walking up and down the tent with a cup in his hand, pausing for the right word, sending the secretary back two lines to change *our* to *the Republic’s*. He wrote that he and Decimus were of one mind. He wrote that Antony and Lepidus together were too strong to be attacked by the two of them alone, and that they would hold the line of the Isara and wait. He wrote, every week, that the young Caesar must be sent north with his legions, and the Senate must make him come.

“He won’t come,” Decimus said, the first time.

“Then the Senate will look foolish, and we will look patient.” Plancus sealed the letter. “Patience is very underrated in a general.”

“So is arriving.”

Plancus laughed, and poured, and changed the subject to his brother Plotius in Rome, who wrote him the gossip, and was a fool about money, and wore too much of the same scent.

Decimus wrote his own letters too, shorter. He asked for pay. He asked for grain. He wrote to Cicero that the recruits were good boys and would be soldiers by the autumn if anybody fed them till then. He wrote, in one letter he did not send, that he had spent his life being trusted by men he was not sure of, and that he had never before been on the other side of it.

The valley went brown. Two armies could eat a valley in a summer, and they did. The foragers went further every week, into the hills, and came back with less, and with stories about Antony’s cavalry seen across the river. Across the river, a day’s march south, Antony and Lepidus sat together in one camp with more legions than the two of them could count, and did not attack, and did not need to.

In the second week of August a rider came up from the south with the news from Rome, and Plancus read it first, because Plancus’s riders were faster.

He came to Decimus’s tent with the tablet in his hand and his face arranged. Decimus had seen that arrangement on a hundred faces in Caesar’s atrium, the face of a man bringing news he had already decided how to feel about.

“The boy is consul,” Plancus said.

Decimus took the tablet. Nineteen years old. Elected with his legions camped on the Field of Mars. His colleague a cousin, Pedius, an old man who would do what he was told. And the first law of the new consuls: a court to try the murderers of Caesar. It had sat already. It had condemned them in a day, in their absence, every man of the Ides, with no one to speak for them.

His own name was there. He read it twice.

“Interdicted from fire and water,” Plancus said gently. “It won’t mean anything up here, of course. It’s Rome. It’s a law.”

“Laws are what we told ourselves we were for.”

“We told ourselves a great many things.” Plancus took the tablet back, and Decimus let him. “I am still your colleague, Decimus. Nothing in Rome changes the valley.”

Nothing in Rome changed the valley. The valley changed on its own. After the news the recruits began talking in the evenings in a way they had not talked before. The veterans in Plancus’s lines stopped looking at Decimus with interest. And letters began coming for Plancus from the west, from Pollio in Spain, who had three legions and a habit of writing to everyone and committing to no one, under seals Decimus was not shown.

He did not ask to be shown them. He thought about it for three days. He was very good at reading men by what they wanted, and he knew what asking would buy: a laugh, a cup, a charming account of what was in the letters, and a lie about the rest. And it would tell Plancus that he knew. Better to let Plancus think he was still trusted. There was a little time in that.

On the fourth day he asked anyway, at dinner, because he found he could not bear the cup and the laugh one more evening without it.

“Pollio,” he said. “What does he want?”

Plancus did not pretend not to understand. That was something. He set down his cup and looked at it.

“He wants what everyone wants,” he said. “He wants to be on the side that is still standing in the spring. He thinks there will be peace between Antony and the boy. He thinks the men who were in the Curia that morning are the price of it.” He looked up. “I have not answered him.”

“You haven’t answered me either.”

“No,” said Plancus. “I haven’t.”

They finished dinner. Plancus was charming for the rest of it, and told a long story about a Gallic chieftain and a goose, and Decimus laughed in the right places, and they walked out of the tent together into the warm dark and stood for a moment looking at the river.

“If I go,” Plancus said, quietly, as if to the river, “I will send you word first. I owe you that.”

“You owe me your legions.”

“I owe the Republic my legions,” said Plancus. “I am trying to work out where it is.”

He did not send word first. Decimus woke one morning in September before light because his horse was restless, and went up on the bank of the camp ditch with his cloak over his shoulders, and saw that the higher ground by the river was moving. Plancus’s camp was being struck, not in haste: tents coming down in order, cohort by cohort, wagons being loaded, the eagle of the first legion already out in front of its column on the road toward the bridge Plancus’s engineers had built across the Isara in the spring and never yet used.

Decimus stood and watched it. He did not wake his staff. There was nothing his staff could do, and he wanted to see it alone, the way he had wanted to be alone on the wall-walk at Mutina in the winter.

The columns crossed the bridge in the grey light, one after another, the old Gallic legions first, then the new ones, and went away south down the far bank toward Antony and Lepidus. It took most of the morning. At the end Plancus himself rode across, with his clean escort, and halfway over the bridge he reined in and turned in the saddle and looked back at the camp on the low ground. Decimus could not see his face at that distance. He could see that he raised a hand.

Decimus did not raise his.

A rider came up from the bridge an hour later with a letter, beautifully written, sealed with Plancus’s seal, saying that circumstances had compelled him and that he remained Decimus’s friend. Decimus read it and gave it to the older aide to file with the others. He gave orders for a count of rations. He went to his tent and sat down and found that his hands were steady, and was mildly surprised.

By noon the recruits had begun to go, not all at once and not with noise. A tent-group here, a century there, walking out of the gate with their shields on their backs as if they had been sent on fatigues, and not coming back. Some went south over the bridge after Plancus. Some went east toward the boy, whose name was on the law that had condemned their general. Some just went home. The centurions did not stop them. The centurions were going too.

Decimus watched from the bank of the ditch. He counted them as they went, from habit, as he had counted cohorts at Massilia and hinges at Mutina, and when the number became large enough that it stopped being a number he stopped counting and went to look at the maps.

Then he rode with dwindling staff into country that did not care who freed the Republic. Rain made the road a trough. An aide counted purses and got a number that made him quiet. Another counted betrayals and got a higher number. A third asked whether they should wait for Cassius from the East.

“Wait here and the road learns our number,” Decimus said.

“They will say I did not pursue,” the first aide muttered.

“Let them,” Decimus said. “We take the west fork before dark. The east ford is watched. I smelled horse-lines on the wind this morning.”

At a crossroads shrine someone had left a cheap wreath for “the liberators.” Rain had ruined it. Decimus didn’t stop to fix it. He checked his horse’s mouth for sores and found none.

They slept once in a barn that smelled of wet hay and someone else’s fear. In the dark Decimus thought, briefly, without speech, of Caesar’s atrium and the cheer he had worn like a clean cloak on a March morning. Trust had been the key. He put the thought away the way he put away a broken strap: later, if later existed.

On the second day the staff was smaller. One man had “gone for water” and not returned. Decimus didn’t send a search.

Near a river ford the map promised a contact who could pass them toward the mountains and, beyond rumour, toward Cassius. The contact was three men with ordinary faces and a mule. One smiled too quickly. Decimus’s remaining aide whispered, “Lepidus’s smile.”

“Or anybody’s price,” Decimus said.

He still dismounted. Refusing the ford meant the open road behind, where Antony’s friends were thicker. He kept his hands visible. Appearances, even here.

They asked for his name. They already knew it. He gave it because lying would have bought a few breaths instead of miles. A purse changed hands: not his purse. Someone else’s coin for his head. He counted the pieces by glint before the knives moved, enough metal for a short road and a long silence.

There was no speech. He went for the horse. A hand caught his cloak. Mud took his knee. The ditch at the ford’s edge was shallow and ordinary: meltwater, a broken rein, the smell of wet wool.

If he thought of Caesar’s laugh one last time, the thought stayed inside.

# SC-82a: The Legion That Stood

POV: Brutus · Macedonia, the camp
Date / place: June 43 · the parade ground; the gate of his quarters
Canon refs: T13 · T8 · Cic. *Ad Brut.* 1.9 · Plut. *Brut.* 26, 53 · pays Ch 34 SC-88b (the standing mutiny; the June letter)
Info ENTER: Gaius Antonius working on the legions · EXIT: Porcia dead; Brutus does not ask how; the legion moves; Gaius to the ship

---

On the second morning the legion stood again.

It stood on the parade ground in its lines with its shields grounded and its standards up, in good order, and when the trumpet went it did not move. Nobody shouted. Nobody threw anything. Five thousand men stood in the sun as if they had been told to, and looked across the camp at the good house where Gaius Antonius had dined with their centurions every night that week.

Brutus watched from the gate of his quarters. He had a speech ready. He had had it ready since the first morning, and each time he looked at the lines it seemed more likely that the speech was what they were waiting for, so that they could refuse to listen to it.

“They want to be asked,” said one of his officers.

“They want to be paid,” said another.

“They want to be his brother’s,” Brutus said, “and they do not want to say so.”

A rider came up the coast road in the middle of that, grey with dust, with his mother’s seal on the bag, and the guards let him through. It was Servilia’s freedman, the old one who had carried her letters for thirty years and had once carried Brutus on his shoulders round the atrium to look at the masks. He got down off the horse badly. He did not hand over the bag. He stood in front of Brutus with it held against his chest, and Brutus knew from the way he held it.

“Tell me,” Brutus said.

“The lady Porcia, master. In Rome. Before the Kalends.”

Brutus nodded. He found he was still nodding and stopped.

“Your mother writes that she had been...”

“No,” Brutus said. “Not how. Not yet.”

The freedman opened the bag. There were three letters on top, and the uppermost had Cicero’s seal. Cicero must have written the same day the news went round the Palatine and sent it to Servilia’s house to travel with hers, because Cicero would always know which courier was fastest. Brutus took that one. He could bear Cicero. Cicero would be well written.

It was. It told him he was a philosopher, and that philosophers were watched. It told him that the whole army was looking to see how he bore it, and that a man who had read what he had read would know how to set a measure to grief.

He looked up from the wax. Across the parade ground five thousand men were looking at him.

They had seen the rider. They had seen the old man’s face, and the bag held like that, and their general at his gate reading. Word was already going down the ranks the way word does in a camp, faster than a trumpet: the front cohort, then the second, a turn of heads like wind going over barley.

He gave the letter to the freedman. “Put it with the others.”

Then he walked out onto the parade ground without his lictors and without the speech. The sun was hot on his head. He walked until he was in front of the first cohort, near enough to see the sweat on the standard-bearer’s lip, and he stopped there and said nothing at all. He had nothing he could say that would not be either about her or not about her, and both were impossible.

“Sound it,” he said to the trumpeter, over his shoulder.

The trumpet went.

For three breaths nothing happened. Then the centurion of the front cohort, a broad Picene with a scar through one eyebrow who had dined at Gaius’s table two nights running, lifted his shield off the ground. His men lifted theirs. Down the line, cohort by cohort, the shields came up, and the standards dipped and rose, and the legion turned the way it was meant to turn and marched off the parade ground to its drill.

Brutus stood and watched it go. He knew why they had moved. It was not the Republic, and it was not his speech, and it was not the pay chest. They had moved because their general’s wife was dead and he had walked out into the sun with no words, and they could not bring themselves to make him stand there.

He would have given a great deal for it to have been the Republic.

That evening he gave the order for the ship. Gaius would be well treated, with his own servants and a guard, anchored off the coast and out of reach of anyone’s dinner. Brutus wrote it to the Senate as a measure for Gaius’s own safety, and it was well written.

His mother’s letter he did not open. He knew it would tell him how. He put it in the cedar box with Cicero’s, unopened, and sat with his hand on the lid until the lamp went out on its own. He thought of her hand in March, hot in his until the window greyed, and her voice saying *I can*.

# SC-83: Consul

POV: Octavian · approach to Rome
Date / place: May → autumn 43 · the camp; the Rubicon; Rome: the Vestals’ house, the Forum, the Field of Mars
Canon refs: A12 prep · C5: learning, not serene · S5 · Suet. *Aug.* 26, 95 · App. *BC* 3.88–94
Info ENTER: Armies after Mutina; both consuls dead; Senate favours Decimus · EXIT: Consul (19 Aug) with Pedius; boy with fasces; Decimus’s death reaches him as incomplete rumour; Bononia invited
Overhear risk: Soldiers + Senate terror

---

Octavian entered the politics of force with hands that still remembered shaking in Apollonia.

A courier brought the Senate’s thanks for Mutina, and the thanks were not for him. Decimus had the triumph and the dead consuls’ legions, the Martians among them. Octavian was to be thanked and left behind. He read the decree once and gave it to Agrippa, who read it twice.

Segulius Labeo came to dinner in the camp that month, because Labeo came to dinner everywhere, and brought a saying with him the way other guests brought a jar. He told it at the end of the meal, in front of the tribunes, and watched Octavian’s face while he told it. *Laudandum adulescentem, ornandum, tollendum.*

The tribunes laughed and then stopped, unsure whether they were allowed. Agrippa did not laugh at all.

“Which way does he mean it?” Octavian asked.

“All three, I should think,” said Labeo. “That’s the art.”

Octavian turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. “Tell your friends I have no intention of being lifted. In either sense.” He said it pleasantly. Labeo went away pleased with the answer, which he would carry to the next table, and Octavian sat on after the others had gone with the three verbs going round in his head in their order, like coins being counted, and found that he could not make the last one come out as only a joke.

“They will say you let Antony get away,” Agrippa said.

Octavian’s throat tightened once, shame or fever; he didn’t sort it. He looked at the pay chests, not at Agrippa’s face. “Then we pay before we argue. Men who are owed invent worse stories than senators do.”

He had the clerks open the nearest chest where Agrippa could see the count. Late thanks; complete coin. The order of those facts was the lesson he was learning with his hands. When a tribune started a sentence about the Senate’s honours, Octavian cut him with a nod toward the queue of soldiers. “Honours after the line is fed.”

A centurion from the Martians said it first, loud enough for the pay line: “They want us under the man who held Caesar’s door. Who feeds us now, old men with tablets, or Caesar’s name?” A tribune seconded without looking at Octavian’s face: the men would not march for an assassin on praise that arrived late and sweet. Agrippa translated the noise into one sentence Octavian could keep. The ranks wanted a consul who was *theirs*: paymaster, name, a face young enough to insult the old seats. Two chairs were empty.

Philippus wrote from Rome in the stepfather’s hand Octavian had learned to read sideways, every warning folded inside a piece of news. The House was frightened and could not say of what. Decimus was a Liberator with a triumph and a thinning army, not a consul. Antony was over the Alps with Lepidus’s legions. Some of the fathers wanted a consul who obeyed and would settle for one who was young. And then, as if he were only reminding a boy of his lessons: the law wanted forty-two years for the chair. *You have nineteen. Count them before anyone else does.*

Octavian counted them. Then he sent the centurions.

There were four hundred of them in the end, centurions and picked men from every legion, because the ranks would not trust a smaller number to come back with the truth. They went down to Rome in July with the soldiers’ asks in writing: the money the Senate had voted them and not paid, and a consulship for the man who paid. Octavian did not go. Agrippa did not go. Octavian walked the camp in the days they were away and was polite to everyone and slept badly, and on the eighth night he was sick behind the horse lines and told no one.

They came back angry. The fathers had kept them standing in the porch, and then had them in, and then talked to them as if they were a deputation of farmers about a road. A senator had asked whose idea the consulship was. Another had said, kindly, that the law wanted forty-two years.

A centurion called Cornelius told it at the pay line. He was a broad, slow man with an old burn down one forearm, and he told it the way he must have done it, without hurry. He took his cloak by the shoulder and threw it back, so the men could see the sword at his hip.

“And I said to them, in the House, *If you will not make him consul, this will.*”

The pay line roared. Octavian, at the chest, kept his face still and his fingers on the lid. He had not told Cornelius to say it. He had not told him not to.

“Well,” said Agrippa, under the noise. “That’s the letter answered.”

They crossed the little river at the border of Italy late in July, with the eight legions behind them. Octavian rode ahead with the picked cohorts. The water was low, the colour of weak wine, and the horses did not need to swim. He had expected to feel something at it, because it was the same river, and men in the column were already saying so to each other, *his father’s river*, as if it had been in the will with the gardens. He felt his horse’s shoulder shift under him on the stones. He felt the sun on the back of his neck. On the far side he reined in and let the cohorts go past, and watched the faces, and found that the men felt it for him, and that this was more use.

The Senate sent envoys to meet him on the road with money. Two days later it sent riders after the envoys to take the money back, because two legions had landed from Africa and the fathers had found their courage again. Octavian heard both pieces of news in one evening, and laughed for the first time in a month, and could not stop until Agrippa put a cup in his hand.

“They’re counting legions,” Agrippa said. “It’s progress.”

The two African legions went over to him outside the walls. The urban praetor, Cornutus, who had been given the defence of the city with them, fell on his sword when he heard. Octavian was told this at a well on the Via Flaminia, and stood for a while with the dipper in his hand. He had not known Cornutus. He found he wanted to know what the man had looked like, and did not ask.

His own cavalry had gone into Rome ahead of him with orders to touch nothing and tell everyone. By the time he came in through the gate there were people on the roofs. Some of them cheered. Most watched, the way people watch weather. A woman at a fountain held her child against her skirts and did not look up at all, and Octavian, who had been composing his face for the cheering, composed it again for her.

His mother and his sister were in the house of the Vestals.

Philippus had sent them there in July, when the centurions went down, because the Vestals’ house was the one door in Rome no frightened man would kick in. The Virgins had kept the will there. They kept the flame. And for six weeks they had kept Atia and Octavia, among the girls in white, in two rooms off a courtyard that smelled of laurel smoke.

They came out to him at the steps. Octavia came first, quickly, with her skirts in her fist, and put her arms round his neck before he had finished dismounting, and he stood with one foot in the stirrup and his sister hanging on him in front of half the Forum and found he did not mind. She was twenty-six and married to a man who had been consul, and she smelled of the laurel smoke too. She said his name. His old name. Nobody had said it in a year.

Atia came after, slowly. She was thinner. Her hands were empty.

“I kept the box,” she said, when Octavia had let go of him. She meant the jewels, the ones she had set on Philippus’s table the night he sold the Velitrae land, when he had told her *not yet*. “They’re in my room. I thought,” she said, and stopped, and looked past him at the soldiers in the Forum, at the cohorts drawn up on the Sacred Way with their shields uncovered, “I thought you might need them now.”

“Not now either,” he said.

She looked at him a long moment. Then her face went blank, as it had gone blank in Philippus’s villa when he said the name aloud, as if she had expected nothing else and wished she had been wrong.

“You look ill,” she said.

“I’ve been riding.”

“You’ve been ill.” She put the back of her hand to his cheek, as she had when he was eight, and he let her, in front of the soldiers. “Eat something,” Atia said. “Before you do anything else today. Before you do anything you can’t take back.”

He ate. He ate standing up, bread and cheese from a soldier’s pack, on the steps of the Vestals’ house with his mother watching, and it was the first food he had kept down in three days.

While he ate, Octavia gave him the household news, the way she had put it in letters he had not had time to read. Calpurnia had gone to her father’s. The old house’s freedmen were scattered: one keeping a wine shop by the Circus, two gone to Lepidus with the Domus, one girl in Antony’s kitchen on the Carinae.

“She counted heads at your games for his steward,” Octavia said. “Matius heard it. Somebody laughed at the number at Antony’s dinner.”

Octavian swallowed. He remembered the cheap rows standing. “What’s her name?”

Octavia did not know. Octavian looked past her at Agrippa, who nodded once and wrote nothing down, which meant he would remember.

The senators came to meet him in the afternoon. They came in twos and threes, in their bordered togas, and made speeches on the steps about the Republic and the young Caesar’s piety, and Octavian thanked each of them by name, because he had learned all their names in the winter from Cicero’s letters and Agrippa’s lists. Some of them had voted a week before to send the African legions out against him. They did not mention it. He did not mention it either.

Cicero came last.

He came late in the day with Tiro behind him, and climbed the steps a little slowly, and made a speech that was better than the others and shorter, and took Octavian’s hand in both of his. He smelled of ink and of the Palatine. Octavian looked at him and heard, as clearly as if Labeo were at his elbow, three verbs in their order.

“You are the last of my friends to greet me,” he said.

He said it lightly, the way the old man liked things said. Cicero laughed. Tiro did not. And Octavian, who had meant it as a pleasantry when he began the sentence, found by the end of it that he had meant something else, and could not have said exactly what, and let it stand.

That night a rumour went round that two of his legions had gone back to the Senate. The fathers met by lamplight in the Curia, and Cicero stood at the door to greet them as they came in, and for an hour the old Republic was alive again in one room. Then the rumour was found to be false. Cicero went home in a closed litter, and did not come to the Field of Mars again.

Octavian heard about it in the morning, from Agrippa, over the pay tables.

“He stood at the door,” Agrippa said.

“Of course he did.” Octavian signed a tablet. “He always does.”

The treasury was in the Temple of Saturn, under the Capitol, and the keys came to him in a dish.

He did not touch them. He sent a tribune with a guard and a clerk with tablets, and they brought the chests down the temple steps to the Forum in daylight, where the whole city could watch them, and set them out on trestles in the square. Twenty-five hundred a man: the first payment of what the Senate had voted and not paid. The rest promised. The clerks called the names by century, and the men came up in their order, and took their coin, and signed, or made their mark, and went away. It took two days. Octavian sat behind the tables for most of both, and when a tribune leaned in on the first afternoon to say the fathers had sent to ask when the election would be held, he did not look up.

“Honours after the line is fed,” he said.

One man in the line he remembered afterwards. A centurion in boots resoled more than once came up with his century on the second afternoon, took his coin, made his mark, and then did not move on. He asked the clerk whether the roll still had his plot on it, by Capua, fourth line from the Volturnus road. The clerk found it with his thumb and said that the stones were a matter for after. The centurion nodded as if he had expected nothing else and went away with the coin in his fist. *Laberius*, the clerk had written. Octavian had the line copied on to a separate tablet and put it with the list of his father’s freedmen that Agrippa was making, and could not have said why.

The line was fed on the second evening. The election was on the nineteenth.

They held it on the Field of Mars, where the voting pens were, with the legions camped round it close enough to hear. Octavian had wanted Pedius for his colleague, and Pedius was willing. Pedius was his cousin, another grandson of Caesar’s sister, a share of the last quarter of the will to Octavian’s three, an elderly man who had fought in Spain for his uncle and wanted, now, only to be left a quiet old age. He stood next to Octavian in the white toga of a candidate and coughed into his fist. The centuries came up in their order and voted, and the heralds called them, and the count was never in doubt, and everyone knew it, and still they counted, because counting was what made it a law.

The augur had taken the auspices at dawn. Octavian had stood beside him on the hill in the cold, and watched him mark out the sky with his staff, and they had waited. Birds came over from the river. Vultures, the augur said. Six of them. They went over high and slow and away toward the Janiculum.

Later, while Octavian was speaking to the soldiers, more came over. The men in the front ranks saw them and pointed, and a noise went back through the legions, and by the time Octavian had finished speaking someone was saying twelve, and someone else was saying that twelve had come to Romulus when he founded the city, and by evening the whole camp was saying it.

Agrippa had counted. He said six, and then six. He said it was a hot summer and the river at the bottom of the Field had dead things in it.

“Which is it?” Octavian asked.

“Both, I should think.” Agrippa looked at the sky, which was empty now. “That’s the art.”

Octavian looked at the empty sky a moment longer than he meant to.

He felt sick after the count, stomach, not omen. A taste of metal rose; he swallowed it and kept his fingers on the chest lid until the shake stopped. Publicly he accepted the fasces like owed homework. Twelve lictors shouldered the bound rods and walked ahead of him, and men stepped out of the street for the rods before they looked to see whose face came behind. A herald stumbled over *consul* and *nineteen* in the same breath, as if the two words had never met. Octavian let him find his way to the end.

Pedius had his first law ready before the lictors had learned the way to the new consul’s door: a court for the men of the Ides. It sat in a day. Nobody spoke for the absent, because nobody was fool enough to. Octavian read the list of the condemned at supper, and Decimus’s name was on it, and Casca’s, and Brutus’s and Cassius’s at the top, and he put the tablet face down beside his plate and ate what was on the plate, because his mother had told him to.

“They will hate this,” Agrippa said.

Octavian wiped sweat from his upper lip with the back of his hand, ugly, visible, unfinished. “They hated the knives more. Let them hate a boy who pays after the line eats.”

Agrippa’s mouth twitched, almost the old Apollonia play, then not. “Homework with fasces. Only you could make a consulship sound like a debt you intend to clear before bedtime.” Octavian did not smile. Smiling would have spoiled the pay line. He let the twitch count as the joke landing and kept his fingers on the chest.

By autumn a courier brought Decimus’s end in two lines: a ford in the hills, and somebody’s purse. Octavian did not ask whose. It was enough for the ranks that the last Liberator in Italy was gone, and the north had no one left to hold it but Antony and Lepidus. In the same week a tablet came under two seals pressed side by side in the same wax, Lepidus’s and Antony’s: talk, on the river island below Bononia, between the camps, where no city could listen.
