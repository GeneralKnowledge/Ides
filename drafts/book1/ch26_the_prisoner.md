The Athenians put up his statue in the Agora in the second week, next to the tyrant-killers.

Brutus went to see it because not going would have been noticed. It was bronze, new, a little too tall, and it stood beside Harmodius and Aristogeiton, who had been standing there in bronze for four hundred years with their swords out and their weight on the forward foot. A second statue for Cassius stood on the other side, empty-handed, waiting for Cassius to arrive and deserve it. The magistrates who had voted them came to watch him look. One made a speech about liberty in Greek so good that Brutus wanted to correct nothing in it, which was itself a kind of warning.

“They have made you very handsome,” said Theomnestus that afternoon, at the Academy.

“They have made me very still,” said Brutus. “I was not still.”

The old man laughed and went back to the lecture. Brutus sat on a stone bench among boys twenty years younger than he was and listened to an argument about whether a man could know the good and still not do it. He had come to Athens, he told people, to read. It was true. He went to Theomnestus in the mornings and to Cratippus in the afternoons, and walked between the two schools under the plane trees with a tablet he did not write on, and for whole hours at a time nobody asked him about money.

Then somebody did.

He was a broad young man of twenty-one with none of his father’s caution, who came up to Brutus at the gate of the Lyceum after Cratippus’s lecture with his toga over the wrong shoulder and wine on his breath at the eighth hour.

“Marcus Cicero,” he said. “The son. Not the one who writes.” He grinned. “My father writes to me twice a week about my debts and my drinking and my Greek, in that order. He writes to you about the Republic. I thought you should know we’re on the same list.” He was enjoying himself. “Trebonius came through in the spring on his way to Asia. He took me to dinner and wrote my father that I was a reformed character. My father believed him for almost a month.”

Brutus had read some of those letters to the son. Half of Rome had. The father sent copies of his advice to friends, the way other men sent copies of their speeches. Now the father was writing a whole book of it, on duty, addressed to *my son Marcus*, which the son had promised to read and possibly would.

“What do you want, Marcus?”

“A horse.” The young man did not hesitate. “And something to do with it. I can’t sit in Cratippus’s garden and hear about the good life while you raise legions down the road. Everyone knows you’re raising legions.”

“I’m reading,” said Brutus.

“Everyone knows that too.” Marcus Cicero put out his hand. “I can ride. I can’t do much else. My father says I’m a disappointment to philosophy. Let me be one to Antony.”

Brutus took the hand before he had decided to. That was how it went, that autumn. The young men of the schools came to him one at a time, sons of senators sent to Athens to be finished, who had heard about the Ides in a lecture hall and felt they had missed it. They came to him under the plane trees with their tablets and their good Greek and their fathers’ money running short, and asked for a command. He gave them commands. There were not many Romans in Athens, and those there were had been trained from birth to hold a sword and a law in the same hand.

One of them asked for nothing. Statilius came in October, with the dust of the Piraeus road on him and no letter, because he had never needed one at Brutus’s table, and sat at the back of Theomnestus’s lectures as if those were what he had crossed the sea for. When Brutus asked what command he wanted he said none. He had come because a friend was here and he was not doing anything else. He took the couch nearest the door at dinner and argued with everyone, and he did not once mention that dinner.

The last one he took was not a senator’s son.

He was small and quick, with a round face and hair already going early at the temples, and he stood at the back of Theomnestus’s lectures and laughed a beat before anyone else at the jokes, as if he had seen them coming. Someone told Brutus his father had been a slave. A freedman from Venusia in Apulia, a collector at auctions, who had sold up everything to send his son to the best schools in Rome and then to Athens, and walked behind him to his lessons in the city with the boy’s satchel, as if he were the boy’s own slave.

“Quintus Horatius,” the young man said, when Brutus sent for him. “Flaccus. Which means flap-eared, if you were being too polite to ask.”

“I was being too polite to ask.”

“That’s what they told me about you.” Horace looked at the letter of appointment on the table and did not touch it. “You know what my father was.”

“I know what you are.” Brutus pushed the letter across. “Tribune. A legion’s worth of men who will hate you for it, because of what your father was. They whispered about me for whom my mother loved. You’ll find it’s survivable.”

Horace picked it up then. He read it twice, which Brutus liked, and put it in the fold of his tunic, and said, “I write verse. Bad verse, so far.”

“Then don’t write about me,” said Brutus.

“No,” Horace agreed. “I don’t suppose I will.”

At the end of the autumn Quintus Hortensius came down from the north to dine with him.

He was the orator’s son, with his father’s good looks gone soft and his father’s name worn thin with use, and Macedonia was his province, and he had held it for a year without anything happening in it. He arrived at Brutus’s house below the Acropolis with an escort and a carriage full of wine from his own estates, and was charming through four courses, and at the fifth he set down his cup.

“Antony has given my province to his brother,” he said. “Gaius. You know Gaius.”

“I know the family jaw.”

“Everyone knows the jaw.” Hortensius turned the cup by its foot. “Gaius is on his way with a commission from the people and a great many debts, and he will expect me to hand him what troops his brother hasn’t already shipped to Brundisium, and the treasury at Thessalonica, and go home to my fish ponds like my father. And I find,” he said, carefully, “that I would rather hand them to you.”

Brutus said nothing for a moment. Through the open door the city was going to bed, lamp by lamp, below the dark bulk of the rock.

“You understand what you’re offering.”

“I understand that Antony has already taken Macedonia’s best legions to Italy and means to have the province too,” said Hortensius, “and I understand that you are here reading philosophy with forty young men who all want horses. I am not a brave man, Brutus. My sister got all the courage in our family. But I can count.”

So Macedonia came to Brutus in Athens, over the fifth course, from a man who could count. He wrote it to Cicero that night as a thing done for the Republic, and it was. He did not write that he had felt, when Hortensius set down the cup, the same quick warmth in his chest he had felt as a young man when Caesar’s hand fell on his shoulder after Pharsalus: the warmth of being chosen. He had disliked it then. He disliked it now, and kept it.
