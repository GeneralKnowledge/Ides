# SC-11b: The First Praetorship

POV: Brutus · Domus Publica, Caesar’s work room
Date / place: Late December 45 · evening · Domus Publica
Canon refs: Plut. *Brut.* 6–8 · *Caes.* 62 · Cic. *Att.* 14.1 (Nicaea) · Caesar’s *Anticato* (45) · pays Ch 7 SC-15 (Cassius has the junior praetorship)
Info ENTER: Brutus and Cassius both want the city praetorship · EXIT: Brutus has it; Caesar’s warmth seen, not recalled; Cassius goes in after
Overhear risk: Household high
**G1:** Brutus POV; the one room Caesar and Brutus share before the Curia

---

The outer court of the Domus Publica was full of men pretending not to wait. Brutus knew most of them by the backs of their heads. Cassius stood by the cold brazier with his arms folded, the way he stood on a ship’s deck, and pretended nothing.

“He’ll see you first,” Cassius said.

“He sent for us both.”

“He sent for us both at the same hour. That is a different thing.” Cassius looked at the door to the inner rooms. “Go on. I’ll warm the brazier with my temper.”

A slave led Brutus through rooms that smelled of lamp oil and old priests. The chief priest of Rome lived in the house of its religion and kept his papers there like a general’s baggage. Caesar sat at a table under two lamps with lists spread in front of him, and his secretary Faberius at the far end copied names in a hand so small it looked like seed.

“Marcus,” Caesar said, and stood, which he did not need to do. “Sit. You look like a man who has come to be sentenced.”

“I have come to be told.”

“In this house that is the same thing.” Caesar turned one of the tablets so Brutus could read it. Next year, written out: the consuls, the praetors, the provinces after. Brutus found his own name before he found the words beside it. *Praetor urbanus.* The city praetorship. The court in the Forum, the edicts, the games of Apollo in July. The first of the praetors, the one men remembered.

Two lines below: *C. Cassius Longinus, inter peregrinos.* The praetor for foreigners. A Greek and a Gaul quarrelling over a cargo of hides.

“Cassius has served longer,” Brutus said. He had promised himself on the walk over that he would say it, and he was relieved to hear that he had. “He held Syria after Carrhae with what was left of Crassus’s army. He beat the Parthians at Antigonea when nobody in Rome could have found it on a map.”

“I know. I have read his letters. He writes them as if the Senate were a slow schoolboy.” Caesar took the stylus from Faberius, looked at the tablet, and handed it back. “Cassius has the juster claim. But Brutus must have the first.”

Faberius wrote nothing. It was already written.

Brutus waited to be sent away, and was not. Caesar called for wine and then forgot to drink it. He asked about Cisalpine Gaul, where Brutus had governed the year before, and listened to the answer, which was rare enough in Rome that Brutus caught himself talking too long. Caesar had ridden through the province on his way home from Spain and found the towns grateful instead of merely afraid, and he said so, and named two of the towns correctly.

“And you have been writing,” Caesar said.

“A little.”

“A little. Your *Cato* reached me in Spain. So did Cicero’s. I had a war to finish and two books telling me Cato was the last Roman, so I wrote back from camp.” He smiled. “You have read my answer?”

“I have.”

“It isn’t good. It’s angry, which is worse than bad. I lost my temper with a dead man, and the dead always win that.” He tapped the table. “Write me another and I will answer that too. We will keep the copyists fed.”

Brutus laughed, and heard himself laugh, and could not take it back.

Caesar talked about Nicaea, two summers gone, when Brutus had stood in front of him and spoken for King Deiotarus, who had backed Pompey and wanted to keep his kingdom anyway. “I sat there thinking, it matters a great deal what this young man wants. Because whatever he wants, he wants hard.” He meant it as praise. Brutus took it as praise, and only later, walking home, wondered who else had heard it said.

Faberius trimmed the lamps and went back to his seed-small names. Two doors away the court was still waiting. Caesar talked about the dull things a man talks about at the end of a day: a leaking cistern, a horse gone lame at Narbo, a priest who could not be made to finish a sacrifice before noon. He asked after Porcia.

“Is she as clever as her father?”

“Cleverer. Less patient.”

“Then you will do well, if she lets you.”

When Brutus rose at last, Caesar walked him to the inner door with a hand on his shoulder. It had the same weight it had had at Larissa after Pharsalus, when Brutus came in out of the mud expecting chains and was given dinner.

“People tell me things about you,” Caesar said at the door, lightly. He laid the flat of his hand on his own chest. “I tell them Brutus will wait for this poor body. He can afford to.”

Then he was back at his lists, and Brutus was in the corridor with his face doing something he had not told it to do.

Cassius was still by the brazier. He looked at Brutus once and unfolded his arms.

“Well?”

“He will tell you himself.”

“He has already told me,” Cassius said. “You came out smiling.” He went past Brutus toward the inner rooms without waiting for the slave. The men in the court watched him go and then watched Brutus, and Brutus put the smile away too late for any of them to miss it.

He walked home the long way, through the Forum, past the tribunal where in a few days he would sit as first praetor of Rome. The weight stayed on his shoulder the whole way. He tried to think of it as a debt, because a debt could be paid.

# SC-12: Forever

POV: Cicero · Tusculum / Senate fringe
Date / place: Early 44 · doorway / letter desk orbit
Canon refs: A3 · C7 · B3
Info ENTER: Accommodation habit · EXIT: Disgust + compliance; hears East packing as clock
Overhear risk: Elite medium
**G1:** Primary POV

---

In a Tusculum doorway that smelled of wet stone and other men’s wine, *dictator perpetuo* landed like a joke told by men with swords.

Cicero heard it and felt his face arrange itself into the old accommodation smile before his mind finished objecting. The mind objected in history, because that was where it lived. Six months: that was a dictator, once. A man the Republic hired in an emergency, handed every power, and thanked when he had put the fire out and gone home. Sulla had broken the habit. Caesar had stretched it, a year here, ten years there, and now some obliging senator had drafted the last clause: not for the crisis, but for good. The couriers had carried it up to Tusculum’s summer villas with the morning bread.

A colleague clapped as if forever were a festival. Another muttered *king* and then swallowed the word with wine. Somewhere behind them a clerk repeated the title carefully, practising the new mouth-shape of power.

“Say it again,” the clapper urged the clerk, cheerful. “If we practise forever, it becomes manners.”

“Manners,” the wine-mutterer said, “are what we call fear when it has learned Latin.”

“You are very brave in a doorway.”

“I am very brave in a doorway with a wall behind me.”

“He has the wall and Cicero has the doorway,” said the clapper, “and between them they have a Republic.”

Nobody laughed at that, which was the first honest thing anyone had said all morning.

Cicero’s smile held. Holding was his craft. He had spent the last three years learning what the best speaker in Rome was worth when the only audience that mattered had stopped needing speeches. Caesar had pardoned him with a courtesy that left no mark and no way to answer it. In private he dissected: *forever* was what kings borrowed from temples, not a republican word. In public he complied, spoke carefully, saved his knives for wax and for Atticus, who had been reading the sentences Cicero could not say aloud for thirty years. He drafted a sentence about the city’s appetite for extraordinary medicine, then softened it until it could travel without getting him killed by courtesy.

He imagined Atticus reading the softened sentence and understanding the blade underneath. Friendship as cipher: the only legion he trusted. Formal office without steel still let him shape periods. Periods did not shape legions. He knew the difference. He hated knowing it in a doorway while lesser men clapped.

The clerk tried the title once more, almost proud. The wine-mutterer asked whether *perpetuo* meant Parthia would wait forever or whether forever meant Parthia had to start now. Someone laughed as if logistics were wit. Cicero set the laugh beside the title. Forever, treated as a departure date, was still a clock.

He drafted a letter that recorded his disgust in a period Atticus would admire, then sanded the tablet and wrote the congratulatory note the hour actually required. When both were sealed he touched the disgusted draft once, still warm, and left it unsent in the drawer.

A courier from Hirtius’s orbit arrived as the seals cooled. Cicero had once fed Hirtius at his own table and corrected his speeches over the fish; Caesar had since promised his officer a consulship. Cicero liked to think the one had helped with the other. The courier carried nothing urgent enough to be honest, only a question whether Cicero had “heard the eastern packing talk” and whether forever made the East safer or hungrier. Cicero gave the courier a softened sentence and no body, accommodation still arranged on his mouth. Softened sentences kept men without armies invited to rooms where armies were discussed.

“Tell Hirtius,” said Cicero, “that I am delighted by both.”

“Both?”

“Forever and Parthia. He will know which is which.”

When the courier’s sandals had left the atrium stone, Cicero’s smile dropped for Tiro alone. Tiro’s stylus was already moving in the hooked shorthand he had invented to keep up with that mouth. He had been freed years ago. He had never stopped. “They clap forever into manners and ask me to sand the blade out of the letter. When Parthia packs, those same mouths will call the packing ordinary weather and ask for periods again.”

Tiro did not laugh. He sanded. That was how you survived a master’s private complaint without becoming its audience in the street.

“You are not going to ask what I think?” Cicero said, after a while.

“I have it already, master. It is in the drawer.”

Cicero looked at him a moment longer than the joke deserved.

# SC-14: Closed Market

POV: F4 Hordeonius · offices futures · recruitment fuel · FS22 overheard
Date / place: Banker stalls · early 44
Canon refs: A3 aftermath · grievance seed · FS22
Info ENTER: Bribe season memory · EXIT: Multi-year designations = grievance; Parthia clock priced
Overhear risk: Medium
**G1:** Second POV: collides with Cicero’s unfinished question (forever → dead calendar / East packing as market weather)
**G2:** No extra camera for the crates; former SC-13 Parthia crates folded into stall talk (FS22 held without F1 cut)

---

“They have sold years in advance,” Hordeonius said, scandalised on behalf of ready coin. “Consulships sold forward, like olives still on the tree. A Republic should keep some offices for the living to bid on. Otherwise we are only clerks to a dead calendar.”

“Two a year,” a young senator’s agent muttered. “My master’s father stood for it three times and bought half the Velabrum doing it. Now he names them two years ahead because he will be in Parthia at election time, and we are to cheer the list.” He held up a tablet with the names already on it, passed hand to hand round the Forum like a price list for a game nobody had been invited to play. “It is not fair.”

Hordeonius heard politics and priced it. Sosia sniffed. Fairness smelled wrong to him.

“Fairness,” said Sosia, “is what men call it when they are not on the list.”

“And the list is very short,” said the agent.

“The list is always short. That is why it is a list.”

“Late,” Hordeonius said, already reaching for the next tablet. “You want seats near the auction after the years are sold. I charge for that. Not for the sermon.”

A client repeated *dead calendar* with the pleasure of a man borrowing a blade. Another asked whether years sold forward meant bribes should be paid early. Hordeonius entered a number and waved the man on. Stopping other men’s metaphors was unpaid labour. Charging for them was craft. Listeners still left carrying a grievance into darker rooms. That was their business.

“And the East?” the young agent pressed. “I hear the Domus is packing crates like a clock. Maps, boots, grain for ships, months away treated as a neighbour’s farm. If forever has a title, does Parthia get a departure date?”

Sosia grunted. “Crates don’t vote. Men with unpaid offices do.”

Hordeonius entered *Parthia: packing noise* beside *offices: closed forward*. He had not seen the Domus service rooms. He did not need to. Packing talk reached stalls faster than straps found themselves. A house aiming at a horizon with a name was a rate change in laundry’s clothes.

He felt the market’s mood thicken. Thick markets made people invent virtues, and virtues priced wrongly became recruitment talk before anyone admitted the word.

The young agent folded his tablet of names into his sleeve and went off toward the baths, where men who were not on the list had begun to meet in the side rooms. Hordeonius marked him for interest, not for liberty.
