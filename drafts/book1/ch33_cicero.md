# SC-86: The Voice Ends

POV: Cicero · Formiae coast road
Date / place: 7 Dec 43 · litter / coast road
Canon refs: A13 · Chain 6 closes · C7
Info ENTER: Flight from proscription · EXIT: Murder, oratory loses to arms
Overhear risk: Killers / attendants

---

Cicero had always believed a sentence could outrun a sword if the sentence were good enough.

The litter swayed on the Formiae road. Sea air lifted the curtain edges and brought salt that tasted like unfinished letters. Atticus unanswered. Philippics still warm in memory like a fever that thought it was an army. He had left too late and too early. Late for safety. Early for the dignity of a man who still thought negotiation might grow legs if fed enough clauses.

He had already tried the sea. A hired ship out of Astura, Brutus somewhere beyond the far coast with legions and a welcome, and the swell had turned his stomach and then his nerve. He had made them put in at Caieta. He had climbed to the villa above Formiae with salt in his beard and told his slaves, *Let me die in the country I have so often saved.* By dinner the sentence embarrassed him; it had come too easily. At first light the slaves had not asked his opinion. They had lifted him into the litter and turned it back toward the water.

Proscription had turned his name into a price. A freedman had brought the copy to Tusculum: his own name, and his brother Quintus’s, and the nephew’s, under an edict sealed by three men. One of the three was the boy. He tried to treat the price as a rhetorical problem and failed. Rhetoric needed an audience; hunters weren’t one. He catalogued, helplessly, the old skills: period, antithesis, the pause that made juries lean. None of them lengthened a road.

Atticus lived in his head as audience. So did the Capitol’s first day after the knives, enlarged by memory into courage. So did the boy he had aimed at Antony and called statecraft. The boy had learned. Antony had remembered. Brutus’s polite exile at Lanuvium felt, suddenly, like a kinder country. A cage with a view.

He remembered telling a friend that the Republic still had a voice. The friend had said the Republic had Cicero’s voice. The distinction, once clever, now walked beside the litter like a creditor. He had waved caution away with a jury gesture once. The gesture had not grown a cohort.

A boy selling fish by the verge stared at the litter. Fame might have been a colour on it. Cicero almost shaped a greeting and then didn’t. Greeting belonged to mornings when names weren’t prices. He counted the poles’ rhythm and tried to turn it into a period. The period refused to close.

“Faster,” he told the bearers, then hated the undignified word. Speed wasn’t eloquence. Speed was what animals did when the forest closed.

When the killers found the road, his attendants made the noises attendants make. Panic first. Then the reckoning of their own skins. Someone begged. Someone ran. Someone stayed because staying was also a calculation, and calculations had always been Cicero’s neighbours if not his friends. He put his head out enough to be seen.

The officer at the front had a face he knew. It took a moment; names had begun to take him moments. Popillius. A young man once, charged with his father’s death, and Cicero had talked a jury out of sewing him into the parricide’s sack and giving him to the river. The jury had leaned. Popillius had kissed his hand in the street. Now he held a drawn sword and would not look at the litter, and a centurion beside him did the looking for both.

Cicero looked at them the way he had looked at juries: measuring, already composing, already knowing the verdict lived outside the speech.

He didn’t deliver a final Philippic. Rhetoric needed breath and an hour. He had seconds and a neck. He offered them the stillness he had practised in courts when the verdict was already known. He almost shaped a line about the Republic. He let it die unspoken.

He thought, absurdly, of a softened sentence he had once drafted for Atticus about extraordinary medicine. Medicine had become the list. The list had become this road.

The blow came. Pain was brief and total and insultingly ordinary: no period, no antithesis, no applause. Rome’s most expensive voice became silence with blood in it.

The sea kept sounding like itself. That offended him more than the blades. The surf didn’t pause for a verdict.

His last clear thought was the petty wish that Atticus would understand the silence without needing a courier to explain it. And that the boy wouldn’t mistake silence for permission.

# SC-86b: Brothers

POV: F3 Laberius · the Sacred Way
Date / place: 29 Dec 43 · Plancus’s triumph, Rome
Canon refs: T11 · Velleius 2.67 · Val. Max. 6.8.5 · App. *BC* 4.12, 4.37
Info ENTER: Lists working · EXIT: Vibius goes hunting; Laberius stays
Overhear risk: The crowd

---

Plancus triumphed over the Gauls in a cold that made the trumpets crack.

Laberius stood on the Sacred Way with Vibius and two men from the old tent, shoulder to shoulder with bakers and clerks and men in good cloaks who did not look at anybody. They were the boy’s soldiers now, the four of them, quartered outside the walls with the rest of his legions, and nobody had asked them to march. It was not their triumph. It was Plancus’s, and in two days it would be Lepidus’s, and the soldiers of both men had been drinking since before light.

The carts went by with the spoils: shields, a gilt boar, tablets with the names of tribes Laberius had never heard of painted in red. Then the captives, not many. Then the lictors, their rods wound with laurel. Then Plancus in the chariot with his face painted red like Jupiter’s, his eyes fixed on the Capitol, a slave behind him holding the gold crown over his head and saying the thing slaves said into a general’s ear on his triumph. Laberius had always wondered whether they really said it, or only moved their lips.

Behind the chariot came the legions, and the legions were singing.

They sang the old songs first, the filthy ones about the general’s debts and the general’s women, because that was the custom and the general had to smile. Then, somewhere in the third cohort, a new verse started, and it went up the column faster than a fire up a thatch, and by the time the eagle came past Laberius the whole legion had it.

*De germanis, non de Gallis, duo triumphant consules.*

The man beside Laberius, a clerk by his ink, snorted and then looked round to see whether anybody had heard him snort.

“What is it?” said one of the tent men. His Latin was for orders and prices.

“Germani,” Vibius said. “Germans. Or brothers. Same word. ‘Not over the Gauls but over their brothers, the two consuls triumph.’” He did not laugh. He was counting the column. “Plancus put his brother on the list. Lepidus put his. That’s the joke.”

Laberius knew about Lepidus’s brother. Paullus had got away; the camp said he had reached the coast and Brutus’s people, and that Lepidus’s soldiers had let him go because they could not face their general across the dinner table if they brought his brother’s head to it. Plancus’s brother had not got away. The story had come up from Salernum in the mouths of carters. Plotius had hidden on his estate there, and the soldiers had come and found nothing, and taken his slaves and put them to the question, and the slaves had said nothing at all, for days, while it went on. Then the soldiers smelled perfume. Plotius had always worn too much of it, a scent from the East that cost more than a slave. They followed it to the cupboard. Some said he had come out of his own accord, to stop what was being done to his people. Some said the smell came out first. Either way he was dead, and his brother was in a red-painted chariot going up to thank Jupiter.

The legion sang it again. *De germanis, non de Gallis.* Plancus, in the chariot, kept his eyes on the Capitol.

“His own men,” said the clerk, quietly, to nobody.

“That’s what a triumph’s for,” Vibius said. “One day a year you can say it to his face.” He spat to the side. “Tomorrow you can’t.”

When the eagle had passed and the crowd began to break up behind the last cohort, Vibius took Laberius by the elbow and pulled him into the mouth of an alley that smelled of piss and cabbage.

“There’s a man in the tombs on the Appian Way,” he said. “A praetorian. Was a praetor, anyway. A carter saw a woman take bread out there three nights running. Five of us are going tonight. Old Mutina hands.”

Laberius looked at him.

“Twenty-five thousand,” Vibius said. “Split five ways, that’s still five thousand each. That’s Capua. That’s Capua twice. That’s a plough and an ox and a roof, and you never have to stand in another line in front of another clerk with a thumb on your name.”

“And the woman with the bread?”

“She’s not on the list.”

“The slaves at Salernum weren’t on the list,” Laberius said.

Vibius let go of his elbow. “Don’t make it philosophy. You hate philosophy.”

“I do.” Laberius looked down at his boots, the same boots, resoled twice since Mutina. “I fight for the man on my left. I don’t dig him out of a tomb for a clerk to count.”

“He isn’t on your left. He’s a praetor. He’d have voted your land to his cousins.”

“Probably.”

“Then come.”

Laberius thought about it honestly, because Vibius deserved honesty. Five thousand denarii. He thought of the twine and the dirt near Capua that was always a matter for after. He thought about digging in the cold among the dead, and the woman’s bread, and the smell of a frightened man in a tomb. He thought about Plancus’s face, painted red, not moving while his own men sang.

“No,” he said.

Vibius looked at him for a long moment. Then he shrugged the shrug they had both learned in Spain, the one that meant the other man was a fool and would probably live longer for it.

“Win,” Vibius said. “Then count.”

“You count,” said Laberius. “I’ll be at the brazier.”

Vibius went. Laberius walked back out through the gate alone, in the cold, past the end of the procession where the soldiers were still singing, and found that the tune was stuck in his head. It was a good tune. He hummed it all the way to the camp, without the words.

# SC-86c: The Daughter of Hortensius

POV: F1 Nysa · Antony’s door, then the Forum
Date / place: Early 42 · the Carinae; the triumvirs’ tribunal in the Forum
Canon refs: T11 · App. *BC* 4.32–34, 4.37 · Ch 25 count
Info ENTER: The women’s tax · EXIT: The list cut; Nysa gives a name
Overhear risk: The whole Forum

---

The women came to the Carinae in the middle of the morning, more of them than the vestibule could hold, and Nysa saw them first because she was on her knees scrubbing the threshold.

They were not the kind of women who walked. They came in litters and got out of them at the corner, as if walking the last stretch were part of what they had come to say. Old women and young, matrons in good wool with their hair dressed high, a few with a slave girl each and most without. The doorkeeper looked at them the way a man looks at weather coming over a hill.

There was an edict. The whole city knew it, and the Carinae kitchen had known it a day sooner. Fourteen hundred of the richest women in Rome to declare their property, and pay what the triumvirs assessed for the war against the men in the East. Any who hid anything to be fined. Informers rewarded. The kitchen had laughed at that last part, because by now informers were rewarded for everything.

A woman at the front spoke to the doorkeeper. She was perhaps forty, tall, plain, with a voice that carried without being raised.

“We have been to Octavia,” she said. “The triumvir’s sister received us. We have been to Julia, the triumvir’s mother. She received us. We ask to see Fulvia.”

The doorkeeper went in. Nysa went on scrubbing the same stone.

Fulvia came out herself. She stood in the vestibule in a house dress with a stylus still in her hand, and she looked at the women for about as long as it took to count them.

“You have come to the wrong door,” she said. “I don’t make the laws in this house.” And she turned and went back in, and the doorkeeper shut the door on fourteen hundred women, or on the forty of them who had come, and Nysa was on the inside of it with her brush.

Fulvia’s woman found her in the passage a little later, the one who counted the silver, the one who had asked Nysa for the true number at the games.

“They’re going to the Forum,” she said. “To the tribunal. All three are sitting this morning. Go and count them. Not for the steward. For me.”

The women had gone ahead. Nysa caught them in the Argiletum, and by the time they reached the Forum there were more of them, and more people following to see what the women would do, and the crowd had that quality Nysa had learned to read on the morning of the Ides: it did not know yet what it was for.

The triumvirs sat on their chairs on the tribunal in a row. Lepidus in the middle, because he was consul. Antony on one side, big and bored. On the other side the boy, who was not a boy any more, in a toga with a purple border, his face still and pale.

Nysa had seen him once since he came back to Rome, at a distance, in the street with the lictors in front of him. He was her patron. In law, the old man’s freedwoman belonged to the old man’s son. She owed him her duty and her vote, if she had had a vote, and her presence when he called for it, which he never had. She did not think he knew her name. She stood at the edge of the crowd and counted.

The lictors stood across the front of the tribunal with their rods. The tall woman walked straight up to them, as if they were not there, and the lictors looked at each other, and somehow she was through, and the others behind her.

Somebody near Nysa said, “That’s Hortensia. The orator’s girl. Hortensius.”

“Who?”

“Her father argued against Cicero in the courts for thirty years. Won half the time.”

Hortensia did not shout. She spoke the way her father must have spoken, Nysa thought, the voice placed and let go so that it reached the back of the Forum without seeming to try.

“You have already taken our fathers,” she said, “our sons, our husbands and our brothers, on the charge that they wronged you. If you take our property too, you will bring us down to a condition unworthy of our birth and our ways and our sex.”

The Forum went quiet in the way it had not been quiet since the funeral.

“If we have done you wrong, as you say our husbands did, proscribe us as you proscribed them. But if we women have voted none of you an enemy, nor pulled down your houses, nor destroyed your army, nor led another against you, nor kept you from office or honour, why should we share the penalty when we did not share the crime?”

Antony shifted in his chair. Lepidus looked at his hands. The boy did not move at all.

“Why should we pay taxes,” said Hortensia, “when we have no part in the honours, the commands, the statecraft, for which you fight each other with such harm to us all?”

A man near Nysa laughed, and then stopped laughing because nobody else had.

Hortensia went on. She spoke of the women of the old wars who had given their gold freely when Hannibal was at the gates, and how they had given it of their own will, not from their land, not under assessment by informers. Nysa stopped listening to the words after a while and listened to the voice instead. She had heard Antony speak over Caesar’s body and heard the crowd turn under it like a ship. This was not that. This voice did not turn anybody. It simply stood in the middle of the Forum and would not get out of the way.

*No part in the honours.* Nysa had no honours and no property, and no husband or son to lose. She owned two tunics, a comb, a cracked oil flask she had stolen back from a steward, and her duty to a pale man on a chair who did not know her name. If there was a list below the bottom of all the lists, she was on it. She found that she did not mind as much as she had expected. Nobody was going to tax her.

The triumvirs were angry. Nysa saw it go along the row like a lamp passed from hand to hand. Antony said something to the lictors, and the lictors stepped forward with their rods to drive the women off the tribunal.

The crowd made a noise. Nysa had never heard it make that noise before. It was not a roar. It was lower and uglier, the sound of a lot of men who had lost brothers and fathers in the last six weeks discovering that they all minded the same thing at once. Somewhere behind her someone shouted *Shame!* and someone else took it up, and the lictors stopped with their rods half raised and looked back at the tribunal.

Lepidus stood up and said that the matter would be considered tomorrow.

The women left the Forum the way they had come, walking. The crowd let them through.

Nysa had counted forty-one at the Carinae door, and three hundred and something by the time they reached the tribunal, though the count had grown too fast to be sure past the first hundred. She held the numbers ready, the way she had held the count at the games.

At the corner of the Argiletum a hand caught her sleeve. Medullina, from the Subura, older and thinner, with chalk on her fingers.

“You were in it,” Medullina said. “You came from that way. From his house. Who was she? The tall one. Give me her name.”

For years Medullina had wanted names from her, senators’ names, the names of men in rooms, and for years Nysa had given her chickens. This month the walls were full of names and Medullina sold them by the spelling.

“Hortensia,” Nysa said. “The daughter of Hortensius.”

Medullina stared at her, waiting for the price.

“It’s free,” said Nysa. “Everyone in the Forum is saying it.” And she pulled her sleeve away and went back up the hill.

The next day it was given out that the list of women was cut from fourteen hundred to four hundred, and that men with more than a hundred thousand would pay instead. The kitchen talked about nothing else. The cook said it was the first time in living memory the three of them had taken a step back. The steward said it was arithmetic. The boy who could read said Lucius Caesar was off the lists too, and had been since before the Saturnalia, after his sister stood in front of this same tribunal and said she had hidden him and would go with him if he went. Nysa had heard the beginning of that, at a door, with a jug in her hand.

She gave Fulvia’s woman the true number, as near as she could make it. Fulvia’s woman wrote it down and did not thank her. Nysa found she had not wanted thanks.
