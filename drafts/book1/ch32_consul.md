# SC-83: Consul

That summer, in his camp in the north and then on the road to Rome, Octavian entered the politics of force with hands that still remembered shaking in Apollonia.

A courier brought the Senate’s thanks for Mutina, and the thanks were not for him. Decimus had the triumph and the dead consuls’ legions, the Martians among them. Octavian was to be thanked and left behind. He read the decree once and gave it to Agrippa, who read it twice.

Segulius Labeo came to dinner in the camp that month, because Labeo came to dinner everywhere, and told the saying from Cicero’s table to Octavian’s face, and Octavian gave him the answer Labeo would carry north to Decimus. Afterwards he sat on alone with the three verbs going round in his head in their order, like coins being counted, and found that he could not make the last one come out as only a joke.

“They will say you let Antony get away,” Agrippa said.

Octavian’s throat tightened once, shame or fever; he didn’t sort it. He looked at the pay chests, not at Agrippa’s face. “Then we pay before we argue. Men who are owed invent worse stories than senators do.”

He had the clerks open the nearest chest where Agrippa could see the count. When a tribune started a sentence about the Senate’s honours, Octavian cut him with a nod toward the queue of soldiers. “Honours after the line is fed.”

A centurion from the Martians said it first, loud enough for the pay line: “They want us under the man who held Caesar’s door. Who feeds us now, old men with tablets, or Caesar’s name?” A tribune seconded without looking at Octavian’s face: the men would not march for an assassin on praise that arrived late and sweet. Agrippa translated the noise into one sentence Octavian could keep. The ranks wanted a consul who was *theirs*: paymaster, name, a face young enough to insult the old seats. Two chairs were empty.

Philippus wrote from Rome in the stepfather’s hand Octavian had learned to read sideways, every warning folded inside a piece of news. The House was frightened and could not say of what. Decimus was a Liberator with a triumph and a thinning army, not a consul. Antony was over the Alps with Lepidus’s legions. Some of the fathers wanted a consul who obeyed and would settle for one who was young. And then, as if he were only reminding a boy of his lessons: the law wanted forty-two years for the chair. *You have nineteen. Count them before anyone else does.*

Octavian counted them. Then he sent the centurions.

There were four hundred of them in the end, centurions and picked men from every legion, because the ranks would not trust a smaller number to come back with the truth. They went down to Rome in July with the soldiers’ asks in writing: the money the Senate had voted them and not paid, and a consulship for the man who paid. Octavian did not go. Agrippa did not go. Octavian walked the camp in the days they were away and was polite to everyone and slept badly, and on the eighth night he was sick behind the horse lines and told no one.

They came back angry. The fathers had kept them standing in the porch, and then had them in, and then talked to them as if they were a deputation of farmers about a road. A senator had asked whose idea the consulship was. Another had said, kindly, that the law wanted forty-two years.

A centurion called Cornelius told it at the pay line. He was a broad, slow man with an old burn down one forearm, and he told it the way he must have done it, without hurry. He took his cloak by the shoulder and threw it back, so the men could see the sword at his hip.

“And I said to them, in the House, *If you will not make him consul, this will.*”

The pay line roared. Octavian, at the chest, kept his face still and his fingers on the lid. He had not told Cornelius to say it. He had not told him not to.

“Well,” said Agrippa, under the noise. “That’s the letter answered.”

They crossed the little river at the border of Italy late in July, with the eight legions behind them. Octavian rode ahead with the picked cohorts. The water was low, the colour of weak wine, and the horses did not need to swim. He had expected to feel something at it, because it was the same river, and men in the column were already saying so to each other, *his father’s river*, as if it had been in the will with the gardens. He felt his horse’s shoulder shift under him on the stones. He felt the sun on the back of his neck. On the far side he reined in and let the cohorts go past, and watched the faces, and found that the men felt it for him, and that this was more use.

The Senate sent envoys to meet him on the road with money. Two days later it sent riders after the envoys to take the money back, because two legions had landed from Africa and the fathers had found their courage again. Octavian heard both pieces of news in one evening, and laughed for the first time in a month, and could not stop until Agrippa put a cup in his hand.

“They’re counting legions,” Agrippa said. “It’s progress.”

The two African legions went over to him outside the walls. The urban praetor, Cornutus, who had been given the defence of the city with them, fell on his sword when he heard. Octavian was told this at a well on the Via Flaminia, and stood for a while with the dipper in his hand. He had not known Cornutus. He found he wanted to know what the man had looked like, and did not ask.

His own cavalry had gone into Rome ahead of him with orders to touch nothing and tell everyone. By the time he came in through the gate there were people on the roofs. Some of them cheered. Most watched, the way people watch weather. A woman at a fountain held her child against her skirts and did not look up at all, and Octavian, who had been composing his face for the cheering, composed it again for her.

His mother and his sister were in the house of the Vestals.

Philippus had sent them there in July, when the centurions went down, because the Vestals’ house was the one door in Rome no frightened man would kick in. The Virgins had kept the will there. They kept the flame. And for six weeks they had kept Atia and Octavia, among the girls in white, in two rooms off a courtyard that smelled of laurel smoke.

They came out to him at the steps. Octavia came first, quickly, with her skirts in her fist, and put her arms round his neck before he had finished dismounting, and he stood with one foot in the stirrup and his sister hanging on him in front of half the Forum and found he did not mind. She was twenty-six and married to a man who had been consul, and she smelled of the laurel smoke too. She said his name. His old name. Nobody had said it in a year.

Atia came after, slowly. She was thinner. Her hands were empty.

“I kept the box,” she said, when Octavia had let go of him. She meant the jewels, the ones she had set on Philippus’s table the night he sold the Velitrae land, when he had told her *not yet*. “They’re in my room. I thought,” she said, and stopped, and looked past him at the soldiers in the Forum, at the cohorts drawn up on the Sacred Way with their shields uncovered, “I thought you might need them now.”

“Not now either,” he said.

She looked at him a long moment. Then her face went blank, as it had gone blank in Philippus’s villa when he said the name aloud, as if she had expected nothing else and wished she had been wrong.

“You look ill,” she said.

“I’ve been riding.”

“You’ve been ill.” She put the back of her hand to his cheek, as she had when he was eight, and he let her, in front of the soldiers. “Eat something,” Atia said. “Before you do anything else today. Before you do anything you can’t take back.”

He ate. He ate standing up, bread and cheese from a soldier’s pack, on the steps of the Vestals’ house with his mother watching, and it was the first food he had kept down in three days.

While he ate, Octavia gave him the household news, the way she had put it in letters he had not had time to read. Calpurnia had gone to her father’s. The old house’s freedmen were scattered: one keeping a wine shop by the Circus, two gone to Lepidus with the Domus, one girl in Antony’s kitchen on the Carinae.

“She counted heads at your games for his steward,” Octavia said. “Matius heard it. Somebody laughed at the number at Antony’s dinner.”

Octavian swallowed. He remembered the cheap rows standing. “What’s her name?”

Octavia did not know. Octavian looked past her at Agrippa, who nodded once and wrote nothing down, which meant he would remember.

The senators came to meet him in the afternoon. They came in twos and threes, in their bordered togas, and made speeches on the steps about the Republic and the young Caesar’s piety, and Octavian thanked each of them by name, because he had learned all their names in the winter from Cicero’s letters and Agrippa’s lists. Some of them had voted a week before to send the African legions out against him. They did not mention it. He did not mention it either.

Cicero came last.

He came late in the day with Tiro behind him, and climbed the steps a little slowly, and made a speech that was better than the others and shorter, and took Octavian’s hand in both of his. He smelled of ink and of the Palatine. Octavian looked at him and heard, as clearly as if Labeo were at his elbow, three verbs in their order.

“You are the last of my friends to greet me,” he said.

He said it lightly, the way the old man liked things said. Cicero laughed. Tiro did not. And Octavian, who had meant it as a pleasantry when he began the sentence, found by the end of it that he had meant something else, and could not have said exactly what, and let it stand.

That night a rumour went round that two of his legions had gone back to the Senate. The fathers met by lamplight in the Curia, and Cicero stood at the door to greet them as they came in, and for an hour the old Republic was alive again in one room. Then the rumour was found to be false. Cicero went home in a closed litter, and did not come to the Field of Mars again.

Octavian heard about it in the morning, from Agrippa, over the pay tables.

“He stood at the door,” Agrippa said.

“Of course he did.” Octavian signed a tablet. “He always does.”

The treasury was in the Temple of Saturn, under the Capitol, and the keys came to him in a dish.

He did not touch them. He sent a tribune with a guard and a clerk with tablets, and they brought the chests down the temple steps to the Forum in daylight, where the whole city could watch them, and set them out on trestles in the square. Twenty-five hundred a man: the first payment of what the Senate had voted and not paid. The rest promised. The clerks called the names by century, and the men came up in their order, and took their coin, and signed, or made their mark, and went away. It took two days. Octavian sat behind the tables for most of both, and when a tribune leaned in on the first afternoon to say the fathers had sent to ask when the election would be held, he did not look up.

“Feed the line,” he said. “Then the honours.”

One man in the line he remembered afterwards. A centurion in old boots, newly stitched at the left heel, came up with his century on the second afternoon, took his coin, made his mark, and then did not move on. He asked the clerk whether the roll still had his plot on it, by Capua, fourth line from the Volturnus road. The clerk found it with his thumb and said that the stones were a matter for after. The centurion nodded as if he had expected nothing else and went away with the coin in his fist. *Laberius*, the clerk had written. Octavian had the line copied on to a separate tablet and put it with the list of his father’s freedmen that Agrippa was making, and could not have said why.

The line was fed on the second evening. The election was on the nineteenth.

They held it on the Field of Mars, where the voting pens were, with the legions camped round it close enough to hear. Octavian had wanted Pedius for his colleague, and Pedius was willing. Pedius was his cousin, another grandson of Caesar’s sister, a share of the last quarter of the will to Octavian’s three, an elderly man who had fought in Spain for his uncle and wanted, now, only to be left a quiet old age. He stood next to Octavian in the white toga of a candidate and coughed into his fist. The centuries came up in their order and voted, and the heralds called them, and the count was never in doubt, and everyone knew it, and still they counted, because counting was what made it a law.

The augur had taken the auspices at dawn. Octavian had stood beside him on the hill in the cold, and watched him mark out the sky with his staff, and they had waited. Birds came over from the river. Vultures, the augur said. Six of them. They went over high and slow and away toward the Janiculum.

Later, while Octavian was speaking to the soldiers, more came over. The men in the front ranks saw them and pointed, and a noise went back through the legions, and by the time Octavian had finished speaking someone was saying twelve, and someone else was saying that twelve had come to Romulus when he founded the city, and by evening the whole camp was saying it.

Agrippa had counted. He said six, and then six. He said it was a hot summer and the river at the bottom of the Field had dead things in it.

“Which is it?” Octavian asked.

“Both, I should think.” Agrippa looked at the sky, which was empty now. “That’s the art.”

Octavian looked at the empty sky a moment longer than he meant to. Six birds belonged to the augur. Twelve belonged to the camp, and the camp had made him a second Romulus before the centuries had finished making him a consul. He would not correct them.

He felt sick after the count, stomach, not omen. A taste of metal rose; he swallowed it and kept his fingers on the chest lid until the shake stopped. Twelve lictors shouldered the bound rods and walked ahead of him, and men stepped out of the street for the rods before they looked to see whose face came behind. A herald stumbled over *consul* and *nineteen* in the same breath, as if the two words had never met. Octavian let him find his way to the end.

There was one smaller vote first, which Antony had kept off the agenda through the whole of his consulship. The thirty lictors who stood for the city’s old wards were called to the Capitol and passed the adoption into law. It took less time than the auspices had. Octavian had been signing himself Caesar since the May before; now the clerks could write it without a note in the margin.

Pedius had his first law ready before the lictors had learned the way to the new consul’s door: a court for the men of the Ides. It sat in a day. Nobody spoke for the absent, because nobody was fool enough to. Octavian read the list of the condemned at supper, and Decimus’s name was on it, and Casca’s, and Brutus’s and Cassius’s at the top, and he put the tablet face down beside his plate and ate what was on the plate, because his mother had told him to.

# SC-82c: The Bridge

POV: Decimus · the Isara; north Italian roads
Date / place: Late Aug → Sept 43 · Cularo; the bridge over the Isara; the roads east; a ford
Canon refs: A10 end · App. *BC* 3.97–98 · death path fog OK
Info ENTER: The boy is consul; the Pedian court has sat · EXIT: Plancus crosses to Antony; the recruits walk off; death at the ford
Overhear risk: Dwindling staff

At Cularo, in the last days of August, a rider came up from the south with the news from Rome, and Plancus read it first, because Plancus’s riders were faster.

He came to Decimus’s tent with the tablet in his hand and his face arranged. Decimus had seen that arrangement on a hundred faces in Caesar’s atrium, the face of a man bringing news he had already decided how to feel about.

“The boy is consul,” Plancus said.

Decimus took the tablet. The boy was nineteen, elected with his legions camped on the Field of Mars, and his colleague was a cousin, Pedius, an old man who would do what he was told. Their first law set up a court to try the murderers of Caesar. It had sat already and condemned them in a day, in their absence, every man of the Ides, with no one to speak for them.

His own name was there. He read it twice.

“Interdicted from fire and water,” Plancus said gently. “It won’t mean anything up here, of course.”

“Laws are what we told ourselves we were for.”

“We told ourselves a great many things.” Plancus took the tablet back, and Decimus let him. “I am still your colleague, Decimus. Nothing in Rome changes the valley.”

Nothing in Rome changed the valley. The valley changed on its own. After the news the recruits began talking in the evenings in a way they had not talked before. The veterans in Plancus’s lines stopped looking at Decimus with interest. And letters began coming for Plancus from the west, from Pollio in Spain, who had three legions and a habit of writing to everyone and committing to no one, under seals Decimus was not shown.

He did not ask to be shown them for three days. On the fourth he asked at dinner, because he could not bear the cup and the laugh one more evening without it.

“Pollio,” he said. “What does he want?”

Plancus did not pretend not to understand. That was something. He set down his cup and looked at it.

“He wants what everyone wants,” he said. “He wants to be on the side that is still standing in the spring. He thinks there will be peace between Antony and the boy. He thinks the men who were in the Curia that morning are the price of it.” He looked up. “I have not answered him.”

“You haven’t answered me either.”

“No,” said Plancus. “I haven’t.”

They finished dinner. Plancus was charming for the rest of it, and they walked out of the tent together into the warm dark and stood for a moment looking at the river.

“If I go,” Plancus said, quietly, as if to the river, “I will send you word first. I owe you that.”

“You owe me your legions.”

“I owe the Republic my legions,” said Plancus. “I am trying to work out where it is.”

He did not send word first. Decimus woke one morning in September before light because his horse was restless, and went up on the bank of the camp ditch with his cloak over his shoulders, and saw that the higher ground by the river was moving. Plancus’s camp was being struck, not in haste, cohort by cohort, the eagle of the first legion already out in front of its column on the road to the bridge across the Isara.

Decimus stood and watched it. He did not wake his staff.

The columns crossed the bridge in the grey light, one after another, the old Gallic legions first, then the new ones, and went away south down the far bank toward Antony and Lepidus. It took most of the morning. At the end Plancus himself rode across, with his clean escort, and halfway over the bridge he reined in and turned in the saddle and looked back at the camp on the low ground. Decimus could not see his face at that distance. He could see that he raised a hand.

Decimus did not raise his.

A rider came up from the bridge an hour later with a letter, beautifully written, sealed with Plancus’s seal, saying that circumstances had compelled him and that he remained Decimus’s friend. Decimus read it and gave it to the older aide to file with the others. He gave orders for a count of rations. He went to his tent and sat down and found that his hands were steady, and was mildly surprised.

By noon the recruits had begun to go, not all at once and not with noise. A tent-group here, a century there, walking out of the gate with their shields on their backs as if they had been sent on fatigues, and not coming back. Some went south over the bridge after Plancus. Some went east toward the boy, whose name was on the law that had condemned their general. Some just went home. The centurions did not stop them. The centurions were going too.

Decimus watched from the bank of the ditch. He counted them as they went, from habit, and when the number stopped being a number he went to look at the maps.

That evening the camp was so quiet he could hear the river. He walked the lines alone with a lamp, past tents standing open with nobody in them, a pot still on a cold fire, a dice-board left in the middle of a game. At the end of the last row there was a horse-trough. He washed his hands in it, though there was nothing on them, and went back to the maps.

Then he rode with dwindling staff into country that did not care who freed the Republic. Rain made the road a trough. An aide counted purses and got a number that made him quiet. Another asked whether they should wait for Cassius from the East.

“If we wait here they will count us,” Decimus said. “We take the west fork before dark. The east ford is watched. I smelled horse-lines on the wind this morning.”

At a crossroads shrine someone had left a cheap wreath for “the liberators.” Rain had ruined it. Decimus didn’t stop to fix it. He checked his horse’s mouth for sores and found none.

They slept once in a barn that smelled of wet hay and someone else’s fear. In the dark Decimus thought, briefly, of Caesar’s atrium and the cheer he had worn on a March morning. He put the thought away the way he put away a broken strap: later, if later existed.

On the second day the staff was smaller. One man had “gone for water” and not returned. Decimus didn’t send a search.

Near a river ford the map promised a contact who could pass them toward the mountains and, beyond rumour, toward Cassius. The contact was three men with ordinary faces and a mule. One smiled too quickly.

“Cassius’s friends,” the aide said, low.

“Or anybody’s price,” Decimus said.

He still dismounted. Refusing the ford meant the open road behind, where Antony’s friends were thicker. He kept his hands visible, for appearance’s sake, even here.

The man who came down to the water to meet them was a Gallic chief called Camelus, whose people Decimus had once paid in Caesar’s name for remounts. Camelus asked for his name. He already knew it. Decimus gave it because lying would have bought a few breaths instead of miles.

They kept him a night in a hut above the ford. In the morning a rider came up from the south, from Antony, and a purse changed hands: not his purse. Someone else’s coin for his head. He counted the pieces as they went from one hand to the other. He could not help it.

There was no speech. He went for the horse. A hand caught his cloak. Mud took his knee at the ditch by the ford, shallow and ordinary: meltwater, a broken rein, the smell of wet wool. If he thought of Caesar’s laugh one last time, the thought stayed inside.

Camelus’s man struck once at the neck, and again.
