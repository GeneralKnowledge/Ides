# SC-16 — Diadem

POV: Antony · Lupercal / Forum → evening house · A4  
Info ENTER: Festival heat · EXIT: Diadem offered & refused; monarchy panic Public; *temporary* priced at home  
Overhear risk: Forum extreme; house medium

---

Antony liked the Lupercalia because it let a consul sweat in public without looking weak.

He ran, joked, slapped backs, played the old fertility chaos that Romans pretended was holy. The Lupercus strips stuck to his thighs; goat-smell and winter sweat made a single perfume. Caesar sat above it in the special chair—half god, half tired general—watching the city perform itself. Gold caught the February sun and threw it back into the crowd’s eyes until looking up hurt in a useful way.

When Antony came forward with the diadem—call it wreath, call it crown’s cousin, call it a test—the Forum’s noise changed pitch. Offer. The gesture hung. Wool and metal. A child’s cry somewhere under the press. Antony’s grin was wide enough to hide whether he had been told to offer or had offered to see what broke.

Caesar refused.

Not once only—refused in a way that let every watcher invent a script: humility, theatre, or a trap dressed as a joke. Antony lowered the thing as if lowering a cup he had not meant to empty. The Forum exhaled wrong—half relief, half hunger.

“He will not be king!” someone shouted, relieved.

“He already is!” someone else shouted, not relieved.

In the press Antony saw a familiar face go pale—Seius, one of the climbing men who bought applause and sold prudence. Seius’s mouth opened on festival noise and closed without a joke. Beside him a freedman was mouthing Parthia and crowns in the same breath, and a water-seller’s boy repeated *king* as if the word were a sweet. Antony noted the pallor the way he noted a soft cohort—useful, not urgent—and let the mouths blur back into Forum.

A client of the climbing sort pushed close enough to smell goat and ambition. “Brilliant theatre, consul,” the man said, smile wet with relief. “Rome will sleep easier for the refusal.”

Antony clasped him like a brother of the afternoon. “Temporary symbol. Don’t invent a constitution from a wreath.”

The client’s smile held until Antony’s shoulder turned. Then, to the freedman and anyone else still pricing fear: “Temporary. That’s how they always name the thing that stays. Mark me—he’ll wear it in Parthia and call it weather.”

Antony heard the word *temporary* travel behind his back already rewriting the afternoon, and laughed louder so the rewrite would have to wait.

Afterward he drank and said the symbol was temporary again, to men who wanted the word as a blanket. He could already hear what Fulvia would do with that word—price it, not correct it.

By evening the city’s elite rooms had a new oxygen: fear of *regnum*, whether or not a diadem had ever truly fit. Antony came home still smelling of goat and Forum heat. Fulvia did not meet him at the door with praise. She met him with a cup and a look that inventoried the laugh he had sent ahead by messenger.

“They say you called it temporary,” she said.

He grinned the public grin, smaller now. “It was a festival prop.”

She didn't smile. Temporary symbols were the ones that started wars. She didn't say so while the messenger still hovered with dust on his sandals—saying so early made you the woman who rusted a husband’s joke. She dismissed the man with courtesy that could have been gratitude, waited until the latch settled, then set the cup down harder than wine required.

“Temporary is a debt word,” she said. “Debts collect. Next time you invent a blanket for the Forum, invent one that doesn’t leave the house colder.”

Antony drank anyway. He stored her sentence where he stored unpaid cohorts: not denied, not paid tonight.

# SC-15 — Veiled Talk

POV: Cassius (Brutus in frame) · portico stroll · App. 2.113 energy  
Info ENTER: Separate private thoughts; diadem still sticky in the city · EXIT: Mutual disclosure begins; company plan for Brutus  
Overhear risk: Medium-High

---

They chose a colonnade off the Forum where shadows made faces honest and mouths careful. Goat-reek and diadem talk clung in the city’s teeth: the crowd’s half-cheer, Caesar’s refusal that somehow made kingship louder. A slave too near dusted a column that did not need dusting. A water-seller’s boy, same wrong age as the Forum’s sweet-sellers, loitered with ears that could turn politics into street candy. Cassius waited them both out of useful range before he spent a sentence.

He didn't begin with murder. He began with the difference between name and power, the only door a careful man would open without smelling steel.

“If he refuses the name and keeps the power,” Cassius said, almost idle, “what do we call the difference? Latin?”

Brutus walked half a step slower. Ancestors walked with him whether he invited them or not—imagined shelves, wax faces, the dull pressure of being someone’s correct grandson. Cassius matched the slower step without looking as if he were herding. Herding made Brutus invent philosophy as a fence.

“We call it the Republic enduring,” Brutus said.

“We call it a man packing for Parthia with the Senate in his purse.” Cassius glanced once—no lingering—at the emptied column, then at the boy now arguing a cup price with a muleteer. Ordinary noise was cover; he used it. “I have spoken to men who are tired of enduring. Galba will hear a winter price before he hears a hymn. Ligarius still owes a morning to his own pride. I am asking if you are one of them, or if you prefer to write essays about Cato while the rest of us choose a schedule.”

Brutus stopped. The stop itself was an answer he preferred to speech.

“I am not one of your mornings,” he said. “Not tonight. Not because a wreath made the Forum smell of panic. Panic is how gangs recruit.”

A cart rattled past on the street beyond the columns; someone cursed a mule as if the mule were a tribune. Cassius waited for the noise, then used it.

“Then give me the condition that would make you stop writing and start hearing,” Cassius said. “Or say plainly you will dine as a spared ornament until the East makes the question irrelevant. Clemency has already written you into the furniture. I am asking whether furniture is enough.”

Brutus’ jaw worked. Clemency sat under his tongue like a coin he had not chosen to swallow. The diadem’s sticky fear still hung between them—not as permission, as weather that made postponement look like virtue. “If—only if—anything were ever done, it would have to be law’s face, not a street. I have not agreed to a plan. I have named the only face I could live with. That is not permission.”

“Law’s face,” Cassius repeated, and did not smile. No daggers yet. Only a schedule beginning to grow underneath—men to speak to, rooms to choose, a friend of Caesar’s still unnamed who could open a door. He would bring company next time. Brutus was too good at turning two men into a philosophy lesson.

They walked on as if they had been talking about rain. In Rome, that was often the same conversation.

At the next corner Cassius left Brutus with festival courtesy—hand clasp, soft thanks for the stroll, a smile sized for anyone watching from a doorway. Ten paces on, the smile dropped. He found Trebonius loitering with a cup he was not drinking.

“Well?” Trebonius asked, mild as Monday.

“He refuses the street and has not accepted the surgery,” Cassius said. “He will need a room with more than my voice in it—or he will postpone until Parthia packs for him. I am tired of being his only weather.”

Trebonius’s mouth twitched—audible almost, a dry sound of recognition. “Bring Casca. Casca doesn’t do essays. He does doors.”

Cassius let the twitch count as the joke landing. “And if Brutus still burns invitations?”

“Then we hold Antony with business while better men invent their courage,” Trebonius said, and finally sipped—making the joke look like thirst. “Monday talk. Debt talk. The sort that makes a consul lean in. I am good at Monday.”

Cassius filed *Monday* beside *Casca* and *law’s face* and did not go back to Brutus that night. Going back would have turned resistance into a second chance to postpone. He walked toward Galba’s winter and Ligarius’s pride with the Forum’s *temporary* still sticky on the city’s tongue—and with Brutus’s refusal still warmer than any diadem.
