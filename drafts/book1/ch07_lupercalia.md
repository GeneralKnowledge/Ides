# SC-16 — Diadem

POV: Antony · Lupercal / Forum · A4  
Info ENTER: Festival heat · EXIT: Diadem offered & refused; monarchy panic Public

---

Antony liked the Lupercalia because it let a consul sweat in public without looking weak.

He ran, joked, slapped backs, played the old fertility chaos that Romans pretended was holy. The Lupercus strips stuck to his thighs; goat-smell and winter sweat made a single perfume. Caesar sat above it in the special chair—half god, half tired general—watching the city perform itself. Gold caught the February sun and threw it back into the crowd’s eyes until looking up hurt in a useful way.

When Antony came forward with the diadem—call it wreath, call it crown’s cousin, call it a test—the Forum’s noise changed pitch. Offer. The gesture hung. Wool and metal. A child’s cry somewhere under the press. Antony’s grin was wide enough to hide whether he had been told to offer or had offered to see what broke.

Caesar refused.

Not once only—refused in a way that let every watcher invent a script: humility, theatre, or a trap dressed as a joke. Antony lowered the thing as if lowering a cup he had not meant to empty. The Forum exhaled wrong—half relief, half hunger.

“He will not be king!” someone shouted, relieved.

“He already is!” someone else shouted, not relieved.

In the press Antony saw a familiar face go pale—Seius, one of the climbing men who bought applause and sold prudence. Seius’s mouth opened on festival noise and closed without a joke. Antony noted the pallor the way he noted a soft cohort: useful, not urgent. Beside Seius a freedman was mouthing Parthia and crowns in the same breath. Antony let the mouths blur back into Forum.

Afterward he drank and laughed and said the symbol was temporary. He could already hear what Fulvia would do with that word—price it, not correct it—and he laughed harder so the hearing would have to wait.

By night the city’s elite rooms had a new oxygen: fear of *regnum*, whether or not a diadem had ever truly fit.

# SC-15 — Veiled Talk

POV: Cassius (Brutus in frame) · portico stroll · App. 2.113 energy  
Info ENTER: Separate private thoughts; diadem still sticky in the city · EXIT: Mutual disclosure begins  
Overhear risk: Medium-High

---

They chose a colonnade where shadows made faces honest and mouths careful. The festival was still sticky in the city’s teeth—the diadem’s ghost, the crowd’s half-cheer, Caesar’s refusal that somehow made kingship louder.

Cassius didn't begin with murder. He began with the difference between name and power—the only door a careful man would open without smelling steel.

“If he refuses the name and keeps the power,” Cassius said, almost idle, “what do we call the difference? Latin?”

Brutus walked half a step slower. Ancestors walked with him whether he invited them or not—imagined shelves, wax faces, the dull pressure of being someone’s correct grandson.

“We call it the Republic enduring,” Brutus said.

“We call it a man packing for Parthia with the Senate in his purse.” Cassius glanced once—no lingering—at a slave too near, then at a water-seller’s boy whose ears were the wrong age for politics. “I have spoken to men who are tired of enduring. I am asking if you are one of them, or if you prefer to write essays about Cato while the rest of us choose a morning.”

Brutus stopped. The stop itself was an answer he preferred to speech.

“I am not one of your mornings,” he said. “Not tonight. Not because a wreath made the Forum smell of panic. Panic is how gangs recruit.”

A cart rattled past on the street beyond the columns; someone cursed a mule as if the mule were a tribune. Cassius waited for the noise, then used it.

“Then give me the condition that would make you stop writing and start hearing,” Cassius said. “Or say plainly you will dine as a spared ornament until the East makes the question irrelevant.”

Brutus’ jaw worked. Clemency sat under his tongue like a coin he had not chosen to swallow. “If—only if—anything were ever done, it would have to be law’s face, not a street. I have not agreed to a plan. I have named the only face I could live with. That is not permission.”

“Law’s face,” Cassius repeated, and did not smile. No daggers yet. Only a schedule beginning to grow underneath—men to speak to, rooms to choose, a friend of Caesar’s still unnamed who could open a door. He would bring company next time. Brutus was too good at turning two men into a philosophy lesson.

They walked on as if they had been talking about rain. In Rome, that was often the same conversation.

At the next corner Cassius left Brutus with festival courtesy. Ten paces on, he found Trebonius loitering with a cup he was not drinking.

“Well?” Trebonius asked, mild as Monday.

“He refuses the street and has not accepted the surgery,” Cassius said. “He will need a room with more than my voice in it—or he will postpone until Parthia packs for him.”

Trebonius’s mouth twitched. “Bring Casca. Casca doesn’t do essays. He does doors.”

“And if Brutus still burns invitations?”

“Then we hold Antony with business while better men invent their courage,” Trebonius said, and finally sipped—making the joke look like thirst.
