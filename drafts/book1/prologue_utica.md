# SC-00: The Physician

POV: Cleanthes (Cato’s physician) · Utica · Canon: Plutarch *Cato Min.* 66–70
Date / place: April 46 BC, some days after Thapsus · Cato’s house at Utica, evening to dawn

They sent for Cleanthes because of the hand.

It was the right hand, and it was swollen across the knuckles to the shape of a small loaf, and the skin over the second knuckle had split. Cato held it out to him on the couch the way a man holds out a tablet for sealing, without interest. A lamp stood on the stand beside him and a book lay open under it, a roll of good papyrus, wound most of the way to the end.

“A slave’s teeth,” Cato said. “He would not bring me my sword. I struck him.”

Cleanthes knew which slave. He had passed him in the court with his mouth full of blood and a boy holding a cloth to it. He did not say so. He cleaned the split with wine, felt each finger for a break and found none, and began to bind the hand in linen, firmly, so that it would not swell further in the night.

“Not too tight,” Cato said. “I shall want it.”

Cleanthes kept his eyes on the linen.

All day the harbour had been full of shouting. Since the news of the battle had come from Thapsus, Cato had done nothing but put other men on ships: senators, knights, the young men of good family who had come to Africa to fight for the Republic and would now have to go home and explain themselves. He had stood on the mole in the wind and counted them aboard, and argued with captains about the price, and lent money to men who had none. Caesar was two days off, or one. The people of Utica, who had never wanted any of these Romans in the first place, had begun to speak of mercy in the market as if mercy were a ship that would call for them too.

At dinner, Cleanthes had heard from the kitchen, Cato had argued philosophy with his guests, louder than was his custom. The question was whether only the good man is free. A Peripatetic called Demetrius had taken the other side, as Peripatetics did, and Cato had shouted him down. Afterwards he had walked with his friends, given his orders for the night watch, and embraced his son and each friend more warmly than usual, and gone to his room. In the room he had found his sword gone from the head of the bed, where it always hung. His son had taken it.

That was when he had called for it and struck the slave.

Now the son stood in the doorway with his friends behind him, a young man of twenty-odd with his father’s nose and none of his calm, weeping openly. Cato looked at him over the physician’s head.

“When was I judged out of my mind?” he said. “Has anyone come in to show me where I am wrong? No. You take my weapon. You would tie my hands next, I suppose, and keep me for Caesar like a present.”

“Father…”

“I do not need a sword to die. I can hold my breath. I can beat my head against a wall. Go.”

They went. A little later a small boy came in with the sword and set it against the couch, not looking at anyone, and went out again. Cato drew it a hand’s breadth, looked at the edge, and slid it home.

“Now I am my own master,” he said, to no one Cleanthes could see.

He tied off the linen. Cato flexed the fingers once and winced, and took up the book again. Cleanthes had seen the title on the tag: Plato, on the soul. Socrates in prison, talking with his friends until the hemlock came. Cato read it as men read a contract, close, going back over a passage he had already passed. He had been through it once before dinner, the kitchen said. He was more than halfway through it again.

Cleanthes went out and lay down on a mat in the passage. He did not sleep. Toward midnight he heard Cato snoring, deep and even, the snore of a man who has finished his work for the day, and he lay in the dark and listened to it and did not know what to think.

Before the first birds Cato sent the freedman Butas down to the harbour to see whether all the ships were away. Butas came back with the sky going grey. The ships were gone, he said, all but one; the sea was rough; a wind had got up from the north. Cato groaned aloud at that, for the men at sea, and sent Butas back down to see whether anyone had been forced to put in again and needed help. Cleanthes heard him say it. He heard the concern in it, real concern, and that was the thing he could not afterward explain to anyone: the man had sent Butas out of the room for the sake of strangers on the water.

Butas went. The house was quiet. A bird began in the courtyard, and then another.

Then there was a sound from the room like a heavy sack falling, and after it a clatter of small things on the floor, many small things, and somebody in the passage screamed.

Cleanthes was through the door before the scream ended. The couch was empty. Cato lay on the floor beside it in the lamp’s last light, on his side, with blood all down the front of his tunic and spreading on the tiles. The counting-board that stood by the couch had gone over with him, and its little ivory counters were everywhere, in the blood, under the couch, against Cleanthes’ foot. The sword lay a pace off. The bound hand had not had the strength to drive it home. The blow had gone in under the breast, and wide, and the belly was open, and Cleanthes could see what no physician wants to see in a living man, the bowel come out through the wound.

He was alive. His eyes were open.

The son was there, and the friends, and the household, all shouting. Cleanthes shouted louder than any of them for water and linen and a needle and gut, and for someone to hold the lamp still. Cato’s eyes were open but not seeing. That was a mercy, and Cleanthes used it. He knelt in the counters and the blood and looked. The bowel was not cut. He had expected it to be cut, and it was not; the bound hand had spared it. With clean hands and wine and great care he eased it back into the belly, a little at a time, while a slave held the lamp and wept and the lamp shook. Then he closed the wound and began to sew.

He sewed well. He had been taught in Cos by a man who had been taught by men who had been taught by Hippocrates’ own pupils, and he was proud of his hands, and in the worst hours of that war he had never been prouder of them than now. Small stitches, close together, each knot drawn down firm. The bleeding slowed. Someone behind him began to pray. He tied the last knot, and sat back on his heels, and wiped his hands, and looked up at the son to tell him that his father might live.

Cato was looking at him.

The eyes were seeing now. They went from Cleanthes to the stitches and back, and something in the face changed, and Cleanthes had time to understand it and no time to move. Cato pushed him away with the bound hand, hard enough to put him on his back among the counters. Then he put both hands into the wound Cleanthes had closed and tore it open.

The son caught at his arms. The friends caught at his arms. It was too late, and Cleanthes knew it was too late, because he had seen what the hands did and where they went. Cato did not cry out. He made a sound of effort, as a man does lifting something heavy, and his hands came out of himself red to the wrist, and he lay back on the tiles, and after a little while he died.

He was forty-eight. Cleanthes heard someone say so later, as if it were an argument.

By full light the whole town knew. People came to the door and stood in the street, Uticans who had never loved him, and wept. By the middle of the morning a rider came in from the south road with dust on him to say that Caesar’s horse were on the road, near, and that Caesar himself was not far behind, and was saying already that he meant to spare the town, and the son, and anyone who asked.

Cleanthes sat in the court and washed the needle in a bowl of water until the water was pink and then clear. He had sewn well. That had been the trouble. He had given the man back his life without asking him, and the man would not take it from him either.

Somebody had gathered the ivory counters and set them in a dish on the step, sticky still, all mixed together, so that there was no telling what sum they had been keeping. Caesar, the rider said, would be in Utica by evening, and he would want to know who had sewn him.
