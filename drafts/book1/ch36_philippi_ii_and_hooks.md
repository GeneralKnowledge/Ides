# SC-92: Second Day

POV: Brutus (+ OCT breath) · Philippi
Date / place: 23 Oct 42 · Brutus’s line / Octavian’s camp
Canon refs: A14 · FS02 illness · credit contested C · FS10 seed
Info ENTER: Second engagement · EXIT: Brutus dead; OCT ill/credit contested; land seed in same breath
Overhear risk: Camp

### Brutus

Brutus pressed two fingers under his ribs where the reports had lodged and found no relief there.

He had the versions in incompatible shapes. Cassius broken. Cassius mistaken. Cassius ended by his own hand on a lie the plain told. It did not matter which. What they shared was enough: Cassius was gone. The weight of that sat in the body first, breath short, ribs tight, the old courtesy habit wanting a sentence that would arrange grief into something useful. He had no sentence. He had a line that still needed bodies.

Twenty days since the first fight. He had sent Cassius’s body across the strait to Thasos by night, so the camp would not have to watch it burn and count what was left. Then he had sat on his hill and let the other side go hungry. The sea fed him; the plain fed no one. It had been a good plan and his officers could not bear it. Men slipped across to Antony’s fires after dark, and the ones who stayed called waiting cowardice to his face, in Cassius’s words, as if they had inherited them. This morning he had given them their battle.

He didn’t deliver a speech about it. This was weight and breath and mud that took boots the way grief took time.

He fought like a man hoping philosophy could stiffen a shield-wall. For a while it did. Faces that had minted *EID MAR* with him held because holding was what they still knew how to do. He moved among them with the old etiquette of a man who still believed posture mattered: nod, correct a gap, refuse the luxury of sitting when the men stood. Mud took his boots. Dust took his mouth. He tasted metal and did not ask whether it was blood or only fear. An aide offered water. Brutus took it, swallowed wrong, coughed once, ugly, human, and went back to the gap.

Then mud and numbers undid the stiffening. He saw men who wanted tomorrow’s grain more than yesterday’s name. Both kinds broke when the plain insisted. An officer, face known from the minting days, caught his sleeve and begged him to pull back to a cleaner hill. The cleaner hill was visible. Higher ground. Fresher air. A place where the day might be lost slower and with more witnesses who could later invent dignity.

Brutus looked at it. The cost of taking it was clear in the body. Abandon the knot that still held. Leave the men who still answered *EID MAR* with their shoulders. Trade the day’s shape for a cleaner story about retreat. Cassius would have called delay contemptible. Cassius was not here to call anything.

“Hold what you can,” Brutus said. Not a period. Not an antithesis. Only an order with the paint worn off.

He refused the cleaner hill. The knot shrank. The breath got shorter. The officer’s face went from hope to something flatter. Brutus held what he could until what he could was a shrinking knot that no philosophy could stiffen again. Only then, at dusk, with nothing left on the plain to hold, did he take what remained up into the hills behind the line: the retreat the morning had refused, now with no day left to save.

Night came down cold over a stream bed among rocks. A few hundred men had followed him; fewer were there by midnight. He sent a man down to see whether the camp could be retaken. The man did not come back. Volumnius, who had argued Plato with him for twenty years, refused when Brutus asked him to hold the sword, and wept, and Brutus did not ask him twice.

Strato held it, a friend from the rhetoric schools in Athens, when rhetoric had seemed the hard part. Brutus took his hand first. Then he set the point under his ribs, where the reports had lodged and Cassius had been sitting all day, and leaned. He did not arrange his posture. There was no one left to arrange it for, only Strato, and Strato looked away.

On the plain below, an *EID MAR* coin lay in the dust with the rest of the metal.

### Octavian

Octavian was ill enough that credit became argument among healthier men.

Fever made the tent canvas pulse. Twenty days ago Brutus’s men had come through this camp and run their swords through the litter he had left an hour before. His doctor had dreamed something and had him carried out into the marsh. Octavian remembered reeds, and lying very still, and being told afterwards that it had been prudence. Sweat ran into his eyes and made the map swim. A courier asked for orders. Octavian gave two that mattered: hold the camp ditch; do not chase beyond the markers Agrippa had set. And one that only sounded like command, a sentence about courage he regretted as soon as it left his mouth. He didn’t invent himself as genius of the plain. He survived noise. He survived other men’s versions of his usefulness.

Agrippa’s reports arrived clearer than his own pulse. “Brutus is breaking,” Agrippa said, or something close enough that Octavian could act without pretending he had seen the break.

“Then do not let our men become a rumour of victory before they are paid,” Octavian said. His voice cracked on *paid*. He hated the crack. Hating it did not stop it.

When someone tried to crown him with the day’s glory in a sentence, Octavian kept breathing and didn’t bow. Sweat ran into the corner of his mouth; he swallowed it and asked Agrippa for the next count of the ditch.

Fever eased, later, into a thin clarity on the road-edge of the camp. “They will want farms,” Agrippa said, Italian soil, promises already louder than the plain’s dust.

“They will want farms that belong to someone else,” Octavian said. He didn’t dress it. He kept one hand flat on the list of promised acres until the paper stopped fluttering. Paper would not hold the soil still. That was the next war’s seed, held in the same breath as survival. He did not invent a destiny for it. He named the want. He kept breathing.

# SC-93: Peak

POV: Antony · post-battle tent · Chain 9
Date / place: Late Oct 42 · Antony’s tent, Philippi
Canon refs: underestimates Italy land rage
Info ENTER: Military prestige · EXIT: Settlement talk; underestimates land crisis
Overhear risk: Officers

---

Antony drank the win without sweetness.

Dust still lived in his teeth; wine only moved it around. “We divide,” he said. “I take the East. It has the money and it is coughing blood. The boy takes Italy and the veterans.” An officer asked after Lepidus. “Lepidus keeps Africa,” Antony said, “if it turns out he has not been writing letters to Sextus Pompey.” Officers congratulated him in the register soldiers used for men who had just rearranged a map with bodies.

They had found Brutus in the morning, on the hill, with his own men’s cloaks under him. Antony had gone up to look. He had not expected to want to, and had wanted to, and had stood over the body a long time while the officers waited further down the slope, and then he had taken off his own cloak, the general’s purple, and laid it over the dead man from the chin down. He had told a freedman to see him burned properly and the ashes sent to his mother. He did not explain it to the officers. He was not sure he could have.

Then the prisoners. Most of them were nobody, and he let the boy’s people have them, because the boy wanted names and Antony wanted dinner. One of them he asked for.

They brought Hortensius in with his hands tied in front of him and his good toga gone and a soldier’s cloak over his shoulders that somebody had given him out of pity. The orator’s son. He had his father’s looks still, a little, under the dirt.

“You held the ship,” Antony said.

Hortensius did not pretend not to understand. “I had the order from Brutus.”

“I know you had the order.” Antony turned his cup. The officers had gone quiet. “I asked whether you held the ship.”

“Yes,” said Hortensius.

Gaius had been killed in the spring, quickly, without insult, as the order said, and his servants had buried him in a manner fitting his rank, in a tomb by the road on the coast, and the news had come to Antony in Italy in a letter he had read standing up and then put in the brazier. He had not wept at the time. He had had the lists to finish. He found now, with the man in front of him who had done it, that the tears were still waiting, and that he did not intend to let them out in front of officers.

“Take him to my brother,” he said. “Do it there. On the stones.”

Nobody asked whether he meant the coast, which was a long way off, or whether he would be content with something nearer and quicker and a stone that would do. Antony did not tell them. Hortensius looked at him a moment longer, with his father’s eyes, and then the soldiers took him out, and Antony drank, and the officers began, carefully, to talk about the East.

A tribune mentioned Italian land assignments, farms, promises. Antony waved it toward tomorrow with the same hand he had used to point cohorts. “The boy’s tomorrow. Italy will wait. Italy always complains. The East is bleeding now.”

By the tent-flap, a cup further into the wine than his rank allowed, a junior officer said it to the horse-holder loud enough to carry: “Italy always complains until it bills you in veterans who know which farms have owners.” The horse stamped. Antony heard it, laughed once, short, and went back to the map.

At the end of the table a clerk tried to enter victory in a clean column and stopped. The stylus had nothing honest to finish with. Antony saw the open wax and did not ask why. The East pulled at him. A list of promised acres lay under his cup, unread, the boy’s tomorrow.

# SC-95: Open Wax

POV: F4 Hordeonius · Rome
Date / place: Nov 42 · an ownerless shop, Rome
Canon refs: SIDE-1b OPEN-0 · FS10
Info ENTER: Philippi news by courier · EXIT: Visellius unsolved; land question open
Overhear risk: None

---

The courier said *Philippi* as if it were a receipt.

Hordeonius was in a shop that no longer had an owner, pricing shelves for an auction the lists had paid for. Two Liberators dead. Antony to the East. The boy to Italy and the veterans. He wrote it down in the column where he kept weather.

Under the counter his thumb found a debt mark scratched into the wood, a sum and a name he did not know. He thought of Visellius on his floor, and of the copies he had taken that no magistrate had ever asked for. No one had solved Visellius. Civil war did not keep a magistrate free for a moneylender’s throat.

He left the tablet open on the counter. Soldiers would want farms, and every farm in Italy already carried someone’s name. The open wax and the old floor asked the same question, and his seals could not close it: who pays for dirt that already has owners.

# SC-95a: The True Number

POV: F1 Nysa · Rome
Date / place: Winter 42–41 · the Appian Way at the Capena gate; the Carinae kitchen and its back door
Canon refs: T1 · Phase 3 · Ch 25 (the count) · Ch 33 (the patron)
Info ENTER: Philippi won; Antony gone East; the boy, sick, coming home to Italy · EXIT: Nysa gives the true number to the steward too late, and goes to her patron’s house under her own name
Overhear risk: The kitchen

---

The boy came home to Rome in the winter, ill, up the Appian Way from Brundisium, and the steward sent Nysa out to count.

He did not say *the boy* any more. Nobody in the Carinae house did. Antony had gone East with the money and the legions and the best of the cooks, and left his wife and his brother to keep the city, and in the kitchen they had learned to say *the young Caesar*, quietly, the way you say the name of a creditor in his own street.

“How many,” the steward said. “On the road. At the gate. All of it.” He did not look at her. “Properly this time.”

So he knew. Nysa had wondered for two years whether he knew. She had watched him at the door of the dining room through the whole winter of Mutina and the summer of the consulship and the winter of the lists, a careful man going grey, and had never been able to tell. Now she could.

She went out before light with a heel of bread and stood at the Capena gate, where the Appian Way came in, among the people who had come to see.

There were more than at the games. She counted them the way she had counted the benches, in rows and then in tens, along the road and up the steps of the tombs and on the roofs of the tavern by the gate, and when the rows ran out she counted the people still coming down the hill behind her from the Caelian. Freedmen with their caps on. Shopkeepers with their shutters up. Veterans, a great many veterans, standing in their old units without being told to, with their discharge tablets in their belts. Women. Children on shoulders. A man selling hot chickpeas who sold out before the second hour and stood with his empty pan and cheered.

She thought about the cheer. It was not the cheer of the games, from the cheap rows, that went on after the chair had gone. It was quieter. It had a question in it. The rumour all winter had been that the young Caesar was dying at Brundisium, that he was dead, that the litter coming up the road was empty. People had come out to see a face.

The litter came at noon. The curtains were open. Inside it, propped on cushions, a thin young man with a pale face and a cloak to the chin, looking out at them. Not waving. Looking. His eyes went along the crowd the way Nysa’s went along the benches, row by row, and for one moment, which she knew perfectly well meant nothing, they went along her row.

The cheer, then, was a real one. It went up the road and through the gate like water.

She counted it twice.

The steward was waiting in the scullery when she came back. He had a tablet ready. He had not had a tablet at the games.

She gave him the number.

He wrote it down. He looked at it for a long time. Then he said, without looking up, “At the games you told me some thousands. The cheap rows. Fewer than for the fullers’ guild.”

“Yes.”

“He laughed,” the steward said. “At dinner. I told him your number and he laughed. You heard him. I know you heard, you were in the passage with the jug.” He put the stylus down very carefully. “He laughed for two years, Nysa. Through the whole of Mutina he was laughing at that boy. I think about that sometimes. How much of it was your number.”

“Not much,” Nysa said. “He’d have laughed at a bigger one.”

He looked up at her then. She had expected anger. It was not anger. It was something more tired than that.

“Why?”

She could have said a great many things. That in the Domus the people who carried bad numbers into rooms were the people the rooms remembered. That he had not wanted the true number; his face had told her so before he asked. That the true number had gone to someone who did want it, and done more good there, or at least no harm. That she had been a freedwoman in a stranger’s kitchen for three months and had wanted, very badly, to stay fed.

“You asked me with your face,” she said. “I answered the face.”

The steward was quiet. Then he did a thing she had not expected. He rubbed out the number on the tablet with the flat of the stylus, carefully, line by line, until the wax was blank, and handed it to her.

“There’s a freedman of the young Caesar’s at the back door,” he said. “Since the morning. With a list. The old man’s freedmen and freedwomen in the city, the ones still owing their duty to the house.” He did not quite look at her. “Your name’s on it. Julia Nysa. He asked for you by it. I told him you were out counting.”

She stood holding the blank tablet.

“The mistress would keep you,” the steward said. “Fulvia’s woman has said so. You’d have the scullery and a bed by the oven and the silver room at dawn, and nobody to ask you anything but her. It’s a good house to be in, this spring.” He paused. “It may not be a good house to be in by the summer. You count. You know that.”

She did know it. She had heard Fulvia through the walls all winter, and Lucius, Antony’s brother, the new consul, talking about the young Caesar and the veterans and the land in a voice that was going up.

She went to the back door.

The freedman was sitting on the step in the cold with a list on his knees and his hands in his sleeves. He was older than she had expected, with a clerk’s stoop and a clerk’s patience, and he looked up at her without hope, the way men look up who have been told the person they want is out.

“I’m looking for a freedwoman of the divine Julius,” he said. “Of the old household. Nysa.”

She had been Nysa for most of her life. Before that she had been a name she did not remember, in a place she did not remember, and before the Ides she had been *girl* and *you* and the water-jug that walked. In law she had been something else since the day the old man touched her with the rod in front of the praetor, and she had never once said it out loud.

“Julia Nysa,” she said.

He found the name on his list and made a mark beside it. It was a small mark. It took less time than a breath.

“The young Caesar’s house,” he said. “Tomorrow, before light. They’ll want to know who carries.”

She went back in for her bundle: two tunics, a comb, the cracked oil flask. Fulvia’s woman was in the passage. She did not try to stop her. She only looked at the bundle, and at the tablet Nysa was still holding, and said, “Blank?”

“Blank,” said Nysa.

“Good,” said Fulvia’s woman, and stood aside.

# SC-95b: Twine

POV: F3 Laberius · Campania
Date / place: Spring 41 · the plain north of Capua; the line of twine
Canon refs: T4 · Phase 3 · FS10 (the land war, as a hook)
Info ENTER: Discharged after Philippi; the young Caesar settling the veterans on Italian land; Gaius dead at Mutina, Vibius alive · EXIT: The twine has a farmer standing on it
Overhear risk: The surveyor’s party; the farmer’s family

---

The twine was still there.

Laberius had not expected it to be. Four years since Munda, three since the Ides, two wars, a list, a triumph, a marsh at Forum Gallorum and a marsh at Philippi, and the line of twine some colony clerk had pegged out across a field north of Capua the summer after Spain was still there, grey now, gone to string, tied to stakes the weather had leaned sideways. Somebody had walked round it for years. Somebody had ploughed round it. There was a neat strip of unploughed ground either side of it, a hand wide, like a scar.

He stood at the end of it with his discharge tablet in his belt and Vibius beside him and the surveyor’s party coming up the track behind with their groma and their chains and their two mules, and the young Caesar’s freedman at the head of them with a list.

“That’s it?” Vibius said.

“That’s it.”

“It’s good dirt.” Vibius scuffed it with the toe of his boot. “It’s really good dirt, Cordus. Look at it.”

Laberius looked at it, past his own feet in Gaius’s boots, which had been resoled at Thessalonica by a cobbler who charged double for a Roman and had walked from Mutina to Philippi and back without Gaius in them. It was good dirt, dark and loose and smelling of spring. There were beans in it. Rows of young beans, a hand high, running right up to the twine on one side and right on from the twine on the other side, as if the twine were not there.

The farmer was standing in the beans.

He had come out of the house at the end of the field when he saw the surveyor’s party on the track, and walked down between the rows, not fast, and stopped a little way off with his hands hanging at his sides. A man of fifty or so, brown, square, in a tunic with the sleeves torn off. Behind him at the house door a woman with a child on her hip, and two more children holding her skirt, and an old man sitting on a bench in the sun who did not get up.

“This is the plot,” said the freedman, reading. “Sextus Laberius Cordus, centurion, discharged. Fourth line from the Volturnus road. From the boundary stone to the second stake, thence along the line to the ditch. Marked after Munda, and confirmed.” He looked up. “Is that right?”

“That’s right,” Laberius said.

The surveyor set up his groma by the boundary stone and squinted along it. His men ran the chain out. It went along the twine, more or less, and then straight on through the beans, because the twine had been laid by a clerk in a hurry four summers ago and the surveyor’s chain had been laid by a surveyor. Where the chain went the surveyor’s boy drove a new stake, a clean white one, through a row of beans, and the farmer watched it go in and did not move.

“This land belongs to Gnaeus Aulius,” the farmer said. He said it to the freedman, not to Laberius. “My father bought it from the man who had it before the war. I have the tablet. I can show you the tablet.”

“I’m sure you can,” said the freedman, not unkindly. He had said this before, many times, this spring. “The land of eighteen cities is assigned to the veterans by the triumvirs’ decree. Capua is one of them. There’ll be compensation.”

“When?”

The freedman did not answer that. He made a mark on his list.

There were other veterans in the surveyor’s party, waiting for their own plots further up the plain. One of them came and stood by Laberius now, an older man with a scar through his lip. Laberius had seen him at the funeral with a torch, and at the colony forum after the Ides, and on the bridge at Casilinum the day the boy paid five hundred, and he still did not know the man’s name.

“Dirt with a date,” the scar-lipped man said. “I told you that once. At the funeral.”

“You did.”

The man grinned with half his mouth. “Well. Here’s the date.” He looked at the farmer in the beans, and at the woman at the door, and the grin went away. “Mine’s two fields up. There’s a vine on it. Old one. Somebody’s grandfather planted it.” He spat, carefully, away from the beans. “I fought for it, though. I fought for that vine in Spain and at Mutina and at Philippi. Nobody asked the vine.”

He went back up the track to wait for his turn with the chain.

The surveyor called the measure. The boy drove the last stake. The freedman wrote the plot into his list, and read it back, and asked Laberius to make his mark, and Laberius made it, and the freedman thanked him and moved on up the track with the surveyor and the chain and the mules and the scar-lipped man and the rest of them, toward the vine.

Vibius stayed. Laberius stayed.

The farmer stayed, in the beans, with the new white stake beside him.

For a while nobody did anything. A lark went up out of the next field. The old man on the bench at the house door had not moved at all. The woman had put the child down and was holding the other two by the shoulders, one in each hand.

Laberius thought about the man on his left. He had thought about the man on his left for twenty years, in Gaul and at Munda and on the road to Forum Gallorum and in the marsh at Philippi, and it had always been easy, because the man on his left had always been a soldier, and the answer had always been to stand. He looked at the farmer, who was standing exactly where the twine said the left side of Laberius’s land began, and found that he did not know which side of the line the man was on.

“Well,” said Vibius, at last. “It’s good dirt.”

Laberius did not answer. He took his tablet out of his belt and looked at it, the way he had looked at the boy’s five hundred at Casilinum, and the Senate’s thanks at Mutina, and the lists in the Forum, all the paper that had come to him in his life with his name on it, saying what he was owed.

Then he put it back, and stepped over the twine, and walked down between the bean rows toward the man who was standing in them.
