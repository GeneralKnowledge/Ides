# SC-05: Hard-Pressed

POV: F3 Laberius · Hispania camp
Date / place: Pre-Munda · 45 BC · contubernium / mud lines
Canon refs: A2 · B4
Info ENTER: Rumour Caesar pressed · EXIT: Battle orders; Rome thinks easy
Overhear risk: Contubernium gossip

---

Laberius chewed rumour with hardtack. Rome, the letters said, thought this was mopping. The camp knew mopping that killed. Pompeian sons still hard in the hills. Veterans with nothing left but stubbornness. The ground held last night’s rain like a grudge.

“Pompey’s dead,” said the man on his left, Gaius of the split sandal. He talked to keep his fingers warm. “Cato’s dead. Africa’s shut. And still his two boys find the far end of the world to stand on, and every fool who’d sooner lose with a Pompey than live on a pardon.”

Gnaeus and Sextus. Caesar had come from Rome to the bottom of Spain in under a month to finish them, and Laberius had marched every mile of it, which was why his knees had opinions.

“And Rome thinks we’re polishing a triumph,” Gaius said.

“They can think in warm baths,” Laberius said. “We think in mud.”

Vibius, his optio, spat a black fleck and checked the rivets on a shield that had already argued with Spain once. In a fight Vibius stood behind the century and kept it from running, mostly by being more frightening than whatever was in front of it. You could see their breath. Orders always came in that hour.

Gaius tried a grin that cracked dry lips. “If I die tidy, tell Rome I was mopping. If I die filthy, tell them I was founding a colony with my face.”

“You haven’t got the face for it,” said Vibius.

“Colonies have been founded on worse.”

“Not by anyone who lived to collect.” The next file looked over. Vibius slapped Gaius’s shoulder once, hard. “Found the colony after the donative. Dirt without coin is just a grave with opinions.”

“Donative’s silver in the hand,” Gaius said. “I can tell you the day of the last one. Now tell me the day of the colony.”

Nobody could. The farm at the end of the war had never once come with a date.

Laberius checked his boots. Checked Gaius again. Checked the dull place behind his knees that meant too little sleep. Philosophy could wait for men with tents. He had a century. He had a fear he refused to name. Naming made it louder.

A courier’s gossip claimed Caesar had been hard-pressed yesterday. Another claimed the opposite with the same confidence. The second one said he had seen the general pull a shield from a man’s arm and walk to the front of the Tenth himself, because the line was bending and Caesar had decided he would rather die in front of it than watch it break. Gaius wanted that one to be true. Vibius said generals always walked to the front in the stories, and never in the mud.

“Which is it?” Gaius asked Laberius.

“Noise until steel,” Laberius said, and filed both. When the signal came, packs and lines moved. Men cursed short and got to work.

An older miles on his right mumbled a prayer that sounded like a shopping list: knees, sons, the next donative, land near Capua if the gods and clerks both remembered. Laberius didn’t mock him. He still needed water. He filed *donative dated* beside *colony rumour* the way he filed water: needed, not guaranteed.

He did not know what Rome would call this morning. He knew what his hands would smell like by night if the morning kept its promises.

# SC-06: Sextus Slips

POV: F3 · post-battle roads
Date / place: After Munda · debris roads / stacked shields
Canon refs: A2 · FS11
Info ENTER: Labienus finished; Sextus slipped · EXIT: Sea shrug; donative before boots dry
Overhear risk: Low–Medium

---

After the field stank of ending, iron and shit and the sweet wrongness of opened bodies, word ran that one Pompeian name was finished and another had slipped somehow. The finished one was Labienus. The older men said the name with something close to respect: ten years at Caesar’s right hand in Gaul, then at Pompey’s through the whole war, every trick the general owned and half of them his own invention. Caesar had once trusted him with an army. Now he lay face down in the barley with the others.

The one who slipped was Sextus, the younger son. Escape stories always arrived cleaner than escapes.

An optio spat. “Someone’s always left for the sea.”

Laberius was already thinking of pay and whether the colony rumours were lies. “Let clerks name the fish. I want the donative dated before my boots dry.”

He stacked shields until his forearms hummed. A boy from another century vomited behind a cart and pretended it was dust. Someone sang half a victory and lost the tune. Flies had already found the work. A staff man arrived late with Rome’s easy-win story dressed as fact: mopping finished; Spain settled. Somebody said his name was Sextilius and that he had come out with Caesar’s people from Rome. He had clean sandals.

“Rome is already building the triumph,” Sextilius said, brightly. “They’ll call it a Spanish triumph, naturally. One cannot triumph over Romans.”

“Naturally,” said Vibius. “And what do they call us, when we’ve killed thousands of them?”

“The grateful army.”

Laberius put on the grateful face. He took the water. He nodded like a man in a painting. “Grateful for the clarity,” he said.

When Sextilius’s clean sandals had gone, Laberius spat pink and found Vibius and Gaius still within grumbling range. “Settled,” he said. “My boots disagree. Tell Rome the mopping asked for a donative before it dried. Tell them Capua if they’re feeling generous. Tell them nothing if they only want statues.”

Vibius’s mouth twitched. “You’ll get a statue that owes you pay.”

Gaius laughed once, short, then winced. Laughing pulled a stitch. “Statue first. Colony later. Same as always.”

Vibius asked if Sextus mattered. Laberius answered with the only theology he owned: “If he can pay men, he matters. If he can’t, the sea can keep him.”

“Pompey’s boy,” said Gaius. “He’ll pay men. They always find a man with his father’s name and a ship.”

“Then Rome will have another spring,” Laberius said, “and we will have another set of knees to complain about.”

Someone claimed Caesar had nearly broken. Someone else claimed Caesar had never blinked. Laberius trusted neither story enough to repeat it. He trusted the ache in his shoulders. He trusted that paydays survived speeches. Rome’s easy story would outrun any truth about mud.

He didn’t follow the escape. Following was for cavalry and fools. He followed the next order: water, then sleep that wouldn’t come clean.

# SC-07: Ancestors

POV: Brutus · domus · Porcia pressure
Date / place: Late 45 BC · evening · Brutus’ house
Canon refs: C2 · soft anchors S1 (Porcia marriage ~45)
Info ENTER: Clemency beneficiary · EXIT: Ancestral script louder; refuses another soft-chain dinner
Overhear risk: Household high

---

Brutus sat under masks that judged eating.

Wax faces in a row along the atrium, each taken from a dead man’s own face, each an office held. He had walked behind them at funerals, worn by hired men, and as a boy he had been made to name them in order. At dinner they only looked.

*Lucius Junius Brutus, who drove out the last king, and put his own sons to death when they plotted to bring the king back.* He could still say it in the sing-song his tutor had beaten into him. *Gaius Servilius Ahala, on my mother’s side, who killed a man for reaching after a crown.* Two traditions on one wall, and both ended in blood.

Porcia’s voice stayed low. Low was harder to dodge than shouting.

“Worth is not a dinner conversation,” she said. “It is what you do when dinners become easy.”

“That sounds like your father.”

“It sounds like someone who had to eat with him.” Cato’s daughter, and his wife for less than a year: long enough to learn that her husband could weigh a sentence for an hour and never let it fall. “You are looking at your ancestors as if they owe you a way out. They don’t. They killed a king. You are being asked to accept one.”

“Caesar is not a king.”

“Caesar is a dictator for ten years, and Rome is still deciding what to call it. Names are cheap. Yours is expensive.”

Clemency sat in the room like a soft chain. Caesar had spared. At Pharsalus he had sent word down the line before the fighting began: take Brutus alive, if it can be done. Brutus had lived. Tonight the spared life felt less like gratitude and more like a costume that still fit too well. Spain was “settled” in other men’s mouths. The chain had not loosened. He reached for a philosophical sentence and found only the cup in his hand. Wine gone warm and flat. Unbidden: the hour after the spare. Caesar had kept him late over ordinary talk, laughed at something small, and sent him home as if the war between them had been a misunderstanding their house could afford. That warmth was the part of the chain that still cut.

Before Porcia could press again, a steward brought word that his mother’s messenger waited in the outer court. Servilia. The whole city knew she was the woman Caesar had loved longest; Brutus had spent twenty years not knowing it. Not quite an invitation. A mother’s reminder: pardoned sons still owed dinners they did not choose. Soft chains got priced in women’s rooms first. Men’s masks judged later. Porcia’s mouth tightened. “She sends weather,” Porcia said. “I send refusal. Do not confuse the two.”

“I am not unaware of what the name requires,” Brutus said.

“Then stop practising unawareness as courtesy,” Porcia answered.

A slave’s foot whispered in the corridor. Ordinary. Brutus lowered his voice anyway.

The foot returned with a tablet: an invitation, seal still soft, from a Caesarian host whose dinners made pardoned men feel forgiven and owned in the same course. Brutus had already heard what such rooms did. Clemency toasted as spice last year while Africa’s favourites laughed on cue. Spain’s new men would do the same now. He read the names of the promised guests: two new Spain-postings, a man who still called mercy a flavour. The chain tugged toward the couch he was expected to warm.

Porcia watched his hands, not the wax. “You will go,” she said, not as question.

Brutus held the tablet above the lamp until the edge blackened and the host’s compliments curled. He set the ruined courtesy on the side table carefully. Neatness would not turn refusal into housekeeping, but he tried.

“I will send regrets,” he said. “A cousin’s fever. Something that smells like duty.”

“Regrets are still courtesy,” Porcia said. “Burning is nearer the truth.”

He didn’t decide on knives. He decided, smaller and worse, on a dinner he wouldn’t attend and a smile he wouldn’t owe. He scraped the blackened tablet into the ash pan so no slave would have to invent a cleaner story for the house.

Porcia did not thank him.

He looked at the masks and stopped looking before the paint could answer.

# SC-09: Second Line

POV: Decimus · portico / outer rooms · FS05 · FS21
Date / place: Late 45 BC · Caesar’s outer orbit · afternoon
Canon refs: M5 seed · INS
Info ENTER: Trusted legate glow · EXIT: Hears secondary heir whisper, pride + unease
Overhear risk: Medium

---

Caesar had kept him half an hour on roads.

Not glory roads. Ditch timing. Which bridge would take wagons after rain. Whether a courier could shave a day between Mutina and the city without killing the horse. Decimus had been choosing the width of Mutina’s ditches in his head since the day he heard: a walled town on the great northern road, in the Cisalpine province Caesar meant to hand him next year. Decimus answered with the bluntness Caesar liked: numbers, not compliments. Caesar laughed once at a Spanish memory Decimus had not meant as comedy. The laugh landed in Decimus’s chest. Proof he still belonged in the room where the day was decided.

*Decimus Brutus, not the other one*: he had been hearing it since boyhood. The joke in the clubs was that the family had made two Brutuses, one for philosophy and one for roads, and Caesar had taken the one who knew where the bridges were. Ten years of it: the ships he had built for Caesar against the Veneti, the fleet he had taken in against Massilia, a dozen northern rivers bridged in the rain. Decimus had stopped counting what he owed. Caesar never mentioned it either. That was how you knew it was friendship.

“Come back after the afternoon clients,” Caesar said. “I want your eye on the Campus route before the games talk turns into a fight.”

Decimus tilted his head. “If the games talk turns into a fight, I’ll bring a bigger ditch.”

Caesar’s mouth did the almost-laugh Decimus had learned to aim for. “Bring the ditch. Leave the philosophy. Philosophy trips on stones.”

Useful. Warm. Specific. Decimus left the inner room still tasting it. Essays had never earned him that.

Someone laughed that Decimus was written into the will’s second line.

The laugh came from the outer portico, from a man who collected other men’s secrets like party favours and had nothing better to do that morning. Everyone in Rome who owned a seal had a theory about Caesar’s will; his had a price on it. A second lounger stood with him: thin ring, thinner loyalty, smiling for anyone who might be Caesar’s ear.

“Heirs in order,” the laugher said, soft enough to deny. “First the blood that matters, some great-nephew nobody’s had cause to notice. Then, if the boy fails, the man who knows the roads. Second line.” He let it sit. “Still, imagine being ink while the queen’s boy across the river learns Latin jokes.”

The thin-ring man inclined his head. “Careful. Cleopatra’s boy has a name now. Caesarion, the Alexandrians call him: little Caesar. Two years old, and his mother keeps house in Caesar’s own gardens across the river while the whole city practises not noticing. A joke or a claim, depending who pays for dinner. Transtiberim talk travels. Some men care about the child; some about the gardens; some about whoever still gets half an hour on roads.”

“I care about the dinner,” said the laugher. “The rest is seasoning.”

Decimus felt warmth, then a draft along the portico tiles. He joked once, correctly, about Spanish wine. The joke bought him exit without looking stabbed. Both loungers laughed on cue.

“Spanish wine,” said the laugher, to his friend, as Decimus turned. “A man of the people.”

He touched the portico column once, habit, and went back toward Caesar’s voice. That was still where his day lived. Caesar was already speaking to a clerk about grain. He glanced up, found Decimus, and tipped his chin. As if other men’s envy had never interrupted the afternoon.

“Campus,” Caesar said. “Before the light goes dishonest.”

Decimus fell into step. He didn’t know the wax. He knew the chin-tip. The roads. The laugh about Spain. Other men were already pricing those hours behind his back.
