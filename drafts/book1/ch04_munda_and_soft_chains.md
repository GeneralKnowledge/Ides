# SC-05: Hard-Pressed

POV: F3 Laberius · Hispania camp
Date / place: Munda · 17 March 45 BC · the lines before the battle, the hill, the lines after
Canon refs: A2 · B4
Info ENTER: The hill and the stream; Rome calls it mopping up · EXIT: Caesar seen on foot before the Tenth; Labienus dead, the elder Pompey wounded, Sextus making for the sea; names taken for the Capua list with no day; the donative asked for with a day on it
Overhear risk: Contubernium gossip

---

It had rained in the night and the ground had not finished with it. Laberius sat on his shield to keep his backside out of the wet and worked a piece of hardtack round to the side of his mouth that still had the teeth for it. Caesar had come from Rome to the bottom of Spain in twenty-seven days, men said. The legion had taken longer, and somewhere on that road he had stopped being able to feel the small toe of his left foot, and it had not come back.

“Pompey’s dead,” said Gaius, on his left. He talked to keep his fingers warm. He had them tucked in his armpits and his chin down in his scarf. “Cato’s dead. Africa’s shut. And the two boys go and find the far end of the world to stand on, and every fool who’d sooner lose with a Pompey than live on a pardon goes and stands there with them. On a hill. Have you seen the hill?”

“I’ve seen it.”

“It’s a big hill.”

Laberius had walked out at first light as far as the pickets would let him. There was a long slope of wet grass with the town on top and more standards in front of the town than he cared to count, and at the bottom a stream, soft on both banks. He had not looked at the standards for long. He had looked at the stream, and at how far a man in mail would have to climb after he had got his feet wet crossing it.

“My sister’s husband wrote,” Gaius said. “He says in Rome they’re calling it mopping up. He used the words.”

“He can come and hold the mop.”

Vibius laughed at that, once, without looking up. The optio was going round a shield rim with his thumbnail, one rivet at a time. In a fight he stood behind the century with a staff and kept it facing the right way, and the men were more afraid of him than of most things that came at them from the front. That was what he was for.

The man from the next tent came over with a wineskin under his cloak. Rufrius was a big slow Picene who had been next door since Ilerda and had never yet arrived anywhere with his hands empty. He held the skin out to Laberius first.

“Cordus.”

“Is it yours?”

“It is now.”

It was sour and thin and warm from the man’s body. Laberius drank and passed it left. Gaius had to take his hands out to hold it, and complained, and drank, and then put his left foot up on his knee and said, “Look at that.”

The heel of the boot had come away from the sole for the width of two fingers. There was mud in the gap.

“That’s Spain,” Gaius said. “Spain’s done that.”

“You’ve done that. You walk on the backs of your feet.” Laberius took the foot in both hands and bent the sole. It would hold going up a hill. It would not hold coming down one in a hurry. He found a thong in his pack and bound it twice round under the instep and pulled it tight enough to make Gaius swear.

“If I die tidy,” Gaius said, “tell Rome I was mopping. If I die filthy, tell them I was founding a colony with my face.”

“You haven’t the face for it,” said Vibius.

“Colonies have been founded on worse.”

“Collect the donative first. A dead man gets his plot the same day, and it’s six feet.”

“Donative’s silver in the hand. I can tell you the day of the last one.” Gaius put the foot down and tried his weight on it. “Now tell me the day of the colony.”

Rufrius said there was a list. A man in the Fifth had seen the clerk with it.

“It’s got Caesar’s name on it,” Laberius said.

“That’s a name. I asked for a day.”

“He’s never not paid.”

“He’s never paid on the day, either,” said Vibius, and held the shield up to the light and put it down again.

On Laberius’s right an older soldier, a Campanian with a bad hip, was praying under his breath the way a man goes through what he has been sent to market for: his knees, his two sons, the donative, a bit of land near Capua if the gods and the clerks both remembered. Laberius did not laugh at him. He was going through much the same things himself, and all he had added was Gaius’s heel.

The trumpets went a little after that, and men got up swearing and looked for their helmets.

They went down to the stream in good order and came out of it in worse. The water was only to the knee but the far bank took a man to the ankle and kept what it could, and then the hill began. Laberius remembered the hill afterwards mostly as the back of the man in front of him and the noise the javelins made coming down. He remembered that his breath gave out before his legs did. He remembered Rufrius’s century on the right going backwards one step at a time with their shields still up, as men do when they have not decided to run and are only finding the ground behind them more agreeable, and Vibius’s staff across his own rear rank, and Vibius’s voice.

Then somebody pointed, and he looked right, because everyone was looking right.

Along the slope, where the Tenth was, a man in a red cloak was out in front of the line on foot. He had a shield on his arm that was too big for him, a ranker’s shield. He was by himself. He was walking uphill, not fast, and the javelins were going into the grass round him, and he did not look back to see who was coming.

“That’s him,” Gaius said. “That’s never him.”

Laberius did not know what a general was doing there and nobody was going to tell him. He knew what the Tenth did, because he heard it. After that the men on his right stopped finding the ground behind them and it was uphill again, all of it, until there was no more hill.

By dark his hands smelt as he had known they would. Word came down the stacked shields that Labienus lay face down in the young barley with the rest, Labienus who had been ten years at Caesar’s right hand in Gaul; that the elder boy had been carried off the field with a hole in him; that the younger had never been on it and was making for the sea. Rufrius came by with a rag held to his mouth. A shield rim had taken his front teeth, top and bottom, and he wanted to know if Laberius thought they would grow.

“No.”

“I didn’t think so,” Rufrius said, through the rag.

A clerk walked the lines with a lamp-boy and a tablet, taking names for the Capua list. Laberius gave all three of his and watched the stylus until it had finished. The clerk did not have a day. He said it was not that kind of list.

Behind the clerk came a staff man from headquarters, picking the dry places. His sandals were clean. He told the century that it was settled, that Spain was settled, that Rome would have a triumph out of this, and somebody behind Laberius said the man’s name was Sextilius, as if that helped.

“Sir,” Laberius said. “When you write to Rome. The donative. If it could have a day on it.”

“I’ll mention it, centurion.”

The sandals went on down the line, from dry place to dry place. They were the places Laberius would have picked himself. He watched them out of the lamp and then went to see whether Gaius had got that boot off, or whether it would have to be cut.

# SC-07: Ancestors

POV: Brutus · domus · Porcia pressure
Date / place: Late 45 BC · evening · Brutus’ house
Canon refs: C2 · soft anchors S1 (Porcia marriage ~45)
Info ENTER: Clemency beneficiary · EXIT: Burns Balbus’s invitation, sent by way of Servilia; the old freedman must carry the lie of a cousin’s fever
Overhear risk: Household high

---

Porcia had the small table set in the atrium when they dined alone. Her father had eaten so. From his couch Brutus had the masks in front of him for the whole of the meal, the cupboard doors standing open as she liked them, a row of wax faces each taken from a dead man and each with his offices painted underneath.

As a boy he had been made to say them in order, and he could still do it in the tutor’s sing-song. *Lucius Junius Brutus, who drove out the last king, and put his own sons to death when they plotted to bring the king back. Gaius Servilius Ahala, on my mother’s side, who killed a man for reaching after a crown.* He had been perhaps six. He had not been tall enough to see the faces, and somebody had lifted him.

“You have stopped eating,” Porcia said.

“I beg your pardon.” He took an olive he did not want.

“You dined with Lepidus on the Nones.”

“He is my sister’s husband. It was a family dinner.”

“Who proposed the health?”

“Lepidus.”

“Whose?”

He turned the olive stone over on his plate. She waited for him, and she was better at it than he was.

“And you drank it,” she said. Her voice was low. She did not raise it to him, ever, and he had sometimes wished that she would. “After Pharsalus my father sat up to his meals. He never lay down to dinner again. People thought it was mourning.”

“It was like him.”

“It was uncomfortable. I know, because I had to eat with him.” She had been his wife since the summer. He was still learning which of her remarks were jokes. “You look at them as if they owed you a way out. They don’t. They put a king out of the city. You are being asked to lie down to dinner with one.”

“Caesar is not a king.”

“Caesar is dictator for ten years. What do you call the tenth?”

There was an answer. Antiochus had held that a good man might live under any constitution that left him the use of his judgement, and Brutus had the sentence nearly whole, with its two halves balanced, when he found that what he actually had hold of was his cup. The wine had gone warm. The steward was standing in the doorway, waiting to be seen.

“Your mother’s man is in the outer court, master. With a tablet.”

“How long has he been there?”

“Since the lamps were lit. He would not have you disturbed at table.”

“He is seventy. It is December.” Brutus had sat up without meaning to. “Bring him in. Bring him some of this, warmed. No, bring him the other, it is better.”

It was the old freedman, as he had known it would be, the one who had carried Servilia’s letters since before Brutus could read them. He came in slowly. He had his cloak pinned too high, the way old men wear them, and he would not take the stool. He had been the one who did the lifting. Brutus remembered the smell of his hair, and the faces coming level one after another.

“From Cornelius Balbus, sir. It was left at your mother’s house. By mistake, she says to say.” He held out the tablet. “I’m to wait.”

The seal was still soft. Balbus sent his compliments and hoped for the honour on the fourth day before the Kalends. Two men lately back from Spain would be there to tell the company about Munda. The Dictator had promised to look in, if business allowed.

Porcia was not looking at the tablet. She was looking at his hands.

“You will go,” she said.

He held it over the lamp. He had thought it would burn. It was wood and wax, and what the wax did was run; it ran across the back of his thumb and down over the knuckle, very hot, and he did not let go, because to drop it now would have been absurd. The edge of the wood blackened at last and began to smell. He set it down on the table, square to the edge. The old man had watched the whole of it with a face Brutus knew, the one he had worn when Brutus was six and had done something to a book.

“You may tell my mother that I regret it. There is illness in the family. A cousin’s fever. I am needed.”

The freedman did not move.

“Which cousin, sir?”

“I had not,” Brutus said, and stopped.

“Only they’ll ask. Balbus’s people will ask at her door, since it came by her door. I’d want to say the same name she says.”

Brutus looked at Porcia. She was pouring water into her cup with great attention.

“Servilius. At Tusculum.”

“Is he ill, sir?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll send down to him in the morning as well, so he knows of it.” He said it without any weight, as he would have said that a lamp wanted oil. “And somebody had better carry him something. Your mother will see to that.”

“I am sorry to give you the trouble.”

“It’s no trouble, sir.”

He had been saying so to Brutus for forty years. The wine came, the better one, and Brutus saw him drink it standing, and asked after his chest, and was told about his chest, and sent a boy with a lantern to see him home across the hill. All of that took some time and he gave it his whole mind.

When he came back to the table Porcia had not gone.

“Regrets are a courtesy,” she said. “The burning was nearer.”

“I have refused him.”

“You have refused Balbus. By way of your mother. With a fever.”

He took the tablet to the brazier himself and pushed it down among the coals with the tongs, and stayed until the wood had gone, so that nobody in the house would find it in the morning and have to decide what he had seen. Porcia did not thank him. He had not supposed that she would.

The wax had set white across his knuckle. He peeled it off in one piece. The skin underneath was pink and did not hurt yet. He could not think what to do with the piece, and in the end he put it on the edge of his plate.

# SC-09: Second Line

POV: Decimus · portico / outer rooms · FS05 · FS21
Date / place: Late 45 BC · Caesar’s outer orbit · afternoon
Canon refs: M5 seed · INS
Info ENTER: Trusted legate glow · EXIT: Half-hears that he is in the will’s second line; puts a hand out for Caesar at the step, not needed
Overhear risk: Medium

---

Caesar had kept him half an hour on roads.

“The ditch at Mutina,” Caesar said. He was signing as he talked, and a clerk took each sheet from under his hand. “How wide?”

“Twelve feet where it’s been kept. It hasn’t been kept. On the east side you could walk a cow across.”

“And when it is yours?”

“Eighteen, and I’d face the inner bank with stone.” Decimus had been digging that ditch in his head since the day he heard he was to have the Cisalpine province next year, with the walled town on the great north road in the middle of it. “Sixty days with the men I’ll be given. Forty if nobody asks me to dinner.”

“The bridge.”

“Over the Scultenna. It takes wagons in summer. After three days’ rain I wouldn’t put a loaded one on it. The second pier has moved a hand’s width and nobody has written it down.”

“You have.”

“I walked out on it.”

“A courier, Mutina to the city.”

“Six days. Five if he changes at Ariminum and doesn’t stop to eat at Fanum.”

“Four.”

“Four kills the horse.”

“Then he will want two horses.” Caesar signed another sheet. “You lost a mule at a ford once by being in a hurry. On the Durius.”

“The mule was in a hurry. I was holding its head.”

“You went in with it. I remember your boots going downstream, one after the other, at a decent interval.”

He laughed. Decimus had not told it to be funny. He had been twenty and it had been his first mule. He found he was grinning at the floor.

*Decimus Brutus, not the other one.* He had been hearing it since he was a boy. The joke in the clubs was that the family had made two, one for philosophy and one for roads, and Caesar had taken the one who knew where the bridges were. It was ten years now. There were the ships he had built against the Veneti, and the fleet he had taken in against Massilia, and he did not know how many northern rivers bridged in the rain. He had stopped counting what he was owed for it. Caesar never mentioned it either. That was how you knew it was friendship.

“Come back after the afternoon clients,” Caesar said. “I want your eye on the Campus route before the games talk turns into a fight.”

“If the games talk turns into a fight, I’ll bring a bigger ditch.”

Caesar’s mouth did the thing that was nearly a laugh. “Bring the ditch. Leave the philosophy. Philosophy trips on stones.”

Decimus went out to wait in the portico.

It was forty paces long and he walked it twice. The third column from the door was out of true by a finger; he put his palm on it each time he passed, and each time it was still out. Caesar’s afternoon clients went in past him by twos and came out singly. At the far end two men had taken the bench in the sun. One was stout and wore a new toga. He had done well out of buying dead men’s town houses, and he came to this portico every day as other men went to the baths. The other was thin and wore a thin ring and laughed when the stout one did.

They were talking low. Decimus was not listening. He was reckoning the Campus route, and what he caught of it he caught in pieces at the turn.

“…in the second line, I had it from a man who…”

“…some great-nephew. Nobody’s had cause to notice him…”

“…and if the boy fails, then the one who does his roads.”

He took it at first for a line of battle. The second line was where you put the men you would want when the first gave way. It was a perfectly good place to be put. He had gone ten paces more before he understood that they meant a document, and what kind, and whose roads. The back of his neck went tight. He walked to the end and turned and came back at the same pace.

“Decimus Brutus.” The stout man had got up. He was smiling as a man smiles at a horse he is thinking of buying. “You’ll settle it for us. You’re in and out. The queen’s boy across the river, the little Caesar, as her people call him. Does he get his Latin from his nurse or from his father?”

“I build bridges,” Decimus said. “Ask a man who goes to dinners.”

“Then come to one. I have a Falernian that was laid down under Sulla.”

“I’ve drunk Spanish wine for ten years. A Falernian would kill me.”

They both laughed, on the beat, and the thin one bowed. It was enough to get past them on. Behind him, not quite low enough, the stout man said to his friend, “Spanish wine. A man of the people.”

He put his hand on the third column. It had not moved.

When the last client had come out he went back toward Caesar’s voice. Caesar was talking to a clerk about grain. He looked up, and found Decimus, and tipped his chin, and it was exactly as it had been an hour before.

“Campus,” Caesar said. “Before the light goes dishonest.”

Decimus fell in on his left. There was one step down from the portico to the street, worn in the middle and slick at this season, and he had his hand out for it half a breath before they came to it, as he had put it out on gangplanks and wet timber for ten years. Caesar went down without looking. He was still talking about grain. He had never yet needed the hand. Decimus took it back and looked to see how badly the step was worn, and thought that somebody should turn the stone.
