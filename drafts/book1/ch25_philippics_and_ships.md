# SC-68c: The Hair of the Star

POV: F1 Nysa · Rome · Canon: T9 · `writing/reference/octavian_games_comet.md`
Date / place: Late July 44 · Antony’s Carinae house; the games for Caesar’s Victory; the street home
Canon refs: Pliny *NH* 2.93–94 · Suet. *Aug.* 10 · *Att.* 15.2 (Matius) · App. *BC* 3.28
Info ENTER: Nysa three months in Antony’s kitchen · EXIT: Golden chair kept out; the star seen, meaning left open; Nysa gives the steward a small number and Fulvia’s woman the true one
Overhear risk: Household / crowd

---

The steward of the Carinae house wanted numbers, and he wanted them small.

“You count,” he told Nysa on the morning the boy’s games opened, as if counting were a dish she had brought over from the Domus with her bundle. “Calpurnia’s people say you count doors. Count heads. How many shout when his name is called. How many for the dead man. How many for the horses. The consul is not going. The consul will ask me at dinner, and I will not be the man who tells him *a great many*.”

Three months in that house had taught her its bow and most of its noise. Fulvia’s women counted silver at dawn and nobody in the kitchen pretended to grieve for anyone. Antony came in from the baths shouting for wine and the whole house turned toward the shout the way a field turns toward weather. She had stopped flinching at it in the first month. In the second she had learned which cupboard the steward watered the good wine in.

He gave her a cloak with no colour in it and a kitchen boy to hold her place on the benches, and she went.

At the entrance Matius’s men were handing out the edict on boards: games for the Victory of Caesar, given by Gaius Caesar his son. A fuller beside her read it aloud for those who could not, and stopped at *son* to see whether anyone would argue. Nobody did. “Matius pays,” someone said behind her. “Caesar’s old friend. The boy hasn’t the money; he sold his farms for the legacy.” “Then who’s paying for the beasts?” “Caesar’s friends. Same as always.”

Nysa found the boy’s seats before she found the boy. They were the seats with the space beside them. The same will that made him Caesar’s son had made her Julia Nysa, and she had come in the steward’s cloak to count his crowd.

A procession came along the track before the first race with Caesar’s gilded chair in it, the one the Senate had voted him, carried shoulder-high under a gold crown. The bearers took short steps under it. Gilt or not, it was heavy, and they carried it like a thing that might still be sat in. They brought it as far as the boy’s row, and three men in good cloaks stepped into the aisle in front of them. One of the three was a tribune; she knew it by the way the others let him speak. He did not raise his voice. The bearers looked toward the boy. The boy did not move.

The chair went back under its cloth, and the crown with it.

The noise that came off the benches then was not a cheer. It came from low in the crowd, from the cheap rows, and it went on after the chair had gone. Nysa began to count it. Rows, then tens in a row, then the rows behind that she could see from her place, the way she had counted guests at the Domus when Calpurnia wanted to know whether the bread would last. By the time the first chariots came round she had a number. She did not like it, and so she counted again.

The boy himself she saw only as a shape: pale, very still, in the place where his father would have sat, with a man beside him who stood when the boy did not. When the herald called *Gaius Caesar*, the cheap rows stood. The good rows looked at one another first and then some of them stood.

On the fourth day, an hour before sunset, the star came.

She was on the street outside, buying a cup of water for the kitchen boy, when the people around her stopped talking and turned north. Low over the roofs there was a star where no star should be at that hour, bright, with a tail of light behind it like hair blown back from a running face. A veteran with one ear beside the water-seller put his cup down. “He watches,” he said, not loud.

“It’s his soul,” a woman said. “Going up. They burned him and he’s going up.”

“Comets mean kings die,” said a Greek with a basket of figs. “Ask anyone in Alexandria.”

“It means weather,” said the water-seller, who wanted his cup back.

Someone further along was explaining it to a crowd, and Nysa heard, “They say the hair of the star means…” and then a cheer went up from inside the walls and drowned the rest. She never learned what the hair meant. She thought about it afterwards, scrubbing pans, and decided she had been better off without the end of the sentence.

On the second evening she was alone on the kitchen step when it came, with nobody near to explain it, and she looked up at it and had the feeling she used to have in the Domus when the master came into a room behind her without a sound: that she was being looked at, and had better have her hands busy. She knew it was a star. She said so to herself, the way the water-seller had said it. She went in and did not look at it again that night, and on the third evening she found she had gone out to the step at the hour without meaning to.

It came back every evening for seven evenings, at the same hour, in the same part of the sky. By the third evening the boy’s people had a song about it. By the fifth the song had a verse about the chair.

At the end of the games the steward found her in the scullery. “Well?”

She thought of the number she had counted twice. She thought of his face when he said *a great many*. She thought of the Domus, where the people who carried bad numbers into a room were the ones the room remembered afterwards.

“Some thousands,” she said. “The cheap rows, mostly. Fewer than turned out for the fullers’ guild at a funeral. The rest shouted for the horses.”

He let his breath go. “And the star?”

“Weather,” she said. “The water-seller said.”

That night, through the wall of the dining room, she heard the steward tell it, and heard Antony laugh. It was a big laugh, the kind he gave at dinner when the wine was good. She stood in the dark passage with a jug and listened to her own number go round the table and come back larger as a joke.

Later, when the kitchen was dark, one of Fulvia’s women came down with a lamp and stood in the scullery door. She was the one who counted silver at dawn. She did not sit.

“How many,” she said, “really?”

Nysa looked at the lamp and not at the woman. Then she told her the true number, the one she had counted twice.

The woman nodded once, as if it matched something she already had, and took the lamp back up the stair. Nysa stood in the dark kitchen a while. It was the first time anyone in that house had asked her for the true one.

# SC-68d: Andromache

POV: Brutus · Velia
Date / place: Late Aug 44 · a friend’s house above the harbour, the night before sailing
Canon refs: T13 · Plut. *Brut.* 23 · *Iliad* 6
Info ENTER: Brutus leaving Italy; Porcia to go back to Rome · EXIT: The parting; Brutus will not say Hector’s line
Overhear risk: Acilius; the household

---

The house they borrowed at Velia had a painting in the dining room, and Porcia stopped in front of it on the last evening and did not move.

It was an old painting, Greek work, the colours gone soft with lamp smoke. A man in armour on a city wall, his helmet in his hand. A woman holding out a child to him. The child was frightened of the plume and had turned its face into the nurse’s shoulder. Everybody who had ever had a tutor knew who they were. Hector, going out to fight. Andromache, who knew he would not come back.

Brutus saw his wife’s shoulders and knew she was crying, and did not go to her at once, because she would not have wanted to be seen being comforted. He waited by the door. In all the months of their marriage he had never seen her cry. Not at Lanuvium. Not on the morning of the Ides, when she had lain on the floor. Not when her leg was at its worst.

Acilius, whose house it was, came in behind him, saw, and did what a well-read man does when he does not know what to do. He quoted.

“*Hector, you are my father now, and my mother, and my brother*,” he said, softly, in Homer’s Greek. “*And my husband, young and strong.*”

Porcia turned round. Her face was wet and perfectly composed.

“Go on,” she said to Acilius. “The next part is Hector’s. Where he tells her to go back to the house and see to her loom and her maids, and leave the war to the men.” She looked at Brutus. “Go on. Somebody say it.”

Nobody said it. Acilius looked at his feet.

Brutus came into the room. He looked at the painting, at the man on the wall with his helmet in his hand, and thought of the barber’s knife, and the fever in her hand in March, and her voice on the gravel at Lanuvium telling him she had been on the floor while he was in the porch.

“I can’t give her Hector’s answer,” he said, to Acilius, because it was easier to say it to Acilius. “She hasn’t the body to carry a sword. That’s all. She has everything else.”

Porcia did not thank him. She never thanked him for true things. She nodded once, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a girl, and went to see that the baggage for Rome was properly corded.

In the morning she went north by road with her women, and he went down to the harbour, where Cassius was waiting to see him off and pretend he had not come to.

# SC-69: East

POV: Cassius (+ Brutus) · Velia harbour
Date / place: Late Aug 44 · Brutus’s ship, harbour at Velia
Canon refs: A9 · Chain 8 seed · C3
Info ENTER: Humiliation appointments · EXIT: Liberty brand + war-chest need; Brutus refuses one purity cost
Overhear risk: Ship crews

---

Cassius counted the ships at Velia from the quay, and then counted them again, because his eyes were poor at that distance and the second count had given him one more than the first. Brutus had six. His own were still fitting out down the coast. He had three so far and he wanted twelve.

He went aboard to see him off.

He had been doing another sum all the way from Antium, and he did it again on the gangplank. Decimus had been given the Cisalpine province and two legions. Trebonius had been given Asia. Cimber had Bithynia. He and Brutus, who had been praetors of Rome in March, had been given a list of grain ports and a letter of thanks. He had not been able to make it come out differently.

A harbour official wished them favourable gods and looked at the mooring rope while he said it.

Brutus was at the rail. Two gulls were disputing a scrap on the water that plainly belonged to neither. He watched them for some time.

“Even the birds have a lawsuit,” he said.

It was the only kind of joke he ever made. Cassius had heard perhaps six of them in twenty years, and he let this one stand.

One of the crew, coiling line, wanted to know whether it was true the praetor was bound for Athens, and whether Athens counted as running away.

“Athens,” Brutus said. “Men still read there. Somebody ought to.”

“We are going to collect what is owed,” said Cassius.

The man snorted and went back to his rope. Cassius preferred the snort. It was better than leaving *running away* lying on the deck.

He had Trebonius’s letter in his belt. It had come from Smyrna in June and he had read it more often than it deserved. The figs were as good as promised. The walls were better. The province paid its dues on time, which no province had ever done for Trebonius before, and he suspected a trick. *Dolabella is still in Italy, borrowing*, he wrote. *When he comes through I shall feed him and wave. Come and eat my figs on your way to wherever you are going. Bring Brutus. Do not let him bring a book.* Cassius had not answered it yet. Asia would be the first place they went for silver, and the first door he knocked at there would be a friend’s.

A factor came aboard from the quay. He was a Greek who bought and sold for three of the coast cities, and he had a tablet and a wish to be useful. The cities were ready to advance money now, he said, today, before the wind served. They asked only that the word *libertas* should stand on the receipts.

Cassius read the figure upside down. It would have kept a legion for a year. He put out his hand for the stylus.

“If we become what we killed,” Brutus said, and did not go on.

“We shall have that argument when we have won.”

Brutus took the tablet. He looked at it for a long time. Cassius watched him and could see exactly where he was in it: the sum was honest, and it was needed, and it would make the name a tax before it had been anything else.

“Not from this deck,” Brutus said. He gave the tablet back to the factor with both hands, as a thing of value. “The Senate sent me out of Rome to buy corn like a clerk. I will not turn clerk for cities that want a seal. When we are among them, we will ask.”

The factor bowed. He said to the mate, not as low as he supposed, “They will sell the same coin to whoever stamps first.”

“Then let them hurry,” Brutus said.

Cassius did not argue it. He needed that face on the cause, and the face came with the scruple, and he had known the price when he chose it. He embraced him. Brutus held on a moment longer than he did.

He went down the plank behind the factor. At the foot of it he asked the man, pleasantly, which house he kept at Smyrna, and whether he would be there in the autumn. The factor told him.

From the quay he watched the six ships stand out. He could not see the rail at that distance. Somebody there seemed to have put up an arm. He put up his own, in case it was Brutus.

# SC-68b: War of Words

POV: Cicero · Rome / pamphlet circulation
Date / place: Autumn 44 · desk, Curia fringe, street return
Canon refs: Chain 6 · C7 · B3
Info ENTER: Neutrality impossible · EXIT: Oratory war on Antony declared
Overhear risk: Public when delivered

---

By autumn Cicero had stopped pretending the middle existed.

On the desk lay two drafts. One called Antony an enemy of the state in periods that would carry to the back of the Forum; the other called him a man who had forgotten his manners. He chose the first. He worked at a single adjective until it could cut without looking eager, and failed, and left it eager.

There was ink on his fingers. He had not felt like this since his consulship, with Catiline in the city, and it was better than a summer of being polite on the coast.

A friend came to warn him, with the dust of the street still on his sandals. “You heard what he said in the Senate,” the friend said carefully. “Antony stood up with archers at the temple doors and named you the man who planned the Ides. Your chair was empty. Nobody in that room was brave enough to look at it.” He thought. “Except Calenus. Fufius Calenus, on the bench beside him, nodding at every line.”

“Calenus nods for a living,” Cicero said. “He will stand up and answer me for Antony the day I am there to be answered. He has a good voice and a better cellar, and he thinks they are the same thing. Antony spoke to my empty chair. I will answer from his. In every house in Rome that can read.”

The friend lifted the top draft off the desk and weighed it like a brick. “And what do you call this, when the copyists ask?”

“Demosthenes had Philip of Macedon at the gates and talked Athens into fighting him. *Philippics*, he called them.” Cicero took the sheet back. “Let Antony be my Philip. The copyists may have the title for nothing.” He had meant it as a joke once, at a quieter desk, before March. He liked it too much now to keep it one.

The friend did not laugh. “Athens lost, as I remember. And Demosthenes ended drinking poison in a temple with the Macedonians at the door.”

“Athens listened late,” Cicero said. “I intend to be early.”

“Veterans in the streets,” the friend said. “They drink Antony’s name with their pay. Your periods will not outrun a javelin if you make yourself the loudest man in Rome.”

“The Republic still has a voice,” Cicero said.

“The Republic has your voice. That is not the same as legions.”

“Then it will have to be loud,” Cicero said. He waved the rest of the caution away with a gesture practised on juries.

The boy could be pointed at Antony. He need not be loved for that. He drafted a period about Antony’s appetite and another about the state’s dignity. Then he found the sentence that would join them, and it was a good one.

A courier waited with copies for men who would pretend they had not asked for copies. Cicero sealed the packets himself and sent them out.

One packet came back before dusk.

The seal was broken. The wax had been thumb-smeared and pressed again badly: an insult, not a forgery. Across the top margin someone had scored a short line with a knife-point, deep enough to scar the wood beneath: no sentence, only the mark. A smear of something that might have been wine or might have been worse.

The friend, still in the atrium, went pale and said nothing.

Cicero held the scarred tablet in both hands until they were steady. He thought of what Atticus would say, and of the Capitol on the afternoon of the Ides, which was a better day each time he described it. Then he ordered fresh copies. “Circulate it wider,” he said. “If they can mark one, let them learn there are many.”
