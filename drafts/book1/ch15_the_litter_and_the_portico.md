# SC-40: Sour Birds

POV: Calpurnia
Date / place: Morning 15 March 44 · Domus Publica after litter departs
Canon refs: M2 · M4 · Suet. 81 · DIO-OMEN-1
Info ENTER: DREAM-1 spent; Caesar gone with Decimus
Info EXIT: Stay lost; bad sacrifice talk floods the house; she waits without power
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

In the Domus Publica vestibule the litter’s smell still hung: wool, leather, the oil they rubbed into poles so the wood would not raise blisters on other men’s shoulders. Calpurnia stood in it like a woman who had missed a boat by the width of a hand.

Phileros talked too much, which meant he was afraid. “The chickens were sullen, domina. The boy said so. And the *exta*, someone said the liver looked insulted.”

“Someone,” Calpurnia said.

“A man from the college. Or a man who knows a man from the college.” Phileros lifted his shoulders. “They always look insulted if you want them to.”

“Do they?”

“They do if the man holding the knife has had a bad night, domina. Livers are very sensitive to character.”

“And yours?”

“Mine is a servant’s liver,” said Phileros. “It has no opinions.”

Nysa passed with an armful of yesterday’s tablets, eyes down. Calpurnia had seen what the girl tried to do at the door.

“Stop,” Calpurnia said. “What was it, at the vestibule?”

Nysa’s arms tightened on the tablets. “Something I heard, mistress. Not all of it. Phileros wouldn’t let me near.”

“Bring me what you have.”

“I burned it,” Nysa said. Her hand went once to her ribs and came away empty. “It was five words. They did not go together.”

Calpurnia looked at her for a long moment. She did not search the tunic. She had had enough of the morning already. “Then leave omens to the priests. If Phileros sells chickens as prophecy again, send him to me by name, not someone.”

Phileros, still hovering in the vestibule, heard his name and looked hurt.

In the service court Dama swore the chickens were still sullen and that sullenness meant knives. A washerwoman from the next court leaned on the doorpost with the Forum’s news of the morning: Spurinna again, and a bird. “A wren,” she said. “The little one in the story that rode the eagle’s back and stole the crown. Flew into Pompey’s hall this morning with a sprig of laurel in its beak, and the other birds came after it and tore it to pieces on the step. Before the Senate even sat.” Pallas arrived with Calpurnia’s mantle.

“Enough,” Calpurnia said. Three mouths closed at different speeds. “Chickens are breakfast. Spurinna sells beware by the hour. I will not have this house invent his return out of birds.”

“He does not sell it by the hour,” the washerwoman said, wounded. “He gave it away free. That is why it spread. And the wren was this morning. I saw the mouths that sold it.”

“Things given free are always believed,” Calpurnia said.

Dama looked cheated of prophecy. The washerwoman looked cheated of a sale. Pallas held the mantle and said nothing.

Phileros tried again from the vestibule edge, softer. “Domina, if the college sends…”

“If the college sends, they send to me by name,” Calpurnia said. “Not through your someone. And Nysa, if you hear anything more, you bring it to me before you burn it.”

Nysa nodded once, too quickly. Calpurnia let the quickness go.

Then Calpurnia walked to the cubiculum and closed the door that had opened itself in the night. The latch was ordinary.

“He will come back,” she told the empty bed, and hated that she had said it aloud. She sat down on the bed. She could still see Decimus’s chin tip toward the door, the little road habit that had always meant her husband would go with him.

From the street came the ordinary noise of a working day. The litter was already gone.

# SC-41: Among Other Papers

POV: Decimus
Date / place: Litter route Domus Publica → Campus Martius / Curia Pompeia approach
Canon refs: A5 · M5 · NOTE-1 (Artemidorus-tradition) · MAP-1 travel 20–35 min
Info ENTER: Caesar in litter; attendance secured
Info EXIT: Delivered to Senate threshold; unread warning among papers; Decimus peels toward role
Overhear risk: Medium (crowd; note-hander)

---

The litter swayed. Caesar sat as he sat in triumphs and in mud, half present, half already in the next room. Decimus walked beside the near pole when the street narrowed, close enough to speak without shouting.

“Short session,” he said again, because saying it steadied him. “You dismiss them if the birds offend you. You will have been seen. That is what matters.”

“Seen by whom?” Caesar said.

“By everyone who is currently arguing that you have become a god and a recluse in the same week.”

“I have been both for years. They never complain when I am late.”

Caesar made a small sound that might have been assent. His hands rested on a stack of tablets and rolls that people had been pushing at him since the vestibule, petitions mostly. Decimus didn’t look at the stack for long. He had a knife under his toga, and he was sweating already.

Near the turning toward the Campus, a man pushed through the crowd to the litter, a Greek, sweating and in a hurry. Decimus placed him at once: one of the Greek teachers every great house kept for its sons, and this one had the look of a man who had come by a friend’s secret and could not carry it. He pushed a small sealed tablet toward Caesar’s hands.

“Read this,” the man said, or something near it. “Read it now. Alone.”

Caesar took it. Of course he took it. He took everything. Taking was quicker than arguing in a crowd. For a breath the tablet lay in his hand with its seal unbroken. Decimus’s hand twitched toward the stack before his face did. Snatching would look like theft. Letting it ride would look like ease.

The man’s eyes found Decimus’s twitch. “Not you,” the man said, too fast. “Him. Alone.”

“He is a dictator,” Decimus said pleasantly, “not a confessional. Give it to his secretary.”

“It is for him,” the man said. “It matters what hand it is read in.”

Caesar’s thumb found the edge of the seal. Then he put the tablet into the stack with the others, and the litter moved on, and it was under the petitions.

Decimus felt cold under the ribs. He didn’t know what the tablet said. He knew that his hand had moved toward it, and that Caesar had not noticed, and that he himself would remember it.

“Friends everywhere,” Decimus said lightly. “Even the street writes to you.”

“The street writes to everyone,” said Caesar. “It is only a matter of whether they write back.”

At Pompey’s portico it was cooler, in the shade of the columns. The Senate met here while its own house was being rebuilt, and Pompey’s statue stood inside. Senators stood about in twos and threes, as if they had all happened to arrive early. Decimus saw Casca’s shoulder, Tillius with his petition ready, Cassius standing too still. Brutus stood apart and did not look at anyone.

Antony’s laugh carried from farther along the portico, big, soldierly, not yet afraid. Trebonius was already working his way toward it.

Decimus helped Caesar down. Their hands met once more. Caesar’s palm was dry.

“Go in,” Decimus said, and smiled the smile that had opened Spanish gates. “I will see to the edges.”

He didn’t go in at once. The edges meant Antony, and Antony had to be kept out here in the colonnade until it was done.

Caesar went into Pompey’s Curia among the men who owed him. Decimus felt sick. In three days Caesar would have left for Parthia, and there would have been no other morning.

Decimus wiped his clean hands on his tunic, once, where no one would see, and went to find Trebonius’s eye.

# SC-42: Hold Him with Talk

POV: Antony
Date / place: Porticus of Pompey · morning 15 March 44
Canon refs: M6 · CHOREO-1.8 · Trebonius
Info ENTER: Consul arriving for Senate business
Info EXIT: Held in conversation by Trebonius; hears disturbance late
Overhear risk: Medium

---

Antony liked the Theatre of Pompey complex the way soldiers liked any place with shade and a crowd to read. Columns made people smaller. Smaller people told the truth faster, or lied worse; either was useful.

He arrived with the loose stride of a man who had drunk enough last night to prove he was alive and not enough to prove he was soft. The Ides were a workday. Caesar had called them. Caesar would talk ships and provinces and Parthia. Antony would agree where agreement bought him quiet and argue where argument bought him men.

Trebonius found him before he reached the Curia door.

“Marcus,” Trebonius said, warm as old campaign wine. The first name; only old friends and men with business used it. “A word. Not even a word. Three. You’ll thank me for keeping you from the first hour’s boredom.”

Antony grinned. “If it’s market day, send a tribune. If it’s money, speak.”

“It’s a man in my debt who swears he has news from Gaul that won’t keep.” Trebonius’s hand on Antony’s elbow was friendly. Antony allowed himself to be steered a few paces into the corner of the portico, where the Senate’s murmur was only a noise.

“A man in your debt who has news from Gaul,” Antony said.

“I have several. It is a great sorrow to me.”

“How do you get men in your debt and keep your fingers?”

“I never lend them anything they can spend,” said Trebonius.

A legate’s attendant tried to cut in with remounts and a mistress joke already half-told. Trebonius waved him off. “Later. Consuls first.” The attendant shut his mouth and stepped back. Antony laughed at that, and found the laugh went on a little too long.

They spoke of nothing that mattered: a cavalry remount, whether Hirtius wrote better than he fought. Trebonius was good at market days.

“Hirtius fights badly,” Antony said. “I have seen it. He holds a sword like a pen.”

“And writes like a general,” said Trebonius. “It is the one mercy of his career.”

For a moment he had the feeling a soldier has when a door has shut somewhere behind him. He glanced toward the Curia.

“Caesar’s gone in,” Antony said.

“Caesar’s always gone in,” Trebonius answered. “That’s why we have to catch our business in the colonnade like tradesmen.”

He let it go. Antony was consul. Consuls were not dragged by the sleeve unless they chose to be. He chose, for three more breaths, to be polite to a colleague.

Then the sound changed.

It was not speaking and it was not voting. It was men shouting over one another, and benches going over, and something like a scream that stopped, and then a great many feet.

Antony’s body understood before his politics did. He shook Trebonius’s hand off without thinking it rude.

“What in…”

Trebonius’s face was still friendly. Men were pouring out of the Curia, togas pulled loose, heads bare, their eyes too white.

“Caesar,” someone yelled, and then the name became a dozen other words: *blood*, *knives*, *Brutus*, *liberty*, *murder*, *the consul*, *where is the consul*.

Antony felt for a weapon he had not brought; no consul carried iron to a Senate sitting. His palm found only the cloth of his tunic.

Trebonius said something mild. Antony didn’t keep it.

Antony ran for the door, and the crowd ran with him and against him.

At the door Antony looked back once. Trebonius stood alone in the corner of the colonnade, and the warm face had come off him like a cloak. Antony would remember that face longer than the noise.
