# SC-40: Sour Birds

POV: Calpurnia
Date / place: Morning 15 March 44 · Domus Publica after litter departs
Canon refs: M2 · M4 · Suet. 81 · DIO-OMEN-1
Info ENTER: DREAM-1 spent; Caesar gone with Decimus
Info EXIT: Stay lost; bad sacrifice talk floods the house; she waits without power
Overhear risk: Extreme

---

In the Domus Publica vestibule the litter’s smell still hung: wool, leather, the oil they rubbed into poles so the wood would not raise blisters on other men’s shoulders. Calpurnia stood in it like a woman who had missed a boat by the width of a hand.

Phileros talked too much, which meant he was afraid. “The chickens were sullen, domina. The boy said so. And the exta, someone said the liver looked insulted.”

“Someone,” Calpurnia said.

“A man from the college. Or a man who knows a man from the college.” Phileros’s shoulders performed helplessness. “They always look insulted if you want them to.”

“Do they?”

“They do if the man holding the knife has had a bad night, domina. Livers are very sensitive to character.”

“And yours?”

“Mine is a servant’s liver,” said Phileros. “It has no opinions.”

Nysa passed with an armful of yesterday’s tablets, eyes down. Calpurnia almost asked what the girl had tried to do at the door, then did.

“Stop,” Calpurnia said. “What did you try at the vestibule.”

Nysa’s arms tightened on the tablets. “A warning. Incomplete. Phileros wouldn’t let it through.”

“Bring me what you have.”

“I burned it,” Nysa said. Her hand went once to her ribs and came away empty. “I kept the habit.” Her eyes flicked once toward the street noise, then down again. “It wasn’t Senate shape. It was splinters.”

Calpurnia held the girl’s gaze until inventing an answer would have cost more than silence. She did not search the tunic. Searching would have made the lie into a trial, and the house already had enough theatre. “Then stay off omen theatre. If Phileros sells chickens as prophecy again, send him to me by name, not someone.”

Phileros, still in the vestibule orbit, heard his name and looked insulted on cue. Calpurnia did not soften it.

She did not go to the cubiculum yet. The house wanted her to become furniture that waited. She refused that shape for one more room of air.

In the service court Dama swore the chickens were still sullen and that sullenness meant knives. A washerwoman from the next court leaned on the doorpost with Forum merchandise fresh that morning: Spurinna, beware, and a king-bird. “A wren,” she said. “The little one in the story that rode the eagle’s back and stole the crown. Flew into Pompey’s hall this morning with a sprig of laurel in its beak, and the other birds came after it and tore it to pieces on the step. Before the Senate even sat.” Pallas arrived with Calpurnia’s mantle. Dressing would not stop a morning.

“Enough,” Calpurnia said. Three mouths closed at different speeds. “Chickens are breakfast. Spurinna sells beware by the hour. I will not have this house invent his return out of birds.”

“He does not sell it by the hour,” the washerwoman said, wounded. “He gave it away free. That is why it spread. And the wren was this morning. I saw the mouths that sold it.”

“Things given free always spread,” Calpurnia said. “It is the reason nobody gives away anything good.”

Dama looked cheated of prophecy. The washerwoman looked cheated of a sale. Pallas held the mantle and said nothing, loyalty that knew when silence was the only clean service.

Phileros tried again from the vestibule edge, softer. “Domina, if the college sends…”

“If the college sends, they send to me by name,” Calpurnia said. “Not through your someone. And Nysa, if another splinter arrives at the door, you bring it to me before you burn it. Incomplete is still a shape.”

Nysa nodded once, too quickly. Calpurnia let the quickness go.

Only then did Calpurnia walk to the cubiculum and close the door that had opened itself in the night. The latch was ordinary. Ordinary things had begun to feel like insults.

“He will come back,” she told the empty bed, and hated that she had said it aloud. Speech could not hire a return. She did not invent the sound that would prove her wrong. She sat with the order she had given, and with Decimus’s chin-tip still bright behind her eyes: the little road habit that had always meant the friend would go. She practised the silence she would keep when the house tried again to make omen into conversation.

The bed did not argue. From the street rose the ordinary roar of the Ides. The litter was already gone. Refusing theatre until news arrived with a body attached was all the power left.

# SC-41: Among Other Papers

POV: Decimus
Date / place: Litter route Domus Publica → Campus Martius / Curia Pompeia approach
Canon refs: A5 · M5 · NOTE-1 (Artemidorus-tradition) · MAP-1 travel 20–35 min
Info ENTER: Caesar in litter; attendance secured
Info EXIT: Delivered to Senate threshold; unread warning among papers; Decimus peels toward role
Overhear risk: Medium (crowd; note-hander)

---

The litter swayed. Caesar sat as he sat in triumphs and in mud, half present, half already in the next room. Decimus walked beside the near pole when the street narrowed, close enough to speak without shouting.

“Short session,” he said again, because repetition steadied him like a hand on a litter pole. “You dismiss them if the birds offend you. You will have been seen. That is what matters.”

“Seen by whom?” Caesar said.

“By everyone who is currently arguing that you have become a god and a recluse in the same week.”

“I have been both for years. They never complain when I am late.”

Caesar made a small sound that might have been assent. His hands rested on a stack of tablets and rolls someone had thrust in at the vestibule: petitions, lists, the city’s endless paper hunger. A Roman of his rank was petitioned in the street the way a river is fished: constantly, hopefully, by men who had nothing to lose by throwing a line. Decimus didn’t look too long at the stack. Men with knives under togas who stared at paper started sweating through wool.

Near the turning toward the Campus, a man pressed in, Greek face, urgent manners, the particular sweat of someone who believed information could still alter a morning. Decimus placed him at once: one of the Greek teachers every great house kept for its sons, and this one had the look of a man who had come by a friend’s secret and could not carry it. He pushed a small sealed tablet toward Caesar’s hands.

“Read this,” the man said, or something near it. “Read it now. Alone.”

Caesar took it. Of course he took it. He took everything. Taking was cheaper than arguing in a crowd. For a breath the seal sat in the open, wax dull, string tight, the particular weight of a message that wanted to be a door. Decimus’s hand twitched toward the stack before his face did. Snatching would look like theft. Letting it ride would look like ease. Ease was the costume he was wearing.

The man’s eyes found Decimus’s twitch. “Not you,” the man said, too fast. “Him. Alone.”

“He is a dictator,” Decimus said pleasantly, “not a confessional. Give it to his secretary.”

“It is for him,” the man said. “It matters what hand it is read in.”

Caesar’s thumb brushed the seal. Curiosity almost woke. Then he slid the tablet into the stack with the others, among other papers, and the litter moved on because litters moved on. The seal vanished under petitions and lists. Unread became ordinary again. Ordinary was how mornings killed.

Decimus felt a cold stitch under the ribs. He didn’t know what the tablet said. He knew only that something sealed had nearly cost him his face in front of the man whose trust he was currently wearing like a clean cloak. The almost-snatch would live longer in his wrist than in Caesar’s attention.

“Friends everywhere,” Decimus said lightly. “Even the street writes to you.”

“The street writes to everyone,” said Caesar. “It is only a matter of whether they write back.”

Caesar did not open it. The unread paper rode against his knee like any other chore.

At the portico of Pompey the air changed. Shade, columns, the smell of stone that had watched other men’s ambitions. The Senate sat here while their own house stayed unfinished; Pompey’s statue waited inside. Senators clustered in the false casualness of men who had arrived early on purpose. Decimus saw Casca’s shoulder, Tillius’s mouth already shaped for petition, Cassius too still. Brutus stood carved for a different century.

Antony’s laugh carried from farther along the portico, big, soldierly, not yet afraid. Trebonius was already angling toward that laugh with the patience of a door.

Decimus helped Caesar down. Their hands met once more. Caesar’s palm was dry.

“Go in,” Decimus said, and smiled the smile that had opened Spanish gates. “I will see to the edges.”

He didn’t go in at once. Edges meant Antony. Edges meant keeping the consul’s body in the colonnade while the room inside did what rooms did when enough men had decided.

Caesar entered the Curia of Pompey among men who loved him, hated him, owed him. Decimus knew with a clarity that felt like nausea: they needed him stopped before the Parthian clocks started again.

Decimus wiped his clean hands on his tunic, once, where no one would see, and went to find Trebonius’s eye.

# SC-42: Hold Him with Talk

POV: Antony
Date / place: Porticus of Pompey · morning 15 March 44
Canon refs: M6 · CHOREO-1.8 · Trebonius
Info ENTER: Consul arriving for Senate business
Info EXIT: Held in conversation by Trebonius; hears disturbance late
Overhear risk: Medium

---

Antony liked the Theatre of Pompey complex the way soldiers liked any place with shade and a crowd to read. Columns made people smaller. Smaller people told the truth faster, or lied worse; either was useful.

He arrived with the loose stride of a man who had drunk enough last night to prove he was alive and not enough to prove he was soft. The Ides were a workday. Caesar had called them. Caesar would talk ships and provinces and the endless eastern itch. Antony would agree where agreement bought him quiet and argue where argument bought him men.

Trebonius found him before he reached the Curia door.

“Marcus,” Trebonius said, warm as old campaign wine. The first name; only old friends and men with business used it. “A word. Not even a word. Three. You’ll thank me for keeping you from the first hour’s boredom. Think of it as another market day: Gaul talk, debt talk, the long nothing that keeps consuls useful.”

Antony grinned. “If it’s market day, send a tribune. If it’s money, speak.”

“It’s a man in my debt who swears he has news from Gaul that won’t keep.” Trebonius’s hand on Antony’s elbow was friendly. Friendly hands steered. Antony allowed himself to be steered a few paces into the portico’s shoulder, where the Senate’s murmur became a wall.

“You have a man in my debt who has news from Gaul,” Antony said.

“I have several. It is a great sorrow to me.”

“How do you get men in your debt and keep your fingers?”

“I never lend them anything they can spend,” said Trebonius.

A legate’s attendant tried to cut in with remounts and a mistress joke already half-told. Trebonius waved him off with the same warmth. “Later. Consuls first.” The attendant swallowed the remount and stepped back. Antony laughed at that, then found the laugh lasting a little too long. The morning wanted him facing the wrong way.

They spoke of nothing that mattered: a cavalry remount, whether Hirtius wrote better than he fought. Trebonius was good at market days. That was the point.

“Hirtius fights badly,” Antony said. “I have seen it. He holds a sword like a pen.”

“And writes like a general,” said Trebonius. “It is the one mercy of his career.”

A thin suspicion crossed him, not plot, not dagger, only the soldier’s sense that a door had been closed behind his back. He glanced toward the Curia.

“Caesar’s gone in,” Antony said.

“Caesar’s always gone in,” Trebonius answered. “That’s why we have to catch our business in the colonnade like tradesmen.”

The suspicion sat down and became patience. Antony was consul. Consuls were not dragged by the sleeve unless they chose to be. He chose, for three more breaths, to be polite to a colleague.

Then the sound changed.

Not a speech-sound. Not a vote-sound. A human noise wrong in the throat, shout stacked on shout, the scrape of benches, something like a scream cut short and replaced by many feet.

Antony’s body understood before his politics did. He shook Trebonius’s hand off without thinking it rude.

“What in…”

Trebonius’s face was still warm. Warm, while the Curia poured men out like a broken jar. Togas, bare heads, eyes too white.

“Caesar,” someone yelled, and then the name became a dozen other words: *blood*, *knives*, *Brutus*, *liberty*, *murder*, *the consul*, *where is the consul*.

Antony felt for a weapon he had not brought; no consul carried iron to a Senate sitting. His palm found only the cloth of his tunic.

Trebonius said something mild. Antony didn’t keep it. The mildness sat wrong, warmth while the Curia broke open.

Antony ran toward the door, not toward philosophy, and the crowd ran with him and against him, a single animal with too many mouths.

At the door Antony looked back once. Trebonius stood alone in the colonnade’s shoulder, and the warm face had come off him like a cloak. Market day had done its job. Antony would remember that face longer than the noise.
