# SC-60: Petty News

POV: Cicero · Campania villas
Date / place: Fundi / Matius orbit · letter desk
Canon refs: *Att.* 14.1–4 · Chain 4 residual · D3
Info ENTER: Joy at tyrannicide · EXIT: Needs forces/money; hungers for daily scraps
Overhear risk: Letter couriers

---

Cicero wrote as other men breathed, until the room filled and writing had to wait on mouths.

The tablet under his palm was still warm from the last courier’s bag when a second courier arrived dusty, and a neighbour with a taste for Athens arrived thirsty. Fundi’s shade became a small Forum with worse wine. Wax took fingerprints. Cicero liked that: proof that news had a body before it became opinion. Bodies arguing in one room beat stacked tablets for truth’s temperature.

“You came south early this year,” the neighbour said, as if a villa were a confession.

“The coast road was dry,” Cicero said. It had also been the road away from Antony’s Forum, where a former consul could walk out in the morning and not be sure of walking home. Formiae next, then Puteoli. He meant to call it a season.

“If they have no money and no men,” Cicero told the neighbour, “they have a beautiful murder and a short future.”

The neighbour smiled as if force were a vulgar digression. “Surely the Senate…”

“The Senate is a throat,” Cicero said, and hated the sentence as soon as it left him. “Throats need fists behind them, or they only make sounds.”

He hated that he sounded like Cassius. He hated more that Cassius might be right.

The first courier, sandals cracked, spoke over the neighbour without apology. “Antony’s latest posting: seal looked tired. Theatre, not industry.”

“Another law out of Caesar’s papers?” Cicero asked.

“Cut in bronze and up on the Capitol by noon. The men reading it say Caesar never wrote it. The men who paid for it say he did.” The courier shrugged. “The amnesty says what Caesar wrote stands. So Antony keeps finding things Caesar wrote.”

Cicero had stopped guessing which were real and started counting.

“Baker-rumour says the gardens aren’t walked yet but already sold in talk. And in three wine shops near the Circus, old men are using the boy as sport.”

“Which boy?” the neighbour asked, pleased to be scandalised.

“Octavius. Caesar’s paper-son. They call him a hopeful shadow, a squeak, a name that hasn’t learned to pay. One elder swore he’d last until the first unpaid veteran. Another bet a cup he’d be Caesar by Kalends if Campania kept saying it.”

“And where is he,” Cicero asked, “while they bet on him?”

“Off the Apollonia boat at Brundisium. Caesar’s old soldiers walked out on the road to look at him. Now he’s making for his stepfather’s villa on the bay, Philippus’s, near Naples.”

Puteoli was on the same bay. Cicero did not enjoy the arithmetic.

Cicero’s mouth arranged courtesy. Under it he filed *not yet money / already joke*. “Did anyone in the Forum say the name as if it cleared?”

The courier shrugged. “As if it amused. Not as if it cleared.”

He tipped the cracked-sandal boy, scratched *not yet* in a margin, and underlined it twice.

Sphaerus, Hirtius’s secretary, brought a note from his master, polite, useless.

“He asks after your health, Cicero.”

“Tell him it is excellent,” Cicero said, “and that it intends to stay out of Rome.”

Cicero answered later. First he let the room empty. When it had, he stacked finished letters by the door until the pile reached his knee. Then he wrote Atticus a softer version of *the deed was fair; the result is nothing*. Softness kept invitations open. Under it the wine-shop sport still sat unpaid.

# SC-60a: Market Day

POV: Cassius · the Appian Way below Lanuvium
Date / place: Mid-April 44 · a posting station before dawn
Canon refs: T10 · *Att.* 14.10 · Ch 7 (Trebonius’s market day)
Info ENTER: The Liberators shut out of Rome · EXIT: Trebonius gone east to Asia, quietly
Overhear risk: Grooms; a mule driver

---

Trebonius left Rome the way a debtor leaves a lodging house: before light, by the side door, with his best cloak on so that nobody would think he was leaving.

Cassius rode up from his own house to see him off because a note had come saying not to. The posting station sat where the road came down off the hills, a low yard with a well and a lamp and a groom asleep on a pile of harness. Trebonius was in the yard already, in a travelling hat, watching his baggage go on to the mules with the face of a man counting spoons.

“You were told not to come,” Trebonius said.

“That’s why I came.”

“Of course it is.” Trebonius looked pleased, and hid it, and let it show again, because hiding things from Cassius had never been worth the work. “Asia. It’s mine for the year, by the dead man’s own arrangement. If I wait in Rome for the Senate to send me off with a sacrifice, Antony will find a reason to keep me there, and then a reason to forget me there. So I’m going to go and be a proconsul quietly, before anyone remembers to object.”

It was the voice from the old days, before any of it, the voice that had walked beside Cassius through the Forum on market days talking about the price of wheat and who was sleeping with whom and which senator had been seen buying his own applause. Trebonius had been Caesar’s man for ten years. He had held Massilia for him and governed Spain for him and taken the consul’s chair from him for three months. And on the Ides he had stood in the porch of Pompey’s theatre with his hand on Antony’s sleeve, talking about nothing, while the others went in.

Cassius had never asked him what they talked about. He asked now.

“The weather,” Trebonius said. “His mother’s health. A horse he wanted to buy off Lepidus.” He tightened a strap that did not need it. “He’s an easy man to talk to, Antony. That’s the trouble with him. I kept thinking he would ask me what I was doing, and he never did. He thought I was being friendly.”

“You were.”

“I was.” Trebonius stopped with the strap in his hand. “That’s the part I think about.”

The groom woke and began shouting at the mules. Down the road in the dark a cart went by toward Rome, loaded with something that clanked, and the driver sang to himself in a Marsian accent.

“Dolabella,” Cassius said. “When he gets Syria, he’ll go out by your road.”

“Dolabella is twenty-five and in debt to everyone in Italy and he has never been in anything he couldn’t talk his way out of.” Trebonius smiled. “I know Dolabella. I was at his wedding. He kissed the bride’s father on both cheeks and borrowed money from him in the same embrace. He’ll be in Asia in the autumn, if he goes at all, and I will give him a good dinner and wave him on into Syria and he will forget me before he reaches the hills.”

“And if he doesn’t?”

“Then I’ll have walls.” Trebonius took the reins the groom held out. “Smyrna has good walls. I’ve seen them. Friendly town. Good figs.”

He mounted. He was not a natural horseman, and the mule driver watched him do it and decided not to laugh. From the saddle Trebonius looked down at Cassius in the first grey light, and for a moment the market-day face went away and something older and more tired came up under it.

“You were right, you know,” he said. “Back in the winter. About Antony. We should have done him too.”

Cassius said nothing. There was nothing to say to it that was not either a boast or a grief.

“Well.” Trebonius gathered the reins. “Too late for that. I’ll write.”

He went off down the road toward Brundisium in the half dark, with his mules and his spoons and his best cloak, and Cassius stood in the yard until he could not hear the hooves any more, and then rode home and told no one he had been out.

# SC-60b: The Column

POV: F1 Nysa · the Forum
Date / place: Mid → late April 44 · the pyre site; the Sacred Way; the Domus Publica door
Canon refs: T10 · T1 · App. *BC* 3.2–3 · Cic. *Phil.* 1.5, *Att.* 14.15 · Suet. *Jul.* 85
Info ENTER: The funeral pyre (Ch 19); the house emptying · EXIT: The false Marius executed; Dolabella pulls down the column
Overhear risk: The Forum crowd

---

They built the altar where the fire had been.

Nysa went to it the first time because she was sent. The steward wanted oil from a shop on the far side of the Forum, and the way there went past the place, and there was no other way that did not cost an hour. She had meant to walk past. She stopped. Everyone stopped. There was ash still in the cracks of the paving where they had burned him, black under the new white stone, and a crowd of freedmen and shopkeepers and soldiers’ women standing round it with their hands full.

People brought things: bread, honey cakes, little jars of oil no bigger than a thumb. A soldier with one arm laid a bronze arm-ring on the step and walked away without looking at it. A woman Nysa knew from the Subura fountain, who sold thread and had never in her life been inside a house on the Palatine, knelt and put her forehead to the stone and stayed there. Somebody had written on the base in charcoal, then somebody else had cut it properly into the marble: *To the father of his country.*

A man stood by the altar most days. He called himself Marius, the grandson of the great Marius, who had been Caesar’s uncle by marriage. He had a fine voice and a good beard and a story about his descent that grew longer every time Nysa heard it. He led the prayers. He took the offerings in at the end of the day for safekeeping. The crowd loved him, because he loved Caesar louder than anyone, and because he said the names of the men of the Ides out loud with a curse after each one, in the open Forum, where the Senate could hear.

Nysa went again the next day, without being sent. She took nothing. She stood at the back of the crowd and looked at the stone and thought about the morning she had carried Calpurnia’s water in the grey and the master had gone out of the door, and the tablet in her tunic that was ash now. She did not pray. She did not know who she would have prayed to. It was only that the place was there, and she had nowhere else to stand that was his.

On the fifth day the altar had a column.

It went up in a night. Numidian stone, yellow and red, twenty feet of it, and the same words on the base, cut deeper. People said Marius had paid for it. People said the guilds had. By noon there were three times as many in the crowd, and they were swearing oaths on the column, and settling quarrels at its foot by swearing on Caesar’s name, and the Forum had a new court that no praetor sat in.

Antony’s soldiers came for Marius at dawn on the seventh day.

Nysa did not see it. She heard it, carrying water at the Domus gate with Dama: the noise first, a long roar from the Forum like a wave going over, and then a silence, and then the noise again, different. Phileros came back from the street an hour later grey in the face. Marius was dead. Killed in the prison under the Capitol, without a trial, on the consul’s order. The crowd had tried to rush the soldiers. Some of them were dead too. Some had been taken.

“Slaves on crosses,” Phileros said. “Free men off the Rock. I saw them take the thread woman’s son.” He sat down on the step of the gate. “For praying.”

“For praying loudly,” Dama said.

Nysa went in and set the water down and stood in the empty atrium where the master’s chair had been. She found she was holding the rim of the jar so hard her fingers had gone white. She let go of it one finger at a time.

The column stood ten more days.

People still came, fewer, quieter, with nothing in their hands. The soldiers watched them from the steps of the temple and did nothing, and that was worse. Then Antony went south into Campania to see the veterans, and the other consul had the city to himself.

Dolabella came at noon, with lictors and a gang of workmen with ropes and crowbars, and he came in person, which nobody had expected. He was young for a consul, younger than any man Nysa had ever seen behind the rods, with a handsome high-coloured face and the air of a man who has just been given a good part in a play. He stood on the temple steps while the workmen put their ropes round the column, and he made a short speech about order and the law and false gods, which the crowd did not hear because they were shouting.

The column came down at the third pull. It broke in two places. The yellow stone was white inside.

Then they broke the altar, with hammers, and threw the pieces in carts, and swept the paving where it had stood. Nysa watched the brooms go over the cracks where the ash was. They did not get it all. Some of it was too far down.

The men who fought the workmen were taken to the Rock that afternoon, and the slaves to the crosses outside the Esquiline gate, the same as before. This time the Forum did not roar. It watched. Dolabella went home through it with his lictors and men stood aside for the rods and did not look at his face, and by evening people were saying that Cicero would be pleased.

Nysa went back to the place at dusk, when the carts had gone. The paving was swept. You could not tell anything had stood there except by a paler square where the altar had kept the sun off for three weeks. She stood on it. Nobody else was there.

She had nothing in her hands. She stood a while anyway, until the lamps came on along the Sacred Way and the steward would be asking where his oil was, and then she went and bought the oil.

# SC-60c: Wonderful

POV: Cicero · Puteoli
Date / place: 1 May 44 · letter desk
Canon refs: T10 · *Att.* 14.15 · Tullia (d. Feb 45)
Info ENTER: News of the column · EXIT: “O my wonderful Dolabella”
Overhear risk: Tiro

---

The news of the column reached Puteoli on the last day of April, and Cicero read it twice in the garden and once more at the desk, with the sea going on below the window as if nothing had happened anywhere.

*O my wonderful Dolabella*, he wrote to Atticus. *I call him mine now. Before, believe me, I had my doubts.* The Rock, the crosses, the column down, the paving swept: a consul doing at last what a consul was for. He wrote that the boy, meaning Dolabella, not the other one, had done more for the Republic in one afternoon than all of them since the Ides. He wrote that it seemed to him the tyrant was really dead now, for the first time.

“He still owes you the dowry,” Tiro said, from the door.

Cicero did not look up. Dolabella had married Tullia when she was twenty-eight and still hopeful, and been unfaithful to her before the wedding wreaths were dry, and divorced her in the end when she was carrying his child. She had died of the child at Tusculum in the February before last. Cicero had built a shrine for her in his head and never built it in stone. The dowry had been paid back in two instalments of three, and the third was overdue, and Dolabella wrote charming letters about it.

“He was her husband,” Cicero said. “He is the consul. He pulled down the column. Those are three different facts, Tiro, and I am only writing about one of them.”

Tiro went away. Cicero finished the letter, and read it over, and liked it, and sent a copy of the best paragraph to Dolabella himself, with warm congratulations, because it was always well to be generous to a consul.

Then he sat a while with the sea going on below him, and thought about Tullia in her wedding veil, laughing at something the young man said, and found he could not remember what it was.

# SC-61: Cage with a View

POV: Brutus · Lanuvium
Date / place: Garden / study · A9 seed
Canon refs: *Att.* 15.1 flavour · C2
Info ENTER: Hero brand · EXIT: Cannot safely walk Rome; immortality vs peace
Overhear risk: Household

---

Lanuvium wasn’t exile. Exile would have been cleaner.

Exile came with a sentence. Lanuvium came with a road: eighteen miles of the Appian Way back to the city, an easy day’s ride, and since the funeral fire every mile of it a place to meet the wrong veterans.

The Senate’s letter lay on the study table, moved by Antony, sealed in the Senate’s name. He was the city praetor. He was meant to be in the Forum hearing other men’s lawsuits, with ten nights outside the walls allowed him and no more. Now he might stay out as long as he liked. Brutus had read it twice and could not decide whether it was a kindness or a lock.

Brutus walked the same garden paths until the gravel knew his sandals. Clients came to whisper loyalty and left before dusk, loyalty that evaporated at eighteen miles. Rome was unreachable as Alexandria and louder in rumour than in reach. A steward reported which roads still carried Antonian veterans like dust that refused to settle. Brutus thanked him as if thanks were policy.

“They call you immortal in the same breath they ask you to be quiet,” Porcia said. “They call the boy temporary in the same breath they price his paper. Neither compliment is meant for your safety.”

“Peace requires quiet,” Brutus said.

“Peace required a plan,” Porcia answered, and left him the word *plan* like a debt.

Brutus drafted speeches for a city that would not let him deliver them. Ink dried on periods that would never meet a Forum. Twice he began a sentence that needed Caesar’s name as tyrant and found his hand stopping before the stylus could finish the cruelty. He read Cato into the hedges until a steward’s report of Antonian veterans drinking in the Forum made him set the scroll down mid-sentence. He pressed his thumb hard into the wooden rim of the bench.

Immortality, someone had written him, was better than peace. Brutus folded the sentence and didn’t burn it. Burning would have been a decision. He put the folded scrap under a weight shaped like a tiny Liberty, someone’s gift, someone’s joke, and hated how the joke still worked on him.

Cassius’s letters, from his own house nearer the coast, smelled of ships and contempt. Brutus answered carefully, sentence by sentence. Care might still travel where he could not.

That afternoon a municipal clerk from a neighbouring town arrived with a blank tablet and a smile that wanted a signature. “Only a line,” the clerk said, “that we stood with liberty in March. For our records. If the winds change, the line protects us, or you.”

Porcia, listening from the colonnade, said nothing, a question held in the air.

Brutus saw the trap whole: a line that named him as guarantor of other men’s survival, useful to them now, useful to prosecutors later. “Write that your town stood with the Senate,” he said. “Do not write my name as if it were a roof.”

The clerk’s smile died. “Without your name the tablet is nothing we can show.”

“Then keep it blank,” Brutus said. “I will not put my seal on a road full of Antonian dust.”

The clerk left without clasping arms. Before supper the steward returned from the neighbouring town’s market with flour and a report that cost more than flour. Two clients who had meant to sleep at Lanuvium had already turned back, told at the gate there that Brutus’s house would not shield them. One refusal had made his name a local absence by nightfall.

At dusk Porcia walked the gravel with him. She still favoured the leg a little. She had told the Lanuvium doctor it was an old fall, and he had believed her, because she said it the way she said everything.

“You never asked me about that morning,” she said.

He had not. He had come home from the Capitol on the third day with the amnesty in his ears and his mother in the atrium, and there had been no room in the house for anyone’s morning but his own.

“I couldn’t sit,” Porcia said. “After you went out. I sent a boy to the theatre to tell me when you went in, and another to tell me when Caesar came, and then another, and they kept coming back with nothing, because Caesar didn’t come. I asked every one of them what you looked like. They said you looked like a praetor. I sent them back.” Her voice did not change. “At the fourth hour a boy came running and said Caesar had left his house at last, and I stood up, and the floor came up at me. My women said afterwards I was gone for a long time. Long enough that they began the wailing. Long enough that somebody sent a boy to the theatre to tell you I was dead.”

Brutus stopped walking.

He remembered. He had not let himself remember. A slave of his own household at the edge of the porch, white as a sheet, and the words said in his ear, *the mistress*, *dead*, and Cassius’s face turning toward him, and his own voice saying something steady. He had stayed. He had stood in the porch with the others and waited for Caesar, and in the space of perhaps a hundred breaths he had decided, without deciding, that his wife was dead and that it did not change the morning. Then Caesar’s litter had come into the square, and there had been no more room in him for anything.

He had been told by nightfall that she was alive. He had been glad. He had not thought about the hundred breaths since.

“I know you stayed,” Porcia said. “They told me.” She looked at him in the last light, Cato’s look, clear and unforgiving and not unkind. “I would have stayed too. That is not what I wanted to say. I only wanted you to know that I was there, on the floor, while you were there. In case you thought it was only you who had a morning.”

She went in. Brutus stood on the gravel until the steward came out with a lamp to see if he wanted one.
