Cassius smiled with his teeth and counted who received what: commands, lands, praise.

The host had given him the third couch, a seat chosen the way a man chooses which cloak to lend a stranger. Not an insult. Not an honour. Sextilius had the couch beside it, still pink from the bath and the afternoon’s flattery.

“They have put us together as a lesson,” Sextilius murmured, tearing bread. “Here is the man who joined Caesar’s side early. Here is the man who joined it eventually. Observe the difference in the cushions.”

“I can’t see a difference.”

“Then keep looking.”

The host toasted Caesar’s clemency. Mercy made leftover Pompeians sound edible. Cassius had been one since Pharsalus: fought for Pompey, lost, lived on Caesar’s pardon, and was now expected to look grateful on a third couch. Triumph dust still stuck to the city’s shoes; somewhere a mule train from the Africa run was still being unpaid in someone’s kitchen. Sextilius laughed too easily and lifted his cup whenever the host said *forgiven*. The room did not agree on the exact gift. It only agreed that it shone, and that it had not shone on Cassius.

Cassius ate. The fish was good enough to make silence look like gratitude.

A lesser guest leaned too far toward the host’s couch while the toast was still warm. “If your house still remembers my brother’s petition…” he began, loud enough for the wrong ears.

The host did not look at him. He looked at the wine, then at Sextilius, then past the petitioner. Sextilius’s laugh arrived a beat late and aimed at nothing. Cassius almost answered for him. He reached for bread instead.

A servant leaned in with the jug. The petitioner swallowed the rest of his sentence with the pour. Someone talked about rain. Someone else about cousins. A mule that had thrown a clerk. Ordinary noise, the kind a table makes to cover a hole in itself.

Into that noise, from the foot of the couches, came a clear young voice.

“Is it true about Cato?”

It belonged to Tettius, the host’s cousin, up from the country in a good tunic with no instincts to go with it. He had been waiting all evening to be interesting.

“They say he read Plato all night,” Tettius went on, “and then opened himself with a sword, and the physician sewed him shut, and he tore the stitches out with his own hands. Is that true?”

The table went still the way a table does when a tray drops. Everyone looked at the floor. No one looked at the man who had dropped it.

“Tettius,” the host said gently. “We are eating.”

“I only ask because it seems a great deal of effort.”

Sextilius turned a laugh into a cough and made the cough the wine’s fault. On the first couch a grey senator named Aebutius wiped his fingers and took the room back, as men of his age did. “A philosopher keeps working to the end. It is the one thing they share with bores.”

The laugh came, relieved and too loud. The host let it run exactly as long as it was useful.

“Caesar put it better,” he said. “When the news reached him, he said he grudged Cato his death, since Cato had grudged him the sparing of his life.”

“Very fine,” said Aebutius. “He would say that.”

Cups lifted. Sextilius’s lifted highest. Somewhere in the cheer, *forgiven* got said again, and nobody checked what it was being used for.

Tettius was not finished. Country boys never were. “But why refuse? A pardon is a pardon.”

Cassius had meant to stay out of it. His mouth had other business.

“Some men can’t bear to owe,” he said.

“Whereas the rest of us,” said the host, “manage it beautifully.”

Cassius took a grape before he answered. “With practice.”

The host looked at him a moment longer than the line deserved. Then, all courtesy, as if the whole exchange had been a draft under the door: “You look well on mercy, Cassius. Rome needs men who can accept a soft chain without rattling it.”

“I rattle nothing,” Cassius said, equally courteous. “I digest.”

Under the couch his hand had made a fist. He opened it and took another grape.

He knew Cato the way everyone did. Cato had made sure of it. The man had been offered the same cup that was going round this table, had looked at it, and had gone home to his book and his sword. Either that was the stupidest thing anyone had done in the war or it was the only clean one. Cassius had not decided. He resented having to.

Sextilius leaned in, wine-bright, while Aebutius explained the *Phaedo* to Tettius in the tone of a man correcting a tax return. “Digest carefully. There is talk the queen across the river keeps a child under Caesar’s roof. Private hours. Fresh paint. Best not to notice aloud.”

“Whose paint?”

“Nursery paint, they say. Egyptian blue. It is a colour for people who plan to stay.”

“Who says?”

“A painter. They are the only honest gossips in Rome. They send an invoice.”

The host’s smile did not change. He had heard. Of course he had. “We notice gardens and grain,” he said. “Alexandria’s gossip is not a clause until someone seals it.”

“Nobody here would dream of sealing it,” said Sextilius.

“Nobody here owns a seal worth the wax,” said Aebutius, and the laugh went round again.

The petitioner tried a smaller ask, only an introduction, a name in the right ear. The host’s shoulder was already turned toward better amusement. By the fruit course Cassius’s rib still knew the difference between the favour that shone and the one that had not.

Cassius spoke lightly, into the gap between host and Sextilius: “Some of us are forgiven into rooms like this.”

Sextilius barked a laugh that wanted company. The host’s eyes narrowed a fraction. The petitioner laughed too, glad to be included in any sound, then looked sorry he had. Tettius laughed loudest, having understood none of it, which made him the only honest man at the table.

He left early and courteous: thanks for the fish, praise for the lamps, a wish that Africa stay quiet enough to keep the toasts honest. The host clasped him like a friend.

In the street Sextilius caught up, still smiling for the door-slaves.

“He feeds you forgiveness and keeps the favour for men who laugh on cue,” Sextilius said the moment the threshold lamp was behind them. “I laughed. I am not proud. I am employed.”

Cassius walked. “You laughed at the right toast.”

“And you digested.” Sextilius glanced back. “Also, watch the Transtiberim talk. Men who joke about the queen’s boy at dinner want something. I don’t know what. I only know my land came with a silence attached.”

“Land that comes with a silence is cheap.”

“Land that comes with a silence is expensive. You just pay later.”

They went half a street without speaking. A lamp-boy ran past with a flame in a pot and a mouthful of someone else’s curse.

“You did not laugh at the stitches,” Sextilius said.

“I was busy with a grape.”

“I keep thinking about the physician.” For once Sextilius sounded sober. “All night at honest work. Needle, thread, steady hands. And the man he saved undid it in a minute, to prove a point.”

“Whose point?”

“That is what I cannot work out. Cato’s or Caesar’s.”

Cassius did not answer. He kept the queen talk, and the child, and Sextilius’s silence-on-land, with the names that had smiled wrong. Beneath them, unasked, went a fourth thing: a man who would not owe. He did not yet know which pocket it belonged in. Through the door-slaves’ gap, the petitioner was still smiling at nothing.
