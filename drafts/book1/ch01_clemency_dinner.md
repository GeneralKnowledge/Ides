Cassius smiled with his teeth and counted who received what: commands, lands, praise.

He had come for one thing. Caesar was going to Spain before the winter to finish Pompey’s sons, and the host was one of the men who drew up the names for his staff. A pardoned man who rode on the next campaign stopped being a pardoned man and became an officer again. Cassius had written to the host twice. Tonight he meant to hear the answer in the host’s voice, and he had decided on the way over that he would not ask.

The host had given him the third couch, a seat chosen the way a man chooses which cloak to lend a stranger: not an insult, not an honour. Sextilius had the couch beside it, still pink from the bath and the afternoon’s flattery.

“They have put us together as a lesson,” Sextilius murmured, tearing bread. “Here is the man who joined Caesar’s side early. Here is the man who joined it eventually. Observe the difference in the cushions.”

“I can’t see a difference.”

“Then keep looking.”

The host toasted Caesar’s clemency. Cassius had fought for Pompey, and lost, and had lived since Pharsalus on Caesar’s pardon, and he lifted his cup with the rest. There were three other pardoned men at the table. He had counted them as he came in, and where each had been put. Sextilius laughed too easily and lifted his cup whenever the host said *forgiven*.

Cassius ate. The fish was good enough to make silence look like gratitude.

“They say the Spanish roads are bad in winter,” Cassius said, to the fish.

“Sextilius will tell us,” said the host. “He goes out with Caesar’s staff at the month’s end.” He lifted his cup toward the next couch. “To Spain, and a short war.”

Sextilius lifted his own and laughed, exactly on the beat, the laugh of a man who had been told before dinner. The table drank. Cassius drank with it. He found that he had a piece of bread in his hand and did not remember taking it.

So that was the answer, and it had come in the host’s voice after all, and he had not asked. A servant leaned in with the jug. Someone talked about rain, and someone else about a mule train on the Africa run that had thrown a clerk and still not been paid for. Aebutius, on the first couch, said that his own mules had never been right since the triumphs, and said it at some length.

Into that, from the foot of the couches, came a clear young voice.

“Is it true about Cato?”

It belonged to Tettius, the host’s cousin, up from the country in a good tunic. He had not spoken all evening.

“They say he read Plato all night,” Tettius went on, “and then opened himself with a sword, and the physician sewed him shut, and he tore the stitches out with his own hands. Is that true?”

The table went still the way a table does when a tray drops. Everyone looked at the floor. No one looked at the man who had dropped it.

“Tettius,” the host said gently. “We are eating.”

“I only ask because it seems a great deal of effort.”

Sextilius turned a laugh into a cough and made the cough the wine’s fault. Aebutius, who was grey and a senator, wiped his fingers and took the room back, as men of his age did. “A philosopher keeps working to the end. It is the one thing they share with bores.”

The laugh came, relieved and too loud. The host let it run exactly as long as it was useful.

“Caesar put it better,” he said. “When the news reached him, he said he grudged Cato his death, since Cato had grudged him the sparing of his life.”

“Very fine,” said Aebutius. “He would say that.”

Cups lifted. Sextilius’s lifted highest.

Tettius was not finished. “But why refuse? A pardon is a pardon.”

Cassius had meant to stay out of it.

“Some men can’t bear to owe,” he said.

“Whereas the rest of us,” said the host, “manage it beautifully.”

Cassius took a grape before he answered. “With practice.”

The host looked at him a moment longer than the line deserved. Then, all courtesy, as if the whole exchange had been a draft under the door: “You look well on mercy, Cassius. Rome needs men who can accept a soft chain without rattling it.”

“I rattle nothing,” Cassius said, equally courteous. “I digest.”

Under the couch his hand had made a fist. He opened it and took another grape.

He knew Cato the way everyone did. Cato had made sure of it. The man had been offered the same cup that was going round this table, had looked at it, and had gone home to his book and his sword. Either that was the stupidest thing anyone had done in the war or it was the only clean one. Cassius had not decided. He resented having to.

Sextilius leaned in, wine-bright, while Aebutius explained the *Phaedo* to Tettius in the tone of a man correcting a neighbour’s census declaration. “Digest carefully. There is talk the queen across the river keeps a child under Caesar’s roof. Private hours. Fresh paint. Best not to notice aloud.”

“Whose paint?”

“Nursery paint, they say. Egyptian blue. It is a colour for people who plan to stay.”

“Who says?”

“A painter. They are the only honest gossips in Rome. They send an invoice.”

The host’s smile did not change. He had heard. Of course he had. “We notice gardens and grain,” he said. “Alexandria’s gossip is not a clause until someone seals it.”

Aebutius had not been listening. He had finished with the *Phaedo* and wanted a new subject, and he pointed a grape at Cassius. “Our friend is an Epicurean now, did you know? Gods who take no interest in us. Pleasure in moderation. It must save a fortune on sacrifices.”

“It saves on gratitude,” Cassius said.

That got a better laugh than he wanted. Tettius asked the man beside him what an Epicurean was, and was told, and said that it sounded very sensible. By the fruit course Sextilius had been toasted twice more for Spain.

He left early and courteous: thanks for the fish, praise for the lamps, a wish that Africa stay quiet enough to keep the toasts honest. The host clasped him like a friend.

In the street Sextilius caught up, still smiling for the door-slaves.

“He feeds you forgiveness and keeps Spain for men who laugh on cue,” Sextilius said the moment the threshold lamp was behind them. “I laughed. I am not proud. I am employed.”

Cassius walked. “You laughed at the right toast.”

“And you digested.” Sextilius glanced back. “Also, watch the Transtiberim talk. Men who joke about the queen’s boy at dinner want something. I don’t know what. I only know my land came with a silence attached.”

“Land that comes with a silence is cheap.”

“Land that comes with a silence is expensive. You just pay later.”

They went half a street without speaking. A lamp-boy ran past with a flame in a pot and a mouthful of someone else’s curse.

“You did not laugh at the stitches,” Sextilius said.

“I was busy with a grape.”

“I keep thinking about the physician.” For once Sextilius sounded sober. “All night at honest work. Needle, thread, steady hands. And the man he saved undid it in an hour, to prove a point.”

“Whose point?”

“That is what I cannot work out. Cato’s or Caesar’s.”

Cassius did not answer.

At home he did not go to bed. He had a lamp lit and wrote to Cicero, because Cicero would enjoy the dinner and could be trusted to tell no one but Atticus. He wrote about the fish, and the country cousin, and an Epicurean’s view of gratitude, and made it light. Then, without having meant to, he wrote about Spain: that if it had to be one master or another, he would rather keep the old and clement one than try a new and cruel one.

He read the sentence back. It was true. It sounded like a man thanking his chain. He scraped it off the wax with the flat of the stylus, and sat a while, and wrote it again word for word, because it was still true.
