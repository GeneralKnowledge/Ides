# SC-70a: A Hundred Apiece

POV: Antony (+ Fulvia) · Brundisium · Canon: T9 direction change
Date / place: Mid-October 44 · the harbour ground at Brundisium; his host’s house
Canon refs: App. *BC* 3.43–44 · Cic. *Phil.* 3.4, 5.22, 13.18
Info ENTER: Macedonian legions landing · EXIT: Ranks laugh at a hundred denarii; centurions executed by lot before Fulvia; the boy’s five hundred already in camp
Overhear risk: Whole legions

---

The legions came off the ships from Macedonia looking for a consul, and Antony gave them one.

He stood on a platform of planks on the harbour ground at Brundisium with the sea behind him and four legions in front, the Martian, the Fourth, the Second, the Thirty-fifth, and the old excitement went up through his legs the way it always did before a crowd of soldiers. These were Caesar’s men, raised for Parthia, the best-drilled legions in the world. He had drunk with half their centurions in Gaul. He knew how to stand in front of them. Shoulders loose, voice from the belly. Give them a joke first.

He gave them the joke. A few laughed. Then he gave them the money.

“A hundred denarii a man,” he said, “as a gift from your consul, on the day you land.”

He heard the laugh begin at the back. It was not the laugh he had asked for. It went forward through the ranks the way fire goes along a dry hedge, and the front ranks did not stop it. Somebody near the Martian eagle called out a number. Antony did not hear the number. He heard the shape of it, and that it was larger than his.

He knew then that the boy’s men had been in the camp before him. He had seen the leaflets on the quay that morning, scraps of papyrus trodden into the wet, and had stepped on them.

“Who said that?” he said.

Nobody said who.

“Tribunes,” Antony said, not loud now, and the tribunes came to the foot of the planks. “I want the names of the men who were talking in your camps before I landed. The ones with money in their mouths. You know them. Bring me the lists.”

By evening there were lists. He did not read them. He had the names of the centurions on them written on sherds and put into a helmet, because a consul could not kill four legions and could not let one laugh go unpaid either. In his host’s house, in the colonnade off the courtyard, the men whose sherds came out of the helmet were brought in one at a time.

Fulvia sat beside him. She had asked to. He had not refused her; he did not refuse her much, and tonight he wanted someone in the colonnade who would not look away first. The soldiers doing the work did it quickly, because he had told them quickly. The sound was not loud. A grunt, a scuff on the paving, a weight going down. The second time it was the same. After that he stopped hearing the differences between them.

At some point he looked at his wife. There was a fleck of red high on her cheekbone, under the eye. She had not moved her head and she did not move it now. She did not wipe it away while the next man was brought in, or the one after.

When it was over the courtyard smelled of the sea and of the other thing. A slave brought a basin. Fulvia dipped two fingers and touched them to her cheek, without hurry, and looked at the water afterwards.

“Tomorrow they will take your hundred,” she said, “and not laugh.”

“That’s what I want.”

“Is it?” She dried her fingers on the slave’s cloth. “A man who laughs at your money still thinks you have money. A man who takes it in silence is counting the other offer. The boy said five hundred. They know the sum. Now they also know you kill for less.”

Antony drank. The wine did nothing he asked of it. “The boy has some farms,” he said, “and the loan of Matius’s strongbox. I have four legions.”

“You have four legions on a beach,” Fulvia said. “March them to Rome before they finish counting.”

In the morning the ranks filed past the pay-tables and took the hundred apiece. They took it without a word. Antony stood on the planks and watched them do it and could not find the joke for it. Along the quay the boy’s leaflets lay where they had lain the day before, wet through. No one had picked them up. No one had needed to. A tribune of the Martians came to report the camp quiet and stayed a breath too long, looking at the colonnade.

“They’re saying a name in the Fourth,” he said. “Not yours, consul.”

“Let them say it on the march,” Antony said. “Marching is a cure for most opinions.”

# SC-70b: Five Hundred

POV: F3 Laberius · Casilinum, on the Volturnus near Capua · Canon: T9 · T4
Date / place: October 44 · the town square at Casilinum; a pay-table
Canon refs: Cic. *Att.* 16.8 · App. *BC* 3.40
Info ENTER: Twine unmoved since Munda; “When the shadow learns to feed us” · EXIT: Takes the boy’s five hundred because the clerk can find his plot; twine still unmoved
Overhear risk: Colony square

---

The boy came to Casilinum himself, which nobody had expected.

Laberius had walked over from the colony because Vibius said there would be money and Vibius was usually right about money and wrong about everything else. The square by the Volturnus bridge was full of men who had been told the same thing. Some had come on mules. One had come on a crutch. The boy stood on the steps of a temple with a cloak too warm for the day, thinner than his name, with a big young man at his shoulder who did the looking for both of them.

He made a short speech. It was a bad speech. He said *my father* four times, and *what is owed* twice, and he did not tell a single joke. The men listened the way men listen to a paymaster, which is to say for the number.

“Five hundred denarii,” the boy said. “To every man who enrols. Now, at the tables. More when the work is done.”

Nobody laughed. A noise went round the square that Laberius had heard twice in his life, both times on the morning of a donative, and the man on the crutch said “Five hundred” to himself as if the words had a taste.

The scar-lipped veteran from the colony forum in the spring was standing three men along. He caught Laberius’s eye and lifted his chin. *The shadow learned.* He did not need to say it.

“Five hundred’s a number,” Laberius said to Vibius. “Brundisium will have heard it. Antony will say six.”

“Antony said one,” Vibius said. “Ask the sailors.”

There were three pay-tables under an awning by the bridge, and a clerk at each with the colony rolls open in front of him, the surveyor’s rolls, the ones with the plots marked in a neat hand. Laberius had not seen those rolls since a surveyor had read his name from one at Munda’s end and tied his twine. He had thought they lived in a cupboard in Rome where the dead man’s clerks kept them.

The clerk at the middle table was a freedman with ink on his thumb and a cough. “Name.”

“Sextus Laberius Cordus. Spain. Munda.”

The clerk ran his thumb down a column, turned a leaf, ran it down again. “Laberius. Colony plot by Capua, fourth line from the Volturnus road, marked after Munda.” He looked up. “Twine, it says. Not stones yet.”

“Twine,” Laberius said. “Still.”

“The stones are a matter for after,” the clerk said, not unkindly. He had said it before that morning, many times. “The coin is now.” He wrote the day on a tablet, the fifth before the Ides of October, and Laberius’s name beside it, and pushed the tablet across for his mark.

Laberius looked at the date. He looked at the column where his name sat in someone else’s neat hand, his row of twine, the road, Munda. The boy had not bought his land; the clerk had said as much. But the boy’s people had gone into the dead man’s cupboards and come out with the rolls, and carried them down the Appian Way to a bridge on the Volturnus, so that a clerk with a cough could find a man’s twine with his thumb. Antony’s man in the spring had offered wine and a road. This one knew where the dirt was.

He made his mark.

The bag the clerk counted out to him weighed about what a full water-skin weighed on the march. He felt it pull on his belt. Vibius, at the next table, was laughing for no reason with a bag in each hand, one of them borrowed off a man who had not enrolled.

On the walk back over the bridge the scar-lipped veteran fell in beside him.

“Feeds us now, the shadow,” the veteran said.

“Pays us,” Laberius said. “It isn’t the same.”

“Tastes the same.”

“Ask me when he wants something for it,” Laberius said. The twine near Capua had not moved, and would not move tonight, and he knew it. He held the bag against his hip with one hand all the way home anyway.

# SC-70c: The Statue

POV: Octavian · Rome, then Arretium · Canon: T9 payoff
Date / place: 10 November 44 · Forum by the Temple of Castor; road north; Arretium late November
Canon refs: Cic. *Att.* 16.8–15 · App. *BC* 3.41–45
Info ENTER: Three thousand veterans enrolled; Antony coming from Brundisium · EXIT: Oath at Caesar’s statue; veterans refuse to fight Antony and go home; Martian legion and Fourth come over
Overhear risk: Public contio

---

He came into Rome with three thousand men and found the Forum smaller than he remembered.

They held the Temple of Castor and its steps, which was the nearest thing in the Forum to a fort, and the men who had taken his five hundred stood about on the steps and in the street below with their swords on, looking at the city as if it might owe them something too. Octavian had written to Cicero every day for a week: come to Rome, stand beside me, the Senate listens to you. Cicero had written back from the coast with advice and without himself. Today he was still on the coast.

The tribune Cannutius spoke first. He spoke against Antony well and at length, because he had nothing to lose by it and enjoyed the sound. Then he called Gaius Caesar to the front of the steps.

Octavian had written the speech three times. He gave it the fourth way, shorter. He spoke of his father’s murder and his father’s friends, of the money Antony would not give the people and he had given them himself, of the consul who had killed Caesar’s centurions at Brundisium for laughing. Below him the veterans listened in a way he could not read. They had listened at Casilinum for the number. He did not know what they were listening for now.

At the end, as he had planned, he turned to the bronze statue of Caesar that stood by the temple and lifted his right hand to it.

“As I hope to attain my father’s honours,” he said, “I swear…”

He heard the noise change before he finished. It came from the veterans, and it was not a cheer.

“We came to guard you,” a man called from the street. Octavian knew the face a moment later: a scar through the lip, one of the Casilinum men. “From him. Not to kill him. He was Caesar’s too.”

Others took it up. *Caesar’s too.* *We didn’t enrol to fight the consul.* A man near the steps took off his helmet and held it under his arm like a man leaving a dinner.

Octavian finished the oath, because there was nothing to be done with half an oath. Then he stood and watched his army decide.

By dark a third of them had gone. By the next morning it was more. They went in twos and threes, in the direction of their colonies, some of them still with his five hundred in their belts. He let them go. He could not have stopped them without asking the rest to stop them, and he did not know what the rest would do.

A veteran by the temple steps did not go. He was an ordinary man, broad, quiet, with Campanian dust still on his boots, and he stood with his back to a column and counted the men who were leaving as they went past him, his lips moving. When he caught Octavian looking at him he stopped counting and did not explain.

“Antony will be in the city in a fortnight,” Agrippa said that night. “With four legions.”

“Then we will not be in it,” Octavian said.

They went north into Etruria, to Arretium, where Maecenas’s family had money and houses and the winter came early. Octavian drilled what he had left on frozen ground and wrote to Cicero again, more carefully. He did not mention the oath, or the helmet. He had learned at the Temple of Castor that veterans would take his money to stand near Antony and would not take it to kill him. He did not yet know what he had that would change their minds.

Toward the end of the month a rider came up the road from the south, one of Agrippa’s, with frost in his beard.

“Alba Fucens,” he said. “The Martian legion. They shut the gates on Antony and shot at him from the walls. They’ve declared for you.”

Octavian put the tablet down.

“And the Fourth,” the rider said. “Two days after. Antony came to the walls with money in his hand and talked about Brundisium as if they’d forgotten it. They hadn’t.”

Agrippa let out a breath that was almost a laugh. Octavian did not laugh. He sat down at the table, because his knees wanted him to, and kept his hands flat on it until they were still.

“Pay them,” he said. “What I promised. Then the same again.”

“With what?” Agrippa said.

Octavian did not answer that. He took a clean tablet and began a letter to Cicero, the shortest he had written him. This time he knew he would have an answer.

# SC-70: Cisalpine

POV: Decimus · Cisalpine HQ
Date / place: Late 44 · command rooms / gates / wall-walk
Canon refs: A10 · C1 residual · CLEO-1 as rumour · FS11 as margin unease
Info ENTER: Legal command · EXIT: Antony wants swap; war geometry forms; queen offstage; Sextus unresolved
Overhear risk: Officers / couriers

---

Decimus read Antony’s pressure in dispatches before he heard it in hoofbeats.

The tablets arrived with seals that looked like courtesy and read like a hand on a throat. One letter offered friendship in the same sentence as a deadline. Decimus set that letter under a stone so it couldn’t blow into the yard and become gossip.

At mid-morning an envoy came anyway, cloak clean, smile practised, the sort of man who carried other men’s threats as if they were invitations to dinner. He stood in the command room where the wall map of the Via Aemilia still showed Decimus’s pins as facts. An aide hovered with a wax roll. A levy clerk waited at the door with pay marks still wet. The room wanted to be polite. It was already a siege rehearsal.

“The consul asks only what the law already leans toward,” the envoy said. “Hand him Cisalpine in good order. Keep your honour. Keep your skin. Rome will find you another usefulness.”

“Rome found me this one,” Decimus said. He didn’t offer a chair. Chairs made threats look like visits.

“He will call it a swap,” the envoy went on, mild. “You will call it wisdom. The Senate will call it clarification, when clarification arrives.”

Decimus knew the swap because it had been done to him while he was on the road, by an assembly full of Antony’s veterans. The Senate had never voted it back.

Decimus heard the gap in that sentence: *when*. Clarification that might never leave the stable. “Tell him I call it mine until the Senate says otherwise, or until he brings enough men to rename facts. He can call the renaming breakfast if he likes.”

The envoy’s eyes flicked once to the levy roll on the table: names, marks for pay, a smudge where someone’s thumb had been nervous. “Your men still love Caesar’s pay more than your legality. My principal has a longer memory for that love than you do.”

That was the first true thing said. Decimus felt it land under the sternum where the Ides still sat like a key he couldn’t return. “Get out,” he said. “If you return with an army, return with an army. Do not return with grammar.”


The envoy paused at the threshold long enough for the room to hear the complaint under the courtesy: “He will remember the refusal more than the wit.” Then he left.

After the man was gone, Decimus walked the gates. Hinges took oil.

In the yard he found the levy spokesman, an older miles with a Caesarian scar and a mouth that had learned to ask for bread without kneeling. “We heard the cloak,” the man said. “If Antony comes, do we fight men who still drink to the name we buried with?”

“You fight the men who try to take the road,” Decimus said.

“And pay?”

Decimus looked at the chest. Coin still rattled when the lid shifted, but shallowly, honest about the bottom. He ordered a partial donative for the gate cohorts, coin he’d meant to hold for spring, and watched the spokesman’s shoulders drop half an inch.

The spokesman weighed the purse with his eyes, not his hands. “Partial,” he said. “That’s a word that tastes like next week’s apology.”

Decimus almost grinned, the old cheer that had opened mornings, and stopped it before it became a performance. “Then taste it and still hold the hinge. Next week’s apology is still coin I have to find.” The spokesman barked once, short, surprised, and the yard heard it as weather, not mutiny. Decimus scheduled the rest of the pay talk for morning.

He wrote to Cicero with the stiff pride of a man who still believed praise from Rome was supply, and knew privately that praise was thinner than grain. The same week he wrote Plancus again, shorter, less perfume, asking for two cohorts’ worth of grain and a date, any date, for a meeting on the road.

The answer did not arrive alone. A courier who had eaten at Plancus’s table stood in the yard with mud on one boot and perfume still clinging to the other, Plancus’s weather made flesh. “He admires your hinges,” the man said, practising the pretty line. “He understands Antony’s appetite. His own theatre is unsettled. Grain and cohorts cannot be spared.” Decimus waited for a date. The courier’s eyes found the gate-hinge instead. “He told me to say *yet*, if you pressed. *Yet* is not a day on a road.” Decimus dismissed the man before admiration could invent another perfume. The Lepidus channel he had seeded on the road north, a roof near Mutina’s approaches, a messenger who would not meet his eyes, still returned nothing. He did not send a second tablet to ask why.

The aide asked anyway, one finger holding open the empty slot in the letter-case. “Lepidus has more legions over the mountains than Plancus has. One word from him and Antony has friends at his back as well as a wall in front.”

“One word from Lepidus goes to whoever is standing nearest when he has to say it,” Decimus said. “Pray it’s us.” He did not pray. He sent the aide to count the grain.

A southern courier arrived near dusk with a packet that smelled of harbour pitch. The queen was home in Alexandria, boy and all, months gone from Caesar’s gardens. Decimus set the packet under the stone with Antony’s courtesy-threats. Egypt was Egypt’s business until it had ships in his harbour.

A rider came up the Via Aemilia from Ariminum with his horse lathered to the girth: Antony had left Rome with what the boy had left him of the Macedonian legions and was on the road north. Not hurrying. That was worse.

Decimus did not wait to be caught on the open plain. He took his legions into Mutina, because Mutina had walls, a granary, and the road running past its gate. In the yard, while carts were loading, the levy spokesman asked what came next.

“He comes up the road,” Decimus said. “He can’t march on into Gaul with a town full of legions at his back, so he stops. He digs ditches round us and waits for us to eat the last ox. We wait for an army from Rome to come and break his ring. Whoever’s count runs out first loses.”

“Which army?” the spokesman said. “Rome’s busy writing.”

“Next year’s,” Decimus said. “On the Kalends the consulship leaves Antony’s hands. Caesar named the next two before he died. Aulus Hirtius, who writes better than he rides and has been sick since spring. Gaius Vibius Pansa, who governed provinces for Caesar and can get farm boys into armour faster than most men get senators into a vote. Letters can’t raise legions. Consuls can, and lead them. Those two are the only swords the Senate owns.”

“Hirtius and Pansa.” The spokesman worked it through aloud. “Caesar’s men. Coming to get Caesar’s…” He stopped. “Coming to get you out.”

“Yes.” Decimus did not help him finish the first sentence. It would only be funny if they arrived.

Inside Mutina he had the draught oxen killed and salted in the granary yard, every beast that could pull a cart, because a shut town had no use for carts and a great deal of use for meat. The butchers worked on past dark by torchlight. Brine and blood ran together in the gutter. The spokesman watched with his arms folded and did not say *partial*.

Later he walked Mutina’s wall-walk and tasted snow early. The key in his chest cut both ways. It had opened Caesar’s morning. It might yet open his own throat. He checked a hinge twice and didn’t tell the sentry why.
