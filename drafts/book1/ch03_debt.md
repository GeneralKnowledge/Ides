Seius stood in a morning line of men practising hope.

The atrium smelled of wet wool and yesterday’s garum, the fish-sauce stink of the street. A secretary with a stylus counted faces. Someone ahead whispered a marriage rumour loud enough to be useful; someone behind coughed a debt he pretended was news. Seia Prima had sent Seius with instructions that felt like a rope: be seen, be useful, come home with a nod that can be spent.

Beside him an older client named Naevius held his toga with the weariness of a man who had worn it to two hundred of these. “First rule of the line,” he murmured, without moving his lips. “Never be the man who coughs.”

“Someone behind us is coughing.”

“That is the man who owes. Listen to him. He coughs whenever he thinks of the steward.”

“And the second rule?”

“Arrive at the exact hour of a man who has already eaten. Not early enough to look hungry. Not late enough to look busy.” Naevius shifted his weight. “Nobody manages it. Everyone tries.”

“You’re old Seius’s nephew,” Naevius added, still not moving his lips. “The grain man. My father ate cheap bread off your uncle the year the city went hungry. Every old client in this line remembers it. None of them will give you a nod for it.”

Naevius went up first. The patron said his name, warmly, and the nod travelled the whole distance. Naevius took it, bowed to the exact height, and came back past Seius with a face like a closed shop. “Thirty years,” he whispered. “The nod is a muscle. Yours will grow.”

When Seius’s turn came, the patron’s nod was thin, already halfway to the next man.

“We will see,” the patron said. “Caesar’s sky is large. Do not stand in it foolishly. Bring me something useful, news, a marriage rumour, a debt I can collect with a smile.”

A secretary at the patron’s elbow added, polite as oil, “And do not bring us philosophy. Philosophy is what clients do when they have nothing spendable.”

Seius smiled like a man who owned useful things. He didn’t. He owned a sister’s strained dowry talk and a talent for sweating through wool.

“I am grateful for the guidance,” Seius said, with the exact bow height the atrium taught.

Outside, in the lane’s cold, the middleman who smelled of Hordeonius’s wax waited with the patience of a professional accident. Seius had already borrowed for the canvass: the whitened toga for the aedile elections, the small gifts a candidate is seen to give, a jar of oil good enough to be seen carrying. Now, it appeared, he would borrow for the borrowing. The sum was small enough to feel temporary and large enough to change what dinners Seius could refuse.

“Partial favour upstairs,” the middleman said. “Full paper downstairs. You understand leverage.”

“What did he give you?” the middleman added, when Seius said nothing.

“A nod.”

“How thick?”

“Lint.”

“Then it is a cheap nod, and cheap nods carry dear rates. The thinner the favour, the thicker the paper. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic.”

Seius’s smile lasted until the atrium door-slave looked away. Then it fell. “He nodded like a man brushing lint. The secretary talked like I was a rumour he could sell. I am going to tell Seia Prima it was a warm reception.”

The middleman snorted, audible, unlovely. “Warm receptions are how we sell winter. Sign.”

Seius took the terms because refusal meant standing still, and Seia Prima didn’t reward stillness. He passed two clients still comparing whose patron had smiled wider. Comparison was the atrium’s second religion. Seius did not join. He was busy inventing a wider smile for home.

At the house threshold Seia Prima was already waiting with a broom she did not need; waiting was her way of auditing. She took one look at his mouth and did not ask about the atrium.

“Wider than last week,” she said. “That means thinner paper.”

“He said ‘we will see.’ Twice.”

“Twice is a window. Once is a door.”

“Does the window open?”

“It lets in weather.”

Seius handed her the invented warmth first, then the true sum. She weighed both without raising her voice. “My father sold this city its grain at a loss for a whole year,” she said, “and thirty years on men still nod to him in the street. I was born a Seia and married one, so that nod would stay in the house. Tonight we dine as if yours were meat.” She turned back inside before the lane could hear her correct him. The middleman’s wax had followed him home; Seia Prima set an extra cup anyway and did not drink from it.
