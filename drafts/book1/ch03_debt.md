# SC-03: For Ligarius

POV: Cicero · the Forum, Caesar’s tribunal
Date / place: Late 46 · the Forum, a morning hearing before Caesar as dictator
Canon refs: Cic. *Pro Ligario* (esp. 37–38) · Plut. *Cic.* 39 · Cic. *Fam.* 6.13–14 · B5
Info ENTER: Ligarius, a Pompeian still in Africa, accused by Tubero · EXIT: pardon won; the debt Cicero has written for him
Overhear risk: The whole Forum

---

Caesar had the verdict written before Cicero stood up. Everybody knew it, because Caesar had said so.

A friend of Balbus had carried the remark across the Forum before the hearing began, the way remarks of Caesar’s were always carried, at a walk that pretended to be casual. *Why not hear Cicero speak? It has been a long time. The case is decided anyway. Ligarius is a bad man and an enemy.* Cicero heard it at the foot of the tribunal with his notes in his hand and felt the old sickness and the old pleasure arrive together, as they had at every trial since he was twenty-six.

The tribunal had been set up in the open, for the crowd. Caesar sat on it alone, in the purple of the dictatorship, with a tablet on his knee. He did not look like a man who needed to hear anything. He looked like a man who had come to watch a famous horse run once more before it was sold.

Quintus Tubero spoke first for the prosecution, young and correct and very pleased with his own anger. Ligarius had stayed in Africa. Ligarius had borne arms against Caesar under Varus and Juba. Ligarius had kept Tubero’s own father from landing there in the war, which was the part Tubero minded. All of it was true. Ligarius was not even in Rome to deny it. He was still in Africa, a pardoned army’s unpardoned officer, waiting in some hot town for a letter to tell him whether he could come home.

His brothers were here instead, in black, with their hair uncut, kneeling at the foot of the tribunal as the custom wanted. One of them had grey in his beard. Cicero had dined with that one. He knew the price of the grey.

When his turn came he did not argue that Ligarius was innocent. He had decided that in the night, lying awake with Tiro’s draft beside the lamp. Innocence was the defence for a court, and this was not a court. It was one man with a tablet on his knee.

“I am not pleading a case,” Cicero said, “I am asking a father.”

It went out over the crowd and he felt them take it. Caesar did not move.

He gave them Ligarius as a decent man who had chosen the wrong side, which was half the Senate. He gave them Tubero, who had chosen the same side and only failed to reach it, and asked Tubero gently what exactly his crime had been, if it was not the same. The crowd laughed. Tubero went red. Caesar did not move.

Then Cicero took them to Pharsalus.

He had not meant to stay there long. He spoke of the battle line, and the men on it who had been friends, and those of them now in this Forum who had been spared by the man on the tribunal and stood here alive because he had wanted them alive. He named nobody. He did not need to. Half the faces in the front of the crowd had been on that field.

Caesar’s colour changed.

Cicero saw it and did not believe it, and went on, and saw it again: the dictator of Rome on his chair in the purple, gone pale and then red, his hand moving on the tablet as if it had a life of its own. Cicero had spent thirty years watching juries lean. He had never seen a man shake. He found the next period without knowing where it came from.

“None of your virtues is more admired than your mercy,” he said, “and none is more loved. Men come nearest to the gods when they give other men their lives.”

The tablet slid off Caesar’s knee. It fell on the boards of the tribunal with a flat clatter, and the papers in it went out across the planks, and the crowd heard it and went silent all at once, as if the sound had been part of the speech.

Caesar did not pick it up. A secretary did, on his knees. Caesar sat looking at nothing. Then he said, quite quietly, that Ligarius was acquitted and might come home.

The brothers kissed his hand. The crowd made the noise a crowd makes when it has been given a story. Men came up to Cicero all across the Forum and took his arm and told him he had not lost it, he had never lost it, the old voice, and he let them, and smiled, and felt the thing he had felt at twenty-six, like wine in an empty stomach.

It lasted until he got home.

Tiro had the wax ready. Cicero dictated the letter to Ligarius in Africa standing up, because he could not sit. *You are safe. You may come home. I have spoken to him myself, and I give you my word that he means it. Thank him.* He stopped there.

“Read it back,” he said.

Tiro read it back. *Thank him.*

Cicero had written that phrase a hundred times since Pharsalus, to a hundred men, and had always meant it kindly. He heard it now in his own voice, read out by a freedman, and thought of Ligarius on the quay at Utica or Hadrumetum with the letter in his hand: a proud man, a stubborn one, who had refused to come in when the others came in and had stayed in Africa out of something that was not quite courage and not quite sulk. He would come home now. He would walk into the Forum where his brothers had knelt in black, and every man he met would know that his life was a gift, and whose, and who had begged for it in the most beautiful Latin anyone had heard in years.

*Men come nearest to the gods when they give other men their lives.* It had been the best sentence of the morning. Cicero saw, too late to unsay it, that it was also a bill.

“Leave it,” he said. “Seal it as it is.”

Tiro sealed it. Cicero sat down at last, and found he was tired in a way that had nothing to do with his voice.
