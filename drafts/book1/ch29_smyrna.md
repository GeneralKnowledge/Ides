# SC-74a: Good Walls

POV: F5 Amyntas · Smyrna
Date / place: January 43 · the city treasury; the walls; the agora
Canon refs: T10 · App. *BC* 3.26 · Cic. *Phil.* 11.5–9 · Dio 47.29
Info ENTER: Trebonius governs Asia; Dolabella marching through to Syria · EXIT: Trebonius killed in the night; his head on the governor’s chair
Overhear risk: Clerks; Dolabella’s soldiers

---

Amyntas son of Menodotus had kept the treasury of Smyrna for eleven years, and in all that time he had never been asked to feed an army that was outside the walls on purpose.

“Not in,” the proconsul said. “Under no circumstances in. But fed. Generously. I want him to remember us fondly when he’s in Syria.”

Gaius Trebonius said this in good Greek, with a Roman accent he did nothing to hide, sitting in the council chamber with his feet on a stool because the January damp got into them. He had governed Asia for eight months. Amyntas had come to like him, which he had not expected. He was a short, cheerful man who asked the price of things before he asked their history, and who had once, on a tour of the harbour, stopped to buy figs from a stall and paid what the woman asked without haggling, which had been talked about in the agora for a week.

So the treasury fed Dolabella.

Amyntas wrote it down as he did everything. Barley, so many *medimni*. Wine, so many jars, the second quality; oil; fodder for four hundred horses. Sheep, live, driven out of the north gate each morning to the camp on the plain where the consul of last year, the proconsul of Syria, once the son-in-law of Cicero, sat with his legions and waited to be let in and was not. Each night the gates were shut and the watch doubled. Each morning the carts went out again. Amyntas entered the cost under a heading he invented for it, *hospitality to a passing magistrate*, and looked at it, and thought that the city had paid many kinds of hospitality to many Romans in his lifetime, and that this was the first he had been asked to pay from behind a locked gate.

Dolabella sent courteous letters. He understood entirely. He would not dream of troubling the city. He was on his way to his province and wanted only supplies and a little rest for his men. He sent the treasurer of Smyrna, by name, a cup of good silver as thanks for the barley, and Amyntas entered it in the books as a gift to the city and put it in the strongroom and did not drink from it.

On the eleventh day the camp on the plain was struck.

Amyntas watched it from the wall with half the council. It was done briskly, in the early afternoon, tents down and packed, the column formed on the Ephesus road and marching away south with its baggage and its standards, and Dolabella himself riding at the head of it in a scarlet cloak and waving to the walls as he went, the way a man waves to a house where he has dined well.

“Well,” said Trebonius, beside him, pleased. “There we are. I told you. Friendly town.”

The watch was stood down to its usual strength that night. Amyntas went home to his house below the temple of Nemesis and ate with his wife and his two daughters and went to bed early, because it had been a long eleven days, and slept, and was woken in the dark by a sound he did not at first understand.

It was the sound of a great many men moving quietly.

He went up on to his roof in his night tunic. The streets below the temple were full of soldiers, not running but walking fast, in file, with their shields covered and their swords drawn and nobody talking. They had come over the wall on the seaward side, where it was lowest, with ladders. Later he learned they had turned back on the Ephesus road at dusk, a few cohorts of them, and come up through the olive groves in the dark while the rest of the column went on making noise to the south. Later he learned all of it. That night he only stood on his roof and watched the files go by below, all going the same way, uphill, toward the governor’s house.

There was no fighting to speak of. There was no one to fight. The city had been told it was safe.

In the morning the soldiers were on the gates and in the agora and the city was very quiet. Amyntas dressed and went down to the treasury, because it was the treasury, and someone had to be there when they came for it. On the way he crossed the agora.

They had set up the governor’s chair on the tribunal at the top of the steps, the ivory chair Trebonius sat in to hear cases, and on the chair, where a man would sit, they had put his head.

Amyntas did not stop. He kept walking at the same pace, across the agora and up the steps of the treasury and in, and sat down at his table, and found that he had walked past it so quickly he could not have said for certain whether the eyes were open.

The clerks came in one by one. Nobody said anything for a long time. Then the youngest, who had been in the street at first light, told it, because he could not help telling it. Trebonius had been taken in his bed. He had asked them to bring him to Dolabella. A centurion had said that Dolabella was not here, but that he would take the head to him, and the rest could stay. Afterwards some of the soldiers had…

“Enough,” said Amyntas.

The boy stopped. Later Amyntas would hear other versions. That the governor had been kept alive two days and made to tell where the money was. That he had been killed at once and the two days were a story told in Rome. That the soldiers had used the head in the street like a ball, kicking it from one to another between the colonnades until there was nothing about it a wife would know. Amyntas never asked which was true. He did not go out into the street that day, or the next. He had two daughters.

Dolabella rode into the city at noon, by the main gate, which was opened for him. He wore the scarlet cloak. He went up the steps of the tribunal and stood by the chair and made a short speech to whoever was in the agora, which was not many, about Caesar, and the debt of blood owed to Caesar, and the first of the murderers to pay it. Then he sent for the city’s treasurer by name.

Amyntas went. He took the city’s books. He did not take the other one, the small book of the temple of Nemesis, where the dowries of the city’s orphan girls were kept, which lived under the floor of his own house because the temple roof leaked. Nobody had asked for it. Crossing the agora, with the chair at the top of the steps, he decided that nobody would.

“The cup,” Dolabella said pleasantly. He had a handsome flushed face and very good teeth. “I sent you a cup. I hope you liked it.”

“It is in the city’s strongroom, Imperator.”

“Good. Good. I shall want the rest of what’s in there.” Dolabella glanced at the chair beside him without interest, the way a man glances at a table that has not yet been cleared. “Asia is a rich province. Syria is a long way. I’m sure Smyrna will be generous. It was so generous all last week.”

Amyntas opened the books on the steps and began to read the figures aloud. His voice was perfectly steady. He found that if he kept his eyes on the columns of figures, the barley and the oil and the sheep, the hospitality to a passing magistrate, he did not have to see anything else at all.

# SC-74b: Enemy

POV: Cicero · Rome
Date / place: Early March 43 · the Senate; Cicero’s house after
Canon refs: T10 · Cic. *Phil.* 11 · *Att.* 14.15 (recalled)
Info ENTER: News of Smyrna reaches Rome · EXIT: Dolabella declared a public enemy; Cicero asks for Cassius to have the war
Overhear risk: The House; Tiro

---

The news from Smyrna reached Rome at the end of February in letters from Asia, and by the first of March every house on the Palatine was telling it with more blood than the last.

Cicero told it with the most. That was his work.

He stood in the House on the day of the debate and told it so that the fathers would see it: the ladders in the dark, the man in his bed, the two days, the questions, the chair, the head. He did not know that the two days were true. He knew that they were true enough to move a vote, and that a vote was what Trebonius needed now, more than accuracy. *Gaius Trebonius*, he said, *my friend*, and he let his voice catch on *friend*, and it caught by itself, which surprised him. He called Dolabella a monster, a beast in a man’s shape, a torturer, a thing that had crawled out of Caesar’s camp still wearing Caesar’s scent. He said the Senate must declare him a public enemy that day, and it did, with only a handful against.

Then he asked the House to give the war against Dolabella to Cassius, who was somewhere in Syria already with no commission from anyone, and on that he lost. The consuls wanted it for themselves, once Mutina was done. The fathers preferred a consul they could see to a Liberator they could not. Cicero went home with half a victory and the rest of his speech in his head, still talking.

Tiro had the boxes out when he came in. Tiro kept the letters, copies of everything, the outgoing and the incoming, in cedar boxes by year, so that one day there might be a book. He had the box for the year of the Ides open on the table, and he did not say anything when Cicero came in. He only moved the lamp a little nearer.

Cicero knew what was in it. He did not need to read it. *O my wonderful Dolabella. I call him mine now.* The best paragraph. The copy he had sent to the young man himself, with warm congratulations, because it was always well to be generous to a consul.

“Put it back,” he said.

Tiro put it back, and closed the box.

Cicero sat down. His throat hurt from the speech. He thought, not for the first time and with no more profit than the other times, that the young man had pulled down a column in April and cut off a head in January with exactly the same face, and that he, Cicero, had praised the face once and cursed it once and been sincere both times. He thought of Tullia at twenty-eight in her bridal veil, laughing. He still could not remember what had made her laugh. He had a feeling it had been something Dolabella said, and that it had been funny.

“The fathers will want the speech written up,” Tiro said.

“Yes.” Cicero held out his hand for a tablet. “The eleventh. Leave out the part about friend. It sounded rehearsed.”

It had not been rehearsed. That was why it had to go.

# SC-69b: Apollonia

POV: Brutus · Macedonia, the coast
Date / place: Late winter 43 → March 43 · the road west; the marsh country below Apollonia; the town
Canon refs: T8 · Plut. *Brut.* 25–26 · Dio 47.21–23 · Cic. *Phil.* 10
Info ENTER: Macedonia handed over; Gaius Antonius on the coast with the province his brother arranged · EXIT: Gaius’s men come over; Brutus spares him and lets him keep his lictors
Overhear risk: Officers; Gaius’s lictors

---

By the end of the winter Brutus had an army, and it had been made almost entirely by other people changing their minds.

Hortensius had given him Macedonia. The legion at Dyrrachium had come over with its commander, Vatinius, who opened the gates and said that he had always admired Brutus, which was untrue, and that his men had refused to fight, which was. Cavalry riding east through Thessaly to join Dolabella in Syria had stopped on the road and been talked round. And Piso, Gaius’s own legate, had surrendered his legion on the road to young Marcus Cicero, who had ridden at it with a few squadrons and more confidence than sense. Marcus had come back hoarse and delighted, and Brutus had written to his father about it in terms so warm he had read the letter over twice before sealing it, to be sure he meant them. He did. The boy rode well and was brave in the way that comes before a man has been hurt, and the men liked him, and he had stopped drinking at the eighth hour, mostly.

Gaius Antonius held Apollonia.

He had come over from Italy with a province drawn by a lot his brother had arranged, and found most of his province already gone. What he had left was a legion, the town, and the marsh country south of it where the river came down to the sea through reeds and salt flats and a causeway built on piles. He had tried to march on Dyrrachium and turned back. He had tried to hold the river crossings and lost them. Now he sat inside the walls of Apollonia with the sea behind him and Brutus’s army closing the land.

A letter came from Rome as the army came down to the coast. Cicero, of course, in his own hand. The Senate had met in February and voted, on his motion, that Marcus Brutus should hold Macedonia, Illyricum and all of Greece, with the legions in them, and should keep his army as near Italy as he could. *The Republic is in your hands*, Cicero wrote. *I said so in the House, and they did not laugh.*

Brutus read it on horseback and handed it to an aide and rode on. He had been a corn commissioner in the summer and a commander of the Republic in February, and nothing about him had changed but the vote. He found he had no feeling about that at all, and was a little frightened by how easily he had none.

Apollonia sat on its hill above the plain behind a good wall. Brutus knew it by its school; half the young men he had dined with in Rome had been sent there for their rhetoric. Caesar had sent the boy there, the great-nephew, the one who now called himself Caesar in Italy and paid soldiers out of his own estates. The boy had been in Apollonia on the Ides, studying, and had heard of it here, from a freedman of his mother’s who came off a ship. Brutus looked up at the walls and thought of that, of a young man reading in a garden up there and a ship coming in, and put the thought away because he could not tell what it was for.

He did not storm the town. He did not need to. He camped on the plain within sight of the walls and let Gaius’s men look at his army, and sent riders round under the walls at a safe distance, calling up the names of the men inside who had relatives in his ranks. Marcus Cicero did some of the calling. He had a voice for it. Horace did some. He had jokes for it, and the men on the wall laughed at them before they remembered not to.

On the fourth day a cohort of Gaius’s men came out of the south gate in good order, shields covered, and walked down through the marsh along the causeway, and came over.

On the sixth day the rest came.

Gaius came on the seventh. He came out of the main gate on foot with his six lictors walking ahead of him with the fasces on their shoulders, as if he were going down to the Forum to hear a case, and his toga properly folded, and the family jaw set, and nothing else at all. No soldiers. A secretary with a box. He walked down the road from the town to Brutus’s camp in the bright March cold, a mile or more, with the whole army watching him from the rampart, and nobody said anything, and the only sound was the lictors’ feet on the road.

Brutus went out to meet him at the camp gate. He had not decided to. His legs decided, and he let them.

They stood a pace apart. Gaius was a year or two younger than his brother and looked more, because he had his brother’s face without the weight behind it, and the face had been waiting all its life to be taken seriously. It was not waiting now. It was very tired.

“I’ve come to surrender the town,” Gaius said. His voice was steady. He had practised it. “My men have already surrendered themselves, I think you’ll find.”

“I’d noticed.”

“Yes.” Gaius looked past him at the rampart, at the faces on it, some of them faces he would know. “My brother will say I should have fought.”

“Your brother says a great many things.”

Something moved at the corner of Gaius’s mouth. It was almost the family joke, coming up at last for its turn. Then it went down again. “What happens now?”

And Brutus heard himself answer.

He heard it the way he had heard himself say *GO* in the dark a year ago: a voice slightly outside himself, very calm, very kind, already decided. He said that Gaius was a Roman magistrate, and would be treated as one. That he should keep his lictors and his rank. That he would have a house in the camp and his own servants and the freedom of the lines, and would dine with Brutus when he chose, and that the question of his future would wait upon the Senate, as was proper. That there would be no chains. That there was no need for chains between Romans.

He said all this warmly. He meant it. And somewhere in the middle of saying it he recognised the warmth, and the hand he had raised without noticing to rest it on Gaius’s shoulder, and stopped the hand in the air, and lowered it.

It was Caesar’s voice. Not the words, since Caesar’s words had been fewer, but the warmth, the pleasure in it, the quick sweet feeling in the chest of a man who has someone at his mercy and has decided, generously, to be merciful, and can feel the other man feel it. Brutus had stood where Gaius was standing. He had stood in a tent at Larissa after Pharsalus with mud to his knees and heard Caesar be kind to him, and had felt himself feel grateful, and had hated it, and had carried it four years under his ribs like a stone he could not cough up. And here it was in his own mouth. It tasted of nothing. It tasted like being good.

Gaius was looking at him. He had seen the hand stop.

“Thank you,” Gaius said. He said it the way Brutus had once said it, with his chin up, a man paying a debt he had not chosen to owe.

Brutus nodded. He did not trust his voice with anything more. He turned and walked back through the gate, and the lictors came after him with their rods, Gaius’s lictors, still carrying the fasces of a magistrate whose province had walked out from under him, and the army on the rampart watched them pass and did not know whether to cheer, and in the end did not.

That night Horace found him on the rampart above the marsh. The reeds were black and the sea beyond them was louder than in the day.

“The men are saying you’re very merciful,” Horace said.

“The men are right.”

“They’re saying it like a compliment.” Horace leaned on the stakes beside him. “Some of them. The ones who were with Pompey are saying it like something else.” He was quiet for a while. “My father bought his freedom. He used to say the worst part wasn’t the paying. It was that the man he paid was so pleased with himself for taking the money.”

Brutus did not answer. Down in the camp, in the good house he had given him, Gaius Antonius’s lamp was still lit, and his lictors sat outside his door on their bundled rods and played dice in the cold.
