# The Ides of March

## Book One

*A novel of the last years of the Roman Republic, from Cato’s death at Utica to the plain at Philippi*

by Reuben Dennison

---

Everyone knows what happened on the Ides of March. This is a novel about the people it happened to: the men who carried the knives and the friend they asked to bring Caesar to the door; the household that woke that morning to a bad dream and a door that had opened by itself; the soldier still waiting for the land he was promised; the moneylender who puts a price on every rumour; and a sickly eighteen-year-old who inherits a name that nobody thinks he can carry.

The murder is not the climax. It falls in the middle of the book, and everything after it is the bill.

---

## About the book

Rome, 44 BC. Caesar has won the civil war, and unlike Sulla, the last man to take Rome by force, he has forgiven his enemies. Men who fought against him now owe him their lives, their offices and their places at dinner. Some of them are grateful. Others are discovering that a pardon is a debt that can never be paid off, only rolled over at a worse rate.

Marcus Brutus, raised under the wax masks of an ancestor who drove out a king, has been handed the first praetorship in Rome by the man he will help to kill. Gaius Cassius, the better soldier, has been handed the second, and has not forgotten it. Decimus Brutus, Caesar’s officer and friend for ten years, is asked for the one thing only a friend can give: his easy welcome in Caesar’s house, and the door.

Below them, the city goes about its business and listens. In the dictator’s house a freedwoman overhears half a sentence and presses it into wax. In the Forum a moneylender finds the one debtor who always paid on time lying dead among his tablets, with marks on them that point to both sides of Rome’s quarrel. A centurion who fought Caesar’s last battle in Spain has been promised a field near Capua. It has been marked out with a length of twine, and it still has no date.

Then comes the morning in Pompey’s hall, and after it a city that has to decide what the killing meant. The men who did it expect to be thanked. Mark Antony, whom they spared, holds the consul’s chair, the dead man’s papers and the keys to the treasury. Cicero, who was not told, finds he has the finest voice in Rome and nothing behind it. And across the Adriatic, a young man sent there to study learns that a will has made him Caesar’s son.

*The Ides of March* follows them all, from the dinners of the pardoned to the siege of Mutina, from the kitchens of the great houses to the lists posted in the Forum with a price beside every name, and on to the plain in Macedonia where a quarrel that began in a Senate meeting has to be settled by armies.

---

## The story

There is no single hero. The book is told through more than a dozen viewpoints, some of them famous names and some of them people history never recorded, and most chapters move between two or three of them. Each sees only part of what is happening. News arrives late and in pieces, rumour usually gets there first, and people act on what they have.

The novel is divided into five acts.

**Foundations.** It opens at Utica in Africa in 46 BC, where Cato, the Republic’s most unbending defender, refuses Caesar’s pardon in the most violent way open to him. The story of his death goes round Rome’s dinner tables like a joke nobody can finish, and it hangs over the pardoned men learning to live as Caesar’s guests. Cicero wins a pardon for an exile with the best speech he has made in years, and finds out what the gift will cost the man he saved. Caesar’s veterans finish the last war in Spain and ask when the land is coming. A great-nephew nobody has noticed is sent to the Illyrian coast to study.

**Conspiracy.** At a festival a crown is offered three times and refused three times, and no two people agree on what they saw. Cassius begins to count the men who are tired of being grateful, and to argue with Brutus over whether Caesar’s fellow consul should die with him. In the kitchens omens are bought and sold for the price of a cake. A debtor is found with his throat cut. Calpurnia stops sleeping.

**The Ides.** The morning of 15 March is told from inside Caesar’s house, from the street, from the portico where Antony is kept talking, and from inside the hall. Then come the days after: the Capitol, the amnesty, the reading of the will, the funeral, and a poet who dies because he shares another man’s name.

**Aftermath and the war over a name.** The killers find that Rome will neither thank them nor let them stay. The heir takes the name and sells his own land to pay the dead man’s legacies. Antony holds the money. Cicero finds his voice again. The soldiers learn what their loyalty is worth, and who is bidding for it. The quarrel spreads out of Italy, to the schools of Athens and to a city in Asia whose hospitality is about to be tested.

**Mutina to Philippi.** Two consuls march north to break a siege. Allies make and unmake themselves. Three men meet on an island in a river with a draft of names, and the city learns what a list can do: in back rooms and country villas, in kitchens and in tombs along the Appian Way, people decide whom to hide and whom to sell. The armies of the East and the West meet at last at Philippi in Macedonia, and the book follows its survivors into the season after.

## The world of the book

**A Republic that still works on paper.** Consuls are still elected, two a year. The Senate still debates, the courts still sit, and the tribunes still hold their sacred office. But one man holds every power the old system can give and some it cannot, and in the winter before the Ides he is made dictator for life. The book’s Rome is a city where the forms are carefully observed by people who know exactly where the power has gone.

**Loyalty is personal, and it is owed.** A Roman’s place depends on whom he is bound to: patrons and clients, fathers and sons, the man who spared you and the man you spared. A pardon puts you in debt. A freed slave owes duty for life to the house that freed her, and the duty passes with the estate to whoever inherits it. Families are alliances, and the same family can hold a man on each side of a war.

**Armies have their own politics.** Caesar’s veterans were promised farms, and most of them are still waiting. Legions follow the name they marched under, the man who pays them, and the man beside them in the line, though not always in that order. Whole armies change sides in a night, and generals learn that soldiers can be bought only for the things they are willing to do.

**Money runs underneath everything.** Interest falls due on the Kalends. Lenders set up their tables under the colonnades of the Forum and adjust their rates every time the news changes. Leases rise and fall with a will; a dead man’s property goes to auction; a province is a treasury with a governor attached. When the great men fall out, the cost lands on the people who keep the accounts.

**Ordinary people are in the middle of it.** News moves through kitchens, laundries, wine shops and street fountains long before it reaches the Senate. Servants hear everything and are asked nothing. Shopkeepers, veterans, washerwomen and clerks are not spectators: they are the crowd at the funeral, the informers at the tribunal, the household that hides a man or gives him up.

**Each act of violence makes the next one easier.** It begins with a single killing that its authors call surgery. Then comes a riot, then an execution without trial, then a siege, and then a posted list with a reward beside every name. The book watches what that does to the people who have to live through it.

## The people

A selection, to give a sense of the ensemble. Those marked † are invented; everyone else is in the ancient sources. A full cast list is printed in the book.

**Marcus Junius Brutus.** Praetor, philosopher, a man Caesar spared and kept close and treated almost as a son. He wants the thing done as law and not as murder, and keeps trying to find the difference.

**Gaius Cassius Longinus.** He brought an army home from the disaster at Carrhae and was given a pardon instead of a triumph. An Epicurean who counts everything, and the first of them to say aloud what he wants.

**Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus.** Caesar’s officer through ten years of war, the man who builds his bridges and knows which door his litter uses.

**Mark Antony (Marcus Antonius).** Caesar’s fellow consul: soldier, gambler, a big laugh in a crowded room, and a good deal shrewder than he lets anyone see. His wife **Fulvia** keeps the accounts he doesn’t.

**Gaius Octavius.** Caesar’s great-nephew, eighteen and often ill, studying on the Illyrian coast with his friend **Marcus Agrippa** when the news arrives.

**Marcus Tullius Cicero.** The finest speaker in Rome, living in an age that no longer needs speeches, with his freedman Tiro taking down every word.

**Calpurnia.** Caesar’s wife, who dreams the house falling in.

**Porcia.** Cato’s daughter and Brutus’s wife, who means to know what her husband is hiding.

**Nysa†.** A freedwoman in Caesar’s house who carries water, counts exits and hears far more than anyone asks her.

**Sextus Laberius Cordus†.** A centurion of Caesar’s from Munda onwards, who trusts the man on his left and is still waiting for his field.

**Lucius Hordeonius Felix†.** A moneylender at the Janus arch in the Forum, who prices every rumour and keeps the tablets of a murdered client.

**Amyntas son of Menodotus†.** Treasurer of Smyrna, a careful man who learns what Roman quarrels cost the people who are made to pay for them.

Caesar himself is seen mostly through other people’s eyes. Around these figures move Servilia, Trebonius, Lepidus, the Epicurean Statilius, the scholar Varro, Hortensia, the orator’s daughter, Cleopatra in Caesar’s gardens across the Tiber, and the household slaves, soldiers, clerks and informers who make up the rest of Rome.

## Themes

**Mercy as a debt.** Caesar’s clemency is real, and it humiliates the men who receive it. The book keeps asking what a person owes to the one who spared them, and what it costs to repay.

**What things are worth.** Almost everyone in the novel is keeping an account of something: lenders, treasurers, paymasters, a freedwoman counting a crowd, a soldier measuring a field. A pardon, a name, a legion and a man’s head all end up with a price.

**The power of a name.** A name turns an unknown young man into a force in Italy. A name gets a poet killed in the street. A name on a list is a sentence of death, and a name held back at the right door can save a life.

**Knowing only part of the story.** No one in the book sees the whole board. People act on half a sentence, a garbled report or a sign seen from a distance. Where the ancient sources disagree, the novel lets its characters disagree too.

**Loyalty and survival.** Friends, households, soldiers and whole cities face the same choice in different coin: stand by someone, or save yourself. Some of the hardest choices in the book are made by people history never named.

**Ideals and power.** The men who killed for the Republic, and the man who speaks for it most eloquently, find that law needs force behind it, and that force soon has reasons of its own.

## What kind of read it is

The story is told in close third person, in short scenes that move between viewpoints, so that the reader often knows more than any one character does, and sometimes less than one of them suspects.

Politics happens in rooms: at dinner, in porticoes and gardens, in letters, at a moneylender’s table, on a tribunal. It comes through argument and manoeuvre rather than explanation. The battles are seen from inside the line, through mud and reeds and rumour, rather than from a general’s hill.

There is a good deal of dry humour: soldiers’ jokes, table wit, a moneylender’s maxims, the things servants say once the door has closed. There is also violence, sudden and plainly told. The book includes suicide, a lynching, executions, killings for reward and the display of the dead. It does not linger, but it does not look away.

The Roman texture is kept: dates by the Kalends, Nones and Ides, offices and titles used as the characters use them. A page of offices and terms at the front explains the few that matter. At the back, a historical note sets out what is invented and where the sources disagree, and a timeline gives the order of events. The timeline gives away what happens.

---

## Book One of a longer story

*The Ides of March* is the first book of a longer story. Book One begins at Utica in the spring of 46 BC, spends most of its length between the winter of 45 and the autumn of 42 BC, and ends at Philippi and in the winter and spring that follow. It tells the story of the conspiracy and the war it sets loose through to that point, and some threads are deliberately left open.
