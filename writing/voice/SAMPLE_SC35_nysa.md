# SAMPLE-1 — Gold Voice Scene (SC-35 fragment)

**Purpose:** Calibrate LLM tone. **Not** a finished novel chapter.  
**POV:** Nysa (F1) · **Place:** Domus Publica cubiculum/atrium orbit · **Night–dawn 14/15 March 44**  
**Canon:** DREAM-1, HOUSE-1, NOTE-1 prep, Decimus fetch begins  
**Length:** short model (~900 words)

---

Nysa woke because the house woke wrong.

Not the usual wrong—the cook shouting, a client too early at the vestibule, Phileros cursing a dog in the Sacra Via. This was quieter. Wood complaining. A door that should have stayed shut.

She was already on her feet in the side room off the atrium, blanket kicked away, when she heard Calpurnia’s voice through the wall of the cubiculum—not words at first, only the sound a throat makes when it has been running in sleep.

Phileros’ lamp came bobbing from the vestibule. “If it’s thieves—”

“It isn’t,” Nysa said, and hated that she sounded sure.

In the cubiculum the air was colder than the corridor. The door to the little court stood open as if someone had pulled it from outside. Moonlight lay in a stripe across the bed. Caesar was upright on one elbow, hair flattened on one side, the way powerful men looked when they were briefly only men. Calpurnia sat with her palms pressed to her face, then dropped them and stared at the beam-line of the pediment painted above them as if it might fall again.

“It came down,” she said. “The front of the house. And you—” She stopped. Her eyes found Nysa and did not soften. “Water.”

Nysa brought it. Her hands did not shake until she had turned away. She had dreamed of nothing. She had only listened, which was worse, because listening left you with pieces you could not put back.

Caesar drank. He looked at the open door, then at his wife, then at the wax tablets stacked on the low chest—the eternal unfinished stack. For a moment Nysa thought he would laugh. He often laughed when other men would pray.

He did not laugh.

“You are not usually like this,” he said to Calpurnia, almost mildly.

“I am asking you not to go.”

Nysa backed to the threshold. Phileros hovered behind her, smelling of onion and oil from the night watch brazier. From the atrium came the first scrape of a bench. The house was assembling its morning face too early.

Calpurnia spoke of blood in her lap, of holding him. She did not decorate it. Nysa, who had washed blood from floors after banquets, felt her stomach tip anyway—not at blood, at the way Calpurnia’s voice tried to stay private and failed.

Caesar’s gaze flicked to Nysa once, the way it flicked at doors and lists. “Go and see if the haruspices have sent anything,” he said. Not unkind. Not kind. A redirection of weather.

In the service court the sky was shifting from iron to the colour of dirty pearl. A kitchen boy claimed the sacred chickens had been sullen. Someone else said that was every morning. Nysa heard both and kept both, as she kept everything, because forgetting was a luxury for people who did not get beaten for incomplete messages.

She had a fragment of her own wrapped in linen inside her tunic—a few words pressed into wax the day before, when a visitor’s attendant had muttered near the peristyle about sparing a consul and not sparing a king. She had not understood the shape of it. She understood enough to be afraid of Phileros finding it.

When she returned to the atrium, Decimus was already there.

He had not come like a conspirator. He had come like a friend with a schedule: cloak decent, face open, the particular cheer of a man who intends to move another man from one room to another. Nysa had seen him here a hundred times. That was the obscenity—if it was an obscenity. She did not have a word. She had only the wax against her ribs.

“They are waiting,” Decimus was saying, easy. “The whole meeting. If you stay home for dreams, they will say you are afraid. Or that you are becoming a king who cannot be spoken to.”

Caesar stood in the atrium now, dressed enough for the street. Calpurnia remained at the cubiculum door, one hand on the frame as if holding up the house the dream had broken.

Nysa’s incomplete tablet suddenly felt childish. What would she hand him? A mutter? Against Decimus’ open hands?

Phileros stepped forward with the litter arrangements. Nysa stepped forward too—then stopped, because Decimus glanced at her the way men glanced at furniture that had moved an inch.

“Not now,” Phileros hissed, mistaking her hesitation for a request about water or sandals.

The litter poles knocked. Outside, the Sacra Via was already loud with people who did not know they were living inside someone else’s omen.

Caesar paused at the vestibule. For half a breath Nysa thought he would turn back. He looked at Calpurnia. He looked at Decimus. He did not look at Nysa.

“We go,” he said.

Nysa’s fingers found the linen over the wax and did not draw it out.

She stood in the vestibule until the litter had gone far enough that the sound changed, swallowed by the Forum’s morning throat. Only then did she realise she was counting—steps, heartbeats, something—as if numbers could make a door stay closed.
