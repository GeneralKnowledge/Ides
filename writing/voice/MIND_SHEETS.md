# Mind sheets — Book 1

**Status:** In use for the full-book voice pass, 2026-11-11 (the three-scene pilot was accepted). Descriptive, not prescriptive.
**Why:** An outside reader of Ch 1–6 found that the viewpoint characters have different vocabularies but one way of thinking: each sees a thing, sorts it and states what it means. [`POV_DICTION.md`](POV_DICTION.md) lists what each person says. These sheets describe how each person’s attention works, so that a scene can be told from inside one particular head.

**How to use them.** Read the sheet before writing or revising a scene, then put it away. Nothing here is a rule or a ban. People generalise; the sheets say when this person does and where the thought comes from. If a sentence could be moved into another character’s scene without anyone noticing, it probably belongs to the author and not to the character.

**Three tests for a rewrite** (Reuben, 2026-11-11, after the pilot). A rewrite is not judged by whether it resembles the pilot scenes.

1. **Action.** What actually happens in this scene, and what changes because of it?
2. **Point of view.** What would this particular person notice, misunderstand, conceal or care about?
3. **Explanation.** Is the narration telling the reader something the scene has already shown?

The Nysa scene in Ch 2 is the benchmark for one thing only: whether a rewrite has moved the reader closer to the character. It is not a pattern for sentence shape or for length. No kind of line is banned. Hordeonius keeps his memorable lines and Cicero stays eloquent. The question is whether the line is this person’s, said to someone, with a consequence. Characters should not be made less articulate. They should be given more to do, more to conceal, and people on whom their words have an effect. A homely comparison from a character’s own world (Nysa’s big jar going over) works because it is rare.

**Two tests for a scene’s ending** (from the outside reader, 2026-11-11):

1. Take away the last sentence. Does the scene lose anything it needs? If not, the sentence may be an explanation.
2. End on the physical action instead. What does the reader understand without being told?

Neither test is an order to cut endings. The Ligarius letter and the shared bread stay because they carry weight without explaining it.

**The aim** is people who are more particular. It is not plainer prose. Caesar talking about a leaking cistern and a lame horse (Ch 6) is the model: he is not making a statement about power, he is having a conversation, and the reader learns him from it.

## Nysa

**Notices first:** hands, feet, what is being carried, who is standing apart from whom, which door. She sees a person as a set of things she could describe to the steward later: a boil, a writing case, a clean toga. She counts, in Greek, because it is quicker.

**How a thought moves:** in order of events. This happened, then this. She keeps what she saw and does not turn it over to see what it means, because meaning is for the people upstairs. When she does reach a conclusion it is practical and about the next hour.

**When she generalises:** when she is frightened, and then it is a memory of what happened to a particular person (Chreste, who repeated a fishmonger’s joke and was sent to Labici). She has cases where other people have sayings.

**What she cannot or will not say:** anything that would make her the source. She answers the question asked and stops. Her omissions are choices and she knows which ones she has made.

**What she misunderstands:** she thinks politics is something that happens to senators and can be kept out of a kitchen by not carrying it in. She thinks that if she reports only objects she has reported nothing. She does not see that the count on the steps is politics. She did not know who the man on the painted board was.

**In talk:** short, literal, a beat behind when someone is being clever at her. She is sometimes funny by accident, because she answers exactly what was asked (“So I wasn’t here”).

**Body:** the weight of what she carries, a knotted sandal strap, a hip. She is about twenty-eight, Caesar’s freedwoman, and nobody’s property.

**Sounds like her:** “Two had nothing in their hands at all and stood a little apart from each other in very clean togas, not talking.”
**Does not:** “Cheers were loud. Spit lasted.” (A verdict. She would keep the spit and not rule on it.)

## Cicero

**Notices first:** sentences. Who made one, whether it was good, whether it was his. Then audiences: who is listening, who will repeat it, what Atticus will think of the version he gets.

**How a thought moves:** backwards into history and precedent, in long periods with the verb held back, and then round to himself. He cannot hear a title without knowing who last held it. This is the one mind in the book that properly thinks in built sentences, and the narration inside his head can be longer and more ornate than anywhere else.

**When he generalises:** constantly, and with pleasure, but as an orator does: a claim with a precedent attached, aimed at a listener. He does not produce the two-beat maxim. His generalisations are often about himself.

**What he cannot or will not say:** in public, anything. In private he says all of it, to Tiro and to Atticus, and takes the saying for a kind of action.

**What he misunderstands:** almost everything about force. He believes events must pass through a speech before they happen, that his private disgust is a form of resistance, that the men who compliment his voice still need it. He forecasts with confidence and is wrong (three years in Parthia will take care of “for ever”). He does not notice that Tiro runs him.

**In talk:** the best in the room and aware of it. Kind in small ways when it costs nothing (“I heard nothing at all”). Vain about style even when being servile: he will not send Caesar a compliment Caesar has already had.

**Body:** he walks when he dictates and cannot sit when he is pleased or ashamed.

**Sounds like him:** “Sulla had kept his until he was bored. Caesar had taken a year, and then ten years, the way a guest takes a second cup and then the jug.”
**Does not:** “Friendship as cipher: the only legion he trusted.” (A caption. He would give that thought a subject, a verb and a subordinate clause.)

## Hordeonius

**Notices first:** the term on the paper. Dates, seals, security, who is standing behind a borrower. He reads faces for whether they will pay.

**How a thought moves:** from the instrument outwards. He is the one character who really does think in rates and entries, and he is allowed his maxims, but they are a banker’s proverbs said aloud to a client across a board, solemnly, as moral instruction. He means every word. He is funny only because he is sincere: he mourns a ruined bribery market as other men mourn the Republic.

**When he generalises:** when he is teaching someone younger what paper is, or defending his own decency. Once or twice in a scene, in speech. The narration around him does not need to make ledger jokes on his behalf.

**What he cannot or will not say:** that he is afraid, or that he enjoyed something. He does his real business last and presents it as a kindness.

**What he misunderstands:** he thinks every grievance is a request for credit. He can tell exactly who will repay and has no idea who will kill. He believes he stands outside politics because he lends to all sides.

**In talk:** grave, slow, courteous to the people he is squeezing. He lets a silence work. Sosia gets the quick lines and Hordeonius reproves him for them.

**Body:** ink on two fingers, a ring, a cloth he wipes his hands on when a matter is closed. He acts before he explains: turns the tablet, moves the list aside, draws the wax to him.

**Sounds like him:** “I have buried clients who paid me out of the province they were robbing to do it, and I spoke well of them at the pyre.”
**Does not:** “A house aiming at a horizon with a name was a rate change in laundry’s clothes.” (The narrator performing him.)

## First notes on the other viewpoints

To be filled out and checked against the text when each one’s scenes come up. Each already has a **Misbelief** line in `continuity/character_profiles/`.

| Viewpoint | Attention goes to | Generalises when | Misunderstands |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cassius** | Who was given what; hands; who is tested and how they take it | Aloud, as a blade, when cornered at a table. Rarely in his own head, where he is counting | That being right about a man is the same as knowing what he will do |
| **Brutus** | What is fitting; the dead; his own conduct seen from outside | He starts to and cannot finish. He reaches for the philosophical sentence and finds the cup | That an act can be kept clean by intending it cleanly; who is paying for his scruples |
| **Decimus** | Measurements: widths, hours, loads, how long a bridge takes | Almost never. He likes or dislikes a thing and gets on | That being useful to Caesar and being his friend are the same, and that Caesar’s notice is not something other men can trade in |
| **Laberius** | Kit, ground, feet, who has eaten; the men either side of him | After the twentieth time he has watched the same thing go wrong, and then as a joke about pay | Why the gentlemen do anything. He does not need to know and distrusts anyone who explains |
| **Octavian** | Who was looked at and who was skipped; what older men do with their hands | To himself, coldly, and he is ashamed of it afterwards | At seventeen, how much of the room’s indifference is about him. Later, less |
| **Antony** | Bodies, noise, whether a crowd is with him | Loudly, for effect, and he does not believe his own sayings | The boy |
| **Calpurnia** | The house: sounds, who is up, what is out of place | Not at all. She knows things in her body and has no words that would persuade a man | That being right will be enough to be heard |

Minor and occasional viewpoints (Statilius, Calenus, Amyntas, Porcia, Cleanthes, Caesar) get a sheet when their scenes are audited.
